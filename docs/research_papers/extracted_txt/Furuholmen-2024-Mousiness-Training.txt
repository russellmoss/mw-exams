1. Summary  
 
Mousy taint is perceived to be increasing. Variation in wine professionals’ (WPs) 
detection abilities and a lack of consensus regarding the taint represent challenges to 
sensory quality control. Several studies have found an induction of sensitivity in 
subjects after repeated exposure to a substance. Thus far, no study has explored 
whether this also applies to mousiness in wine. 
 
The aim of this research paper was to test whether repeated exposure to one of the 
mousy compounds, 2-acetyltetrahydropyridine (ATHP), could induce sensitivity in 
WPs. Fifty volunteer WPs were randomly divided into a training group that conducted 
systematic training on ATHP, as well as a control group not subject to training. 
Measurement of detection thresholds were used to monitor changes in sensitivity. 
The WPs also described two concentrations of ATHP in wine and these descriptions 
were compared to terms used in the literature. 
 
The results show that WPs’ detection thresholds of ATHP can be reduced and 
exposure to a limited number of clear benchmarks is sufficient to achieve this. Also, 
the number of subjects truly anosmic to ATHP seems to be much lower than what is 
commonly referred to in the literature, while the inducible subjects represent a more 
important category. WPs’ descriptors of ATHP also indicated a relationship between 
retro-nasal odour quality and stimulus intensity, and that the degree to which a 
mousy wine is unpalatable depends on the concentration.

2. Introduction  
 
Mousiness is a wine fault that has re-emerged in recent years (Tempère, et al., 2019, 
p. 1) and has even been referred to as “the wine fault of our times” (Goode, 2020). It 
was described by Thudicum (1894), referenced in Grbin & Henschke (2000, p. 255), 
as “closely resembling to the smell of a residence of mice,” representing one of the 
earliest known reports of the fault. 
 
Under normal wine tasting conditions, retro-nasal evaluation is most commonly 
necessary for detection as the mousy compounds in general are not sufficiently 
volatile at the low pH of wine, meaning they are first released when the wine comes 
into contact with an individual’s higher saliva pH (Tucknott, 1977, cited in Costello, 
1998, p. 23). It is generally perceived after the wine has been expectorated or 
swallowed (Snowdon, et al., 2006, p. 6466). 
 
Mousiness is referred to as both unpleasant, persistent and even unpalatable (Grbin, 
et al., 1996, p. 57), potentially causing significant financial loss to a wine producer. 
There is, however, a lack of consensus, including among wine professionals (WPs), 
regarding both the detection and the characterisation of mousy off-flavour in wine 
(Tempère, et al., 2019, p. 2). Thus, discussions on the topic can be challenging, as a 
wine may seem faultless to one person yet unpalatable to another.  
 
There have been no recent studies on the actual incidence of mousiness in wine on a 
larger scale. In contrast, for detection rates of mousiness, one of the most extensive 
studies to date was undertaken by the Australian Wine Research Institute (AWRI, 
2011). The AWRI found that as many as 25% of WPs could not detect the taint, 
===== PAGE 7 =====
3 
 
which makes this wine fault particularly interesting. However, whether this was due to 
actual specific anosmia or the assessors’ lack of familiarity with the taint is unclear. 
 
Several studies have reported an induced sensitivity to odorous compounds following 
repeated exposure (Tempère, et al., 2012); (Cain & Schmidt, 2002); (Wysocki, et al., 
1989). Specific anosmia has for instance been related to the flavour compound 
diacetyl (Lawless, et al., 1994). However, Tempère et al. (2012) reported a decrease 
in detection thresholds for both diacetyl and linalool in WPs following a period of 
training. 
 
Variation in the ability to detect mousiness is partially related to differences in the 
composition of a person’s saliva and the pH of the tongue’s surface (Snowdon, et al., 
2006, p. 6466). However, if repeated exposure to 2-acetyltetrahydropyridine (ATHP), 
one of the compounds that produce the taint, could influence WPs’ detection ability, 
these may be effective in reducing both the lack of consensus among WPs and 
increase their sensory quality control ability to detect mousiness under normal wine 
tasting conditions. 
 
This research paper (RP) will therefore investigate the following:  
1) What are wine professionals’ detection levels of ATHP pre- and post-
exposure?  
2) Can wine professionals’ sensitivity to mousiness be changed by repeated 
exposures and systematic training? 
===== PAGE 8 =====
4 
 
3) What are wine professionals’ descriptors of one of the mousy compounds, 
ATHP, in wine and how do these relate to established terms by previous 
literature? 
 
Given the apparent increase in the incidence of mousiness and the high degree of 
specific anosmia reported by the AWRI (2011), exploring whether repeated 
exposures to mousiness can improve WPs’ detection ability may be of significant 
value to the wine trade.

3. Background and context 
 
3.1 Mousiness in wine 
 
3.1.1 Occurrence of mousiness in wine 
 
Historically, mousiness has been described as both “sporadic” (Snowdon, et al., 
2006, p. 6465) and “infrequent” (Grbin, et al., 1996, p. 57), though these authors 
acknowledge the strong detrimental effect on wine quality when mousiness does 
occur. 
 
However, more recently, both wine experts and researchers share an impression that 
the presence of the taint is increasing (Tempère, et al., 2019, p. 1); (Kiyomichi, et al., 
2023, p. 1); (Snowdon, et al., 2006, p. 6465); (A. Feiring, 2017, personal 
communication, 22 October). Indeed, several articles on the topic have recently been 
published in the international wine press (Woolf, 2017); (Goode, 2020); (Feiring, 
2022). Further, the AWRI (AWRI, u.d.) reported that an increasing number of wine 
samples suspected of mousiness had been submitted to its technical services group 
in the 1990s and 2010s. 
 
The rise of the natural wine movement over the past two decades may be of 
relevance to explaining the apparent increase in mousy wines. Massini & Vuchot 
(2015) associated mousiness in wines with a significant reduction in the use of SO2 
(supported by recent research by Kiyomichi, et al., 2023), increasing pH levels, and 
the use of native microbiota in fermentations. Thus, the fault is particularly relevant to 
natural wines made with minimal intervention. Another explanation for the increased 
===== PAGE 10 =====
6 
 
reported incidence of the wine fault may be increasing awareness and familiarity with 
the taint among WPs. 
 
3.1.2 Formation of mousiness  
 
Mousiness is usually of microbial origin (AWRI, u.d.) and is mainly attributable to 
Dekkera/Brettanomyces yeast (Grbin & Henschke, 2000) and certain strains of lactic 
acid bacteria (Costello, et al., 2001). There is no method for removing mousy off-
flavour once it has occurred, but an effective form of protection against the taint 
seems to be SO2  (AWRI, u.d.). 
 
Three N-heterocyclic molecules have been associated to the mousy off-flavour in 
wine: 2-acetyltetrahydropyridine (ATHP) (Strauss & Heresztyn, 1984), 2-
ethyltetrahydropyridine (ETHP) (Grbin, et al., 1996, p. 59) and 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline 
(APY) (Herderich, et al., 1995). Of the three, ETHP was previously considered the 
least important compound. In fact, its sensory threshold measured in water is higher 
than both ATHP and APY (Tempère, et al., 2019, p. 2). Nevertheless, it is difficult to 
compare the detection threshold values per se, especially when the compounds are 
diluted in a complex matrix such as wine (Kiyomichi, et al., 2023, p. 2). 
 
Research by Hayasaka (2019) and Kiyomichi, et al. (2023) recently provided reliable 
and rapid methods for the quantification of the N-heterocyclic compounds associated 
with mousiness, which can be useful for the routine screening of wines. In fact, the 
latter showed that, based on a sample of 68 wines produced with limited or no SO2, 
ETHP was found in almost all and ATHP was found in nearly all wines suspected of 
===== PAGE 11 =====
7 
 
mousiness. In contrast, APY was only found in 11 wines and therefore represented 
only a few cases compared to ATHP and ETHP. Also, in Strauss & Heresztyn’s 
research (1984), ATHP was only present in mousy and not sound wines.  
 
3.1.3 Sensory character and the detection ability of individuals 
 
Various research referenced in Tempère, et al. (2019, p. 2) described a mousy 
character in wine as similar to rodent urine (dirty mouse cage) and grilled foods, such 
as popcorn, rice, crackers and bread crusts (Tucknott, 1974); (Buttery, et al., 1983); 
(Strauss & Heresztyn, 1984); (Herderich, et al., 1995). Other descriptions include 
dried sausage skin, vomit and dirty mops (Tempère, et al., 2019, p. 2). The wide 
range of descriptions may be due to the potential and unexplored matrix effect 
between the compounds involved, and their relative levels of intensity and variation in 
individual detection abilities (Tempère, et al., 2019, p. 2).  
 
Ever since the early 1900s, variations in individuals’ ability to detect mousiness have 
been recognized (Müller-Thurgau & Osterwalder, 1913). Using ATHP, Grbin, et al. 
(1996), examined individuals’ detection thresholds and concluded that their range of 
detection was large.  
 
APY, ETHP and ATHP are generally not sufficiently volatile at the pH range of wine 
to be detected ortho-nasally (Bartowsky & Henschke, 1995). However, when the wine 
(2.8–3.8 pH) comes into contact with the higher pH range of a person’s saliva 
(usually near 7), the off-flavour can be detected retro-nasally. Nevertheless, there are 
===== PAGE 12 =====
8 
 
wide inter-individual variations in oral pH of between 5.76 and 7.96 (Larsen, et al., 
1999), which may partially explain the differences in individuals’ ability to detect 
mousiness (Snowdon, et al., 2006, p. 6466). 
 
3.1.4 Evaluation techniques 
 
Ortho-nasal evaluation techniques have been developed to detect mousy off-flavour 
in wine without actually tasting it, such as the “palm and sniff” method, where one 
drop of wine is placed on the back of the hand and the skin is then sniffed (Grbin, et 
al., 1996, p. 58); the alkaline strip method, using alkaline strips dipped in culture 
media or wine (Grbin & Henschke, 2000); oxidation of the wine due to the instability 
of APY and ATHP in its presence (Weerawatanakorn, et al., 2015); and pH 
adjustment (Tempère, et al., 2019). Tempère, et al. (2019) found that by adjusting the 
pH to around 5, the consensus among WPs increased significantly, as well as their 
ability to discriminate. These methods, however, are not representative of normal 
wine tasting conditions. 
 
3.1.5 Previous research  
 
Important wine literature, such as the Oxford Companion to Wine, has often stated 
that as many as 30% of WPs cannot detect mousiness. The source of this specific 
figure is not cited. However, this percentage is also widely reported elsewhere 
(Feiring, 2022); (Goode, 2018, p. 180); (Woolf, 2017). 
 
The 30% anosmia figure likely originated from the wine taint and fault tasting clinics 
organized by the AWRI during their Advanced Wine Assessment Courses (A. 
===== PAGE 13 =====
9 
 
Coulter1 2022, personal communication, 18 February) and is also referred to in “Ask 
the AWRI column” of Grapegrower & Winemaker (2015). In accumulated data from 
2006 to 2011, only 75% of WPs could detect mousiness (AWRI, 2011). As the 
anosmia data were collected over time, the 30% figure often quoted in various 
sources of wine literature was based on 148 study participants and not the final 
number of 238 participants who accounted for a lower average anosmia of 25% (A. 
Coulter 2022, personal communication, 18 February). 
 
These results, however, were based on one single and unquantifiable concentration 
of ATHP presented to the study participants and did not take into account different 
levels of sensitivity. Thus, rigorous sensory investigations to collect the anosmia data 
were not conducted (A. Coulter 2022, personal communication, 18 February). 
Because of its prominence, the AWRI’s study is regarded as important research. 
 
3.2 Sensory evaluation and measurement of detection thresholds 
 
In this RP, sensory evaluation is used to determine the level at which the stimulus of 
mousy compounds is detected and if it can be changed through repeated exposures. 
The 50 WPs of the RP are referred to as “assessors” and their detection levels as 
“thresholds”. These are taken as a description of the assessors’ performance. 
 
Three-alternative forced-choice (3-AFC) is a common procedure for measuring 
odour, flavour and taste detection thresholds (ISO 13301, 2018), and was used in the 
RP. During a 3-AFC test, the participants of a panel are presented with three

samples, one of which contains the substance to be detected (test sample), while the 
other two samples (references) do not contain the substance in question. The 
participants are asked to choose the test sample, and if uncertain they are required to 
guess (ISO 13301, 2018).2  
 
An ascending series is mostly used in taste and smell to avoid adaptation and carry-
over effects from a strong concentration affecting a successively weaker one 
(Lawless & Heymann, 2010, p. 21). Lawless & Heymann (2010, p. 19) refer to 
sensory adaptation as “a commonly observed decrease in responsiveness to 
conditions of more or less constant stimulation.” The potential effects of adaptation 
must therefore be anticipated in the experimental design of a sensory study 
(O'Mahony, 1986) and are highly relevant when taking into account the persistent 
character of the mousy compounds and the need for retro-nasal detection (S. 
Tempère3 2023, personal communication, 13 October). 
 
3.2.1 Criteria for a correct answer 
 
Because 3-AFC testing requires the participants of a panel to guess when they are 
uncertain, the literature has taken different approaches to the kind of criteria that 
should apply to a correct answer when establishing individual detection thresholds. 
Lawless and Heymann (2010, p. 131) refer to a participant’s first correct answer 
when all higher concentrations are also correct. At the same time, they (p. 137)

4. Methodology 
 
4.1 Pilot Study 
 
In 2022, an in-depth pilot study was conducted involving 30 WPs from the Norwegian 
Wine Monopoly (Vinmonopolet). All the pilot study participants performed two 
ascending 3-AFC tests based on eight different combinations of two of the mousy 
compounds: ATHP and APY, with a period of 13 days of training in between the two 
tasting sessions. The concentration ranges of APY and ATHP (Table 2) were based 
on the concentrations found in spoiled wines in the previous literature (Grbin, et al., 
1996, p. 59), as well as preliminary experiments by Laboratorios Excell Ibérica S.L. 
and the researcher. 
 
The results indicated that, on average, the pilot study participants performed better in 
the second 3-AFC test. However, some of the results also raised questions about 
whether training sometimes resulted in certain individuals adapting, leading to a 
reduced level of sensitivity after training. Also, some of the pilot study participants 
seemed to be affected by adaptation and/or fatigue during the two tasting sessions 
and therefore questioned a) whether the minimum 45 seconds that elapsed between 
each wine tasted in the 3-AFC tests were sufficient and b) the appropriateness of 
using the first correct concentration identified where all other concentrations are also 
correct (Lawless & Heymann, 2010, p. 131) as the criteria for a correct answer for the 
best estimate of individual threshold (BET).  
 
===== PAGE 18 =====
14 
 
12 pilot study participants were able to detect all concentrations in the first tasting 
session, showing that the range of concentrations was probably too high for a 3-AFC 
test. As the interactions between ATHP and APY are unknown, the effect of each of 
the compounds was also difficult to interpret. Furthermore, due to the lack of a fixed 
geometrical dilution factor between the concentration steps, detection thresholds 
could not be estimated in accordance with ISO 13301 (2018). Finally, it could be 
questioned whether the average improvement of the pilot study participants was due 
to repeated exposure from repetition of the 3-AFC test, or to the training.    
 
4.2 Materials and methods 
 
4.2.1 Experimental design 
 
Based on the experience from the pilot study, the new RP study had a higher number 
of assessors (50) to increase the statistical robustness of the research. For the 
threshold measurements, all assessors were required to complete two identical 
sensitivity tastings using ascending 3-AFC tests referred to as T1 (first sensitivity 
tasting) and T2 (second sensitivity tasting). Only ATHP was used, with a consistent 
dilution factor between each concentration step. A minimum break of two minutes 
between each wine tasted was required for the 3-AFC tests. 
 
All assessors were randomized to a control group (C) and a training group (T) to 
distinguish between the effect of repeated exposure through 3-AFC compared to 
systematic training. In between T1 and T2, T trained at home for 14 days. Estimated 
detection thresholds for ATHP were used to measure changes in sensitivity between 
T1 and T2 at individual and group level. 
===== PAGE 19 =====
15 
 
The criterion used for assessing BET for T1 and T2 was set to be the lowest 
concentration detected of three correct answers in a row in line with the research 
referenced in 3.2.1. This criterion would therefore consider the potential effects of 
adaptation that are especially relevant to a persistent taint such as mousiness (also 
indicated by the pilot study), while not potentially losing important data by applying a 
stopping rule. If an assessor did not reach the criterion of three correct answers in a 
row but answered correctly at the end of the series (e.g. the last two concentration 
steps), a correct answer was considered the first of those correct answers following 
research of Wysocki, et al. (1989). 
 
The probability of finding three correct answers in a row at random is only 3.7% (S. 
Tempère 2023, personal communication, 13 October). Despite the low likelihood of 
making three correct guesses in a row, an additional and stricter criterion compared 
to the referenced research was applied: three correct answers in a row were only 
considered correct when the assessor had a maximum of one incorrect answer later 
in the series. As one incorrect answer after three correct answers may occur as a 
consequence of adaptation explained in 3.2.1, several incorrect answers would, in 
contrast, suggest that the assessor had not accurately identified three in a row, but 
had instead guessed correctly. 
 
4.2.2 Location and schedule 
 
All tastings took place at Vinmonopolet’s headquarters in Dronning Eufemias gt. 13, 
Oslo, Norway. The test room was designed in accordance with the general guidance 
for the design of test rooms (ISO 8589, 2007) and the standard for Testing and 
===== PAGE 20 =====
16 
 
calibration laboratories (ISO/IEC 17025, 2017) with seven separate tasting booths 
and a room temperature of 19–21 °C. All booths included an integrated spittoon and 
PC. 
 
All tastings were performed between 09:30 and 17:00. Due to the high number of 
assessors and the capacity of the test room, both T1 and T2 were organized over a 
period of two days each. 
 
4.2.3 Assessors and panel size 
 
The cohort of this study comprised 50 WPs from different sectors of the Norwegian 
wine trade. Their participation was based on their own desire to learn. In the research 
by Parr, et al. (2002), the expert group comprised winemakers, wine science 
researchers, wine professionals (Masters of Wine, wine judges, wine writers, wine 
retailers), graduate students of viticulture and oenology and people who had been 
involved in wine for more than ten years. 
 
Due to the limited amount of wine produced in Norway, the most suitable assessors 
were employees of Vinmonopolet (retail), wine importers, wine educators and wine 
writers. All the assessors had completed a minimum WSET 3/Sommelier degree, 
were currently working full time in the wine trade with regular wine tasting as part of 
their work and had not been part of the pilot study. Twenty-seven female and 23 male 
assessors participated in the study with ages ranging from 18–60+ (Table 1).

4.3 Stimuli 
 
4.3.1 Concentration ranges 
 
Due to ATHP’s relevance in the occurrence of mousiness and its role in previous 
research, this compound was selected for the concentration range. 
 
The concentrations of ATHP were determined based on the concentrations 
measured in spoiled wine by Grbin, et al. (1996, p. 59) from 4.8μg/L to 106μg/L, 
Costello (1998, p. 95) from 0.7μg/L to 106μg/L and Hayasaka (2019, p. 114) from 
0.52μg/L to 26.46μg/L, as well as the results from the pilot study (Table 2).  
 
The final levels of the concentrations selected were tested by using a 3-AFC test, 
performed under the same conditions and circumstances as in the main study of the 
RP, on a selection of 13 participants from the previous pilot study (described in 4.1), 
including the least and most sensitive participants. None of them could detect the 
lowest concentration of 0.2μg/L, and only one of the participants could not detect the 
highest level of 122.07μg/L. Thus, the selected concentrations seemed appropriate to 
cover a variety of thresholds.

4.4.2 Training 
 
The assessors of T were asked to follow a systematic training programme individually 
at home for two weeks in between T1 and T2. Previous sensory training research on 
other compounds has included training periods with different levels of intensity lasting 
from five to 60 days (Tempère, et al., 2012); (Wysocki, et al., 1989); (Cain & Schmidt, 
2002); (Dalton, et al., 2002). The 14 days proposed for this RP were within this 
range. 
 
All assessors completed T1 and T2. However, any assessors who detected all 
concentrations of ATHP in T1 were excluded from assignment to C or T, in 
accordance with research by Tempère, et al. (2012). Nevertheless, ensuring that 
these hyper-sensitive assessors still completed T2 allowed a qualitative exploration 
of potential variations in the thresholds of such WPs. Assessors who could not detect 
C8 during T1, on the other hand, were randomized equally into C and T.  
 
After completing T1, the assessors assigned to T were provided with four 5.7cL 
Vivaplex glass bottles of the base wine to taste over the next 14 days. Two of the 
wines were spiked with the strongest concentration of mousiness (C8) and the

that the assessors could carry the bottles as hand luggage on planes, etc. The 
training kit also included a vial with residues of C8, dissolved in ethanol, to nose daily

without knowing either the compound or the concentrations used and were given a 
reference sample with no added substance for comparison. As a final step, T 
assessors were also asked to report how the training had affected their personal

4.5 Measurement of detection thresholds 
 
4.5.1 Probability of Detection 
 
Probability of Detection (PD) of the panel as a whole was calculated for T1 and T2 in 
order to find the absolute detection level as the concentration where PD=0.5, thereby 
indicating the concentration level at which 50% of the panel had successfully 
detected ATHP. PD was modelled as described in ISO 13301 (2018) by considering 
all data from the threshold tastings, adjusting the data for chance, taking into account 
the guessing model represented by 3-AFC, in which Pc represents the probability of 
making a correct selection11: 
PD (concentration) = 1.5*Pc (concentration) - 0.5 
 
For comparison of the results, a linear model was made with group (categorical 
variables with levels C and T), test (categorical variables with levels T1 and T2) and 
x=log10 (concentration) (continuous variable) as independent variables and PD as 
the dependent variable, including all interaction terms. A variable selection was 
performed with stepwise selection using the Akaike information criterion (AIC)12 using 
the R-function step with default settings (Venables & Ripley, 2002) to obtain the most 
parsimonious model. The final model included only main effects, resulting in 
regression models with different intercepts defined by group (C, T) and test (T1, T2). 
The adjusted R-square is reported for both the full and reduced model. Analysis of

4.6 Statistical analyses

T2 within each group, were analysed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.15

were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.  
 
Non-parametric tests were used as the data did not follow a normal distribution 
according to the Shapiro-Wilk test.

5.4 Discussion Research Question 2 results 
 
5.4.1 Induced sensitivity from repeated exposures 
 
At panel level, the assessors achieved a lower detection threshold for ATHP following 
repeated exposures. The results were therefore in line with the research (2012) by 
Tempère, et al., who uncovered an induced sensitivity to diacetyl and linalool 
following a simple training procedure.  
===== PAGE 42 =====
38 
 
These are not the only examples where increased sensitivity has been documented 
for substances often related to a specific anosmia, the possible causes of the latter 
being thoroughly discussed in Wysocki, et al. (1989), who found that the ability to 
detect androstenone17 was induced in 10 out of 20 initially insensitive individuals who 
were systematically exposed to the compound. One of the researchers, who had 
initially been insensitive to androstenone, also experienced an induced sensitivity 
after months of interaction with the compound.  
 
In the above research it was consequently suggested that there were three 
categories of human subjects: the truly anosmic, the inducible, and subjects who are 
naturally sensitive or have experienced incidental induction. Following the research 
by Wysocki, et al. (1989), both Cain & Schmidt (2002) and Dalton, et al. (2002) found 
that an induced sensitivity to the flavour of glutaraldehyde in drinking water, 
benzaldehyde and citralva, respectively, could be achieved through repeated 
exposures. The latter study found that the effect was limited to women, an effect 
which was not in line with the findings of, for example, Tempère, et al. (2012).  
 
In this RP, the six assessors who did not detect C8 in T1 could initially qualify as 
“truly anosmic”. In T2, however, they were all able to detect C8 twice, making “the 
inducible” a more appropriate category. In fact, one of the assessors who did not 
detect C8 in either the pre-screening or in T1 (also confirming his complete lack of 
sensitivity after T1), appeared to “discover” the taint during the systematic training 
 
17 A volatile steroid found in human perspiration, boar saliva, some pork products ( in bacon, for 
example), truffles and celery (Wysocki, et al., 1989) (p. 7976). 
===== PAGE 43 =====
39 
 
programme, especially by nosing the vial in the tasting kit, which led to further retro-
nasal detection. After completing the study, the same assessor also stated that 
during the training period, for the first time in his 20-year wine career he had 
encountered a wine he suspected was mousy, likely due to his increased awareness 
and/or detection ability as a consequence of the training. In fact, two of the pilot study 
participants had a similar experience of having acquired the ability to detect 
mousiness after being presented proper benchmarks for the taint. 
 
In the pilot study, only one of the participants, despite the training, was still unable to 
detect the highest concentration in either the second sensitivity tasting or during a 
retest. When exposed to C8 used in the RP in a separate tasting to further 
investigate this participant, he could still not detect the strongest concentration of 
ATHP. Thus, this individual represented the only one of a total of 80 WPs who 
participated in either the pilot study or in the experiment in the RP who qualified as 
“truly anosmic”, even after repeated exposures. 
 
5.4.2 Effect of systematic training versus repeated exposure 
 
The systematic training of T did not lead to a more substantial reduction in detection 
threshold compared C, which perhaps could be expected due to a higher frequency 
of exposure. When comparing C and T, surprisingly, it was C that had a tendency to 
improve more.  
 
===== PAGE 44 =====
40 
 
In fact, in research by Tempère, et al. (2012), the increase in the WPs’ sensitivity was 
only related to the compound (diacetyl or linalool) included in the systematic training 
programme, not the control compound, which also presented repeated exposure to 
the WPs of the study through repetition of the 3-AFC test. Also, in research by 
Wysocki, et al. (1989), the shift in sensitivity was unique to androstenone.  
 
Contrary to these studies, less frequent exposures seemed to be enough to induce 
sensitivity to ATHP. While a single high intensity exposure (in this case, through the 
pre-screening) is not sufficient to train someone, multiple repeated exposures may be 
sufficient. However, a high level of exposure, as presented by the systematic training 
programme, seems unnecessary as there is no marginal gain versus less frequent 
repetitions. Nevertheless, following the study of the RP, as many as 17 assessors of 
T reported their impression of an increase in their own sensitivity following the 
systematic training. Thus, the effect of systematic training cannot be excluded.  
 
Similar to studies by Tempere, et al. (2012) and Wysocki, et al. (1984) the decrease 
in the detection threshold was not uniform among all assessors of the RP. 
Furthermore, the effect of repeated exposure was very uneven, ranging from small to 
substantial improvement. 
 
5.4.3 Effects of awareness 
 
Knowing what mousiness actually is, and having previous experience of the taint, 
may be of importance to detection ability and might at least partially explain the 
===== PAGE 45 =====
41 
 
differences reported in sensitivity (Cain & Schmidt, 2002). This was supported by the 
lower BET in T1 of the 32 assessors who responded affirmatively to detecting 
mousiness prior to the pre-screening. As WPs are perhaps less familiar with 
mousiness than, for example, 2,4,6-trichloroanisole (TCA), being certain of how to 
separate the taint from, for example, “brett character”, which shares some of the 
descriptors of mousiness (revealed in 5.5) such as animal and stable (Tempère, et 
al., 2014), appears to be important to detection.  
 
In fact, 20 out of 23 assessors of T confirmed an increased certainty about their own 
ability to detect mousiness and 19 assessors confirmed an increased awareness 
after having completed the systematic training programme. 
 
Mousiness is not mentioned in either WSET Level 1–3 or in the WSET Diploma 
materials (Master of Wine H. I. Hansen18 2024, personal communication 23 April). 
Nor is it part of the one-day wine fault clinic in the Sommelier programme of the 
Culinary Academy in Norway. The fact that it is a relatively unknown wine fault 
among many WPs even though they have completed their formal wine education 
may therefore partially explain the lack of consensus regarding the taint.

5.4.4 Potential effects of adaptation 
 
In general, both C and T detected all concentrations more successfully in T2 
compared to T1. However, both groups found it harder to detect C4 in T2 and T to 
detect C7 (Figure 1), indicating the effects of short-term adaptation.  
 
Adaptation, however, is not only relevant to a threshold tasting. In fact, research by 
Dalton & Wysocki (1996) explored the long-term effects of odour exposure, finding a 
significant loss of sensitivity in the subjects of the study after two weeks of exposure 
to an odorant at home while an increase in sensitivity (although not significant) was 
registered for the control odorant. Before any exposure, the subjects in the above 
study completed two threshold tastings where the detection levels decreased 
significantly for both the control odorant and the adapting odorant from the first to the 
second test. Without long-term exposure, an increased sensitivity to both odorants 
across several tastings could therefore be expected. 
 
These results may explain the tendency of increased improvement of C compared to 
T in the RP. With exposure to fewer, but still clear, benchmarks of mousiness, C did 
not seem to adapt as much as T. 
 
The repeated exposure during the training programme for T was much less extensive 
than the research by Dalton & Wysocki’s (1996), and therefore accounted for a 
potentially more limited long-term effect of adaptation. Contrary to the above study, 
the detection threshold of T in the RP actually decreased. Thus, it appears that the 
===== PAGE 47 =====
43 
 
training had a positive effect, although a less intense procedure might, in fact, have 
been even more successful in increasing sensitivity, since a partial effect of the 
training seemed to be a degree of adaptation in addition to sensitization (Dalton & 
Wysocki, 1996).  
 
Thus, the most appropriate level of exposure for achieving the highest sensitivity 
possible is yet to be established and would be a good subject for further research. 
Whether a long-term exposure programme for truly anosmic subjects may induce 
sensitivity would also be worth exploring.

5.5.1 Results and discussion 
 
ATHP was first isolated in mousy wines in research by Strauss & Heresztyn (1984). 
Snowdon, et al. (2006) summarizes research that dates from 1969 to 2002 (pp. 
6467– 6468) regarding ATHP, APY and ETHP where ATHP is regarded as an 
important component of baked bread, crackers, taco shells, tortilla chips, popcorn 
and rice cakes, and is described as having a “cracker biscuit” and/or a “roast-
===== PAGE 48 =====
44 
 
smelling” aroma. However, the latter two descriptions are also attributed to both APY 
and ETHP in their pure form.  
 
Thus far, the sensory interactions between the three causative compounds of 
mousiness have not been explored (Snowdon, et al., 2006, p. 6466) and likely 
depend on their relative concentrations. In fact, when considering some of the more 
negative descriptors of mousiness outlined in 3.1.3 (regardless of the compound 
involved) such as “rodent urine”, “vomit” and “dirty mops”, it would be reasonable to 
assume that these occur at higher concentrations.  
 
The properties of ETHP are less discussed in the literature. APY, on the other hand, 
is in previous research referenced in Snowdon, et al. (2006, p. 6467), explained as a 
key odorant present in wheat bread and particularly in more aromatic rice varieties 
such as Indian Basmati, as well as pandan leaves. Furthermore, it is regarded as an 
important compound in rice cakes, boiled trout, canned, frozen and fresh sweet corn, 
cooked lean beef, dried cured hams, lobster meat, green tea and even tiger urine, 
with descriptions such as “roasted and popcorn-like”, the latter two not unlike ATHP. 
 
The assessors’ personal descriptors of C4 and C8 were used to evaluate whether 
these terms were similar to terms used in the literature in a qualitative context. 
Furthermore, they served to map the variation of descriptors of ATHP in general 
among WPs, and how they differed between a lower and a higher concentration. A 
total of 150 single descriptors were identified (Appendix 5). These were further

example, “attenuation of fruity aromas”, “bitter” and “chemical”, which are terms that 
are less discussed in the literature.

6. Conclusions 
 
Mousy taint is perceived to be increasing and WPs struggle to detect it. Consumer 
risk is therefore growing. Although rapid quantification methods for ATHP, ETHP and 
APY are available for routine screening of wines, these do not replace sensory 
quality control. If repeated exposure to mousiness can induce sensitivity in WPs, we 
have a strong tool to address this challenge. 
 
The results from the RP demonstrate that WPs’ ability to detect mousiness in wine 
can be influenced by repeated exposures. ATHP can therefore be added to the wine-
related compounds for which an induced sensitivity can be achieved.  
 
Nevertheless, it is clear that an in-depth systematic training programme did not 
reduce detection thresholds any more than the simple repeated exposures that took 
place through repetition of the 3-AFC test. Therefore, we can conclude that all that is 
required for WPs to detect mousiness with more confidence is exposure to a limited 
number of clear benchmarks of ATHP. 
 
Thus, a potential approach could be to include proper benchmarks of mousiness in 
wine education programmes such as WSET, Sommelier education and private 
programmes such as Campden BRI in the UK, in order to increase awareness, 
improve the detection ability and therefore also consensus among WPs regarding the 
taint.  
 
===== PAGE 54 =====
50 
 
Importantly, there was little evidence to support the widely-(mis)quoted figure of 25% 
for specific anosmia with respect to mousiness. Indeed, this RP suggests that a 
figure for true, specific anosmia may be much closer to zero. In other words, most 
subjects who cannot initially detect mousiness would be better described as 
“inducible subjects”, which makes them prime candidates for successful training, 
meaning there would be few limitations regarding the effectiveness of such a training 
programme in the wine trade at large. 
 
The assessors’ descriptions of ATHP were extensive and both coincided with, and 
went beyond, what had previously been referred to in the literature for both ATHP, 
APY and ETHP, indicating a similarity in perception across the compounds. Retro-
nasal odour quality appeared to depend on the level of concentration and negative 
descriptors were particularly relevant for the higher concentration. Thus, very weak 
concentrations of mousiness may not actually be a major issue to most individuals at 
all. As a follow-up to this study, measuring the consumer rejection threshold may 
uncover the concentration levels at which mousiness is in fact unpalatable from a 
mass-consumption perspective.

Mousiness is referred to as «the wine fault of our times» by Jamie Goode (www.wineanorak.com, 2020), 
and several other authorities like Alice Feiring (personal communication, 2016) and Simon J. Woolf 
(www.themorningclaret.com, 2017) have expressed concern about the increasing number of mousy 
wines. No updated research exists on the actual occurrence of the taint on a larger scale, however, the 
perception of rising incidences is supported by various sources of research and studies (Snowdon, et al., 
2006, AWRI, 2015, Tempère, et al., 2019, Kiyomichi, et al., 2023).  
Mousiness can render a wine unpalatable and hence represents a significant risk to a wine producer both 
in terms of costs and reputation. Moreover, there is still a lack of both consensus and awareness 
regarding the taint among wine professionals (Tempère, et al., 2019). Thus, discussions on the topic and 
differences in expert reviews can be both challenging and confusing as a wine may seem faultless to one 
person yet unpalatable to another. 
Several studies have found an induction of sensitivity in subjects after repeated exposure to a substance 
(Tempère, et al., 2012, Cain and Schmidt, 2002, Wysocki, et al., 1989). Thus far, no study has explored 
whether this also applies to mousiness. 
 
This research paper will therefore investigate the following:  
 
1) What are wine professionals’ detection levels of ATHP pre- and post-exposure?  
2) Can wine professionals’ sensitivity to mousiness be changed by repeated exposures and 
systematic training? 
3) What are wine professionals’ descriptors of one of the mousy compounds, ATHP, in wine and 
how do these relate to established terms by previous literature?

Mousiness is perceived during retronasal evaluation (Bartowsky and Henschke, 1995), and is most 
commonly detected in a delayed fashion on the palate after the wine has been swallowed or 
expectorated. The off-flavour can rarely be perceived by sniffing as the mousy compounds are not 
sufficiently volatile at wine pH range (Grbin, et al., 1996). When the wine comes into contact with the 
higher pH range of a person’s saliva, however, the taint can be detected retronasally. According to 
Massini and Vuchot (2015) the occurrence of mousiness in wines can be associated with significant 
decreases in the use of SO2, increasing pH levels in wines, and the use of native microbiota in 
fermentations. The fault is therefore especially relevant for natural wines, which use minimal SO2 and 
rely on native microbiota. 
 
Mousiness can be of both microbial and chemical origin. Most strains of lactic acid bacteria and the yeast 
Dekkera/Brettanomyces can produce the character (AWRI, 2015).  
Three N-heterocyclic molecules responsible for mousiness in wine have been identified, namely 2-acetyl-
1-pyrroline (APY) (Herderich, et al., 1995), 2-acetyltetrahydropyridine (ATHP)  
(Strauss, et al., 1984) and 2-ethyltetrahydropyridine (ETHP) (Craig & Heresztyn, 1984). Of the three, ATHP 
is reported to be of major importance to the mousy off-flavour (Hayasaka, 2019). In Kiyomichi et al.’s 
recent research (2023) ATHP was detected in almost all of the wines suspected of mousiness in the 
study, which makes ATHP an interesting compound to investigate further in isolation in terms of 
detection thresholds. The ability to detect mousiness has been associated with a person’s composition of 
saliva and pH on the tongue’s surface (Grbin, et al., 1996). This may explain the findings from the 
Australian Wine Research Institute’s (AWRI) fault tasting clinics, where, accumulating data from 2006 to 
2011, only 75% of 238 wine professionals could detect the taint (AWRI Annual Report, 2011). This study 
is often referred to in articles about mousiness (although erroneously reported 70% instead of 75% 
which was the final result) (www.themorningclaret,com, 2017, www.wine-business-international.com, 
2017, www.wineanorak.com, 2020) and even in wine literature such as The Oxford Companion to Wine. 
It should be noted that the AWRI did not conduct rigorous sensory investigations to collect the anosmia 
data. Moreover, the researchers couldn’t determine the exact concentration of ATHP used in their 
experiment (Adrian Coulter, personal communication, 2022) so presumably it is difficult to say whether 
some participants were anosmic or simply less sensitive than others to whatever concentration was 
being provided.  
In comparison, this study will investigate wine professionals’ sensitivity to mousiness on an eight- step 
scale and whether induced sensitivity can be achieved by means of repeated exposures. The 
experimental design of this Research Paper is based on an in-depth pilot study carried out in 2022 on a 
panel of 30 wine professionals. As no previous research has investigated whether sensitivity to 
mousiness can be influenced by repeated exposures, insight from the pilot study has played an important 
role in defining the most appropriate methodology for answering the research questions of this RP.

The experimental design is based on previous research, books on sensory evaluation and methods, ISO 
and ASTM standards, findings from the pilot study and further discussions with sensory scientists, 
researchers and statisticians defined under “Sources”. 
 
The overall objective of the research is to measure changes in sensitivity to one of the mousy 
compounds, ATHP, in the specific population of “wine professionals” through repeated exposures. 
Changes in detection thresholds are used as a tool to evaluate the potential impact of these exposures. 
To investigate whether a potential change is due to repetitions of the 3-AFC test during “Test 1” and 
“Test 2” (ISO 13301-18) described below or systematic training procedure, the participants will be 
divided into a training group and a control group. The details of the methodology are described below. 
 
The experiment will consist of three phases: two test sessions separated by a training period of 
approx. 2 weeks. 
 
Phase 1: Test session 1 
Objective: Measure the detection threshold of mousiness (ATHP) of each participant 
 
• Participants of the panel: The panel will consist of wine professionals from different 
branches of the Norwegian wine trade: Employees of the Norwegian Monopoly (retail), 
wine importers, educators and wine journalists. The participants will all have completed 
minimum WSET 3/Sommelier degree and currently work in the wine trade with wine 
tasting as part of their work. They will know in advance that they will taste for mousiness 
and their participation is based on their own motivation of learning.  
• Panel size: The intention is to recruit around 40 participants, however a minimum of 30 
participants will be accepted as the final panel size. Twenty-five participants are 
considered sufficient for threshold tastings by Lawless and Heymann (2010, p. 130), while 
Leske, et al., 2013 suggests a minimum of 15-20 panelists for difference testing. The aim 
of the research is not to obtain the absolute detection threshold value for ATHP, but to 
evaluate if it is possible to induce an increased sensitivity in the participants by means of 
repeated exposures. For the same purposes Tempère, et al.’s study (2012) had 16 
participants. The suggested minimum of participants for this research will therefore allow 
for some unforeseen drop out during the experiment.   
• Pre-screening of panelists: The panel will consist of both participants who prior to the 3-
AFC tests confirm their detection of mousiness based on exposure to the highest 
concentration of ATHP used in the experiment, and participants who fail to detect it. All 
participants will therefore have tasted the same benchmark for mousiness prior to the 
tastings, contrary to the pilot study. 
• Randomized groups for training and control: The participants will be equally divided into 
two different groups. One group will take part in an individual training procedure in 
between the two 3-AFC test sessions, while the other group will serve as a control group 
and will not take part in the training procedure. The participants who do not detect the 
highest concentration of ATHP during the pre-screening test and/or the first sensitivity 
tasting measurement will be equally randomized in the training and control group. 
• Location: The tastings will be organized in the Norwegian Wine Monopoly’s seven ISO – 
certified isolated tasting booths with an average consistent room temperature of 19–21°C.  
• Performance of 3-AFC tests by participants: All participants will be exposed to eight 3-
alternative forced choice tests (3-AFC, ISO 13301-2018) in order of ascending 
===== PAGE 69 =====
65 
 
concentrations of one of the mousy compounds, ATHP. The participants are asked to taste 
and identify the mousy sample in each of the eight 3-AFC tests, giving their answers in the 
sensory software EyeQuestion. If they are uncertain, they are told to guess (Lawless and 
Heymann, 2010, p. 129). The participants can taste each sample only once to avoid 
fatigue. They will not be informed of the results during the tasting. During tasting, the 
participants are asked to expectorate the wine after 4-8 seconds. A minimum break of 2 
minutes between each wine is required to avoid residual mousiness to affect a following 
clean sample. This prolonged recovery time is based on the findings of the pilot study, 
uncovering the strong adaptation effect. The participants are also encouraged to wait 
even longer between samples and/or trials if necessary, as the highest concentrations 
may require an even longer recovery time depending on individual sensitivities. The 
participants can rinse their palate with neutral table water in between the samples. The 
participants will also have small pieces of bread/neutral biscuits to eat in between wines if 
in need to neutralize their palate after a mousy example. The participants must complete 
all eight 3-AFC tests to minimize the false positive rate. 
• Concentrations: The concentrations of ATHP are provided by Laboratorios Excell Ibérica 
S.L. The concentration range will be based on the pilot study, previous concentrations 
found in wines (Kiyomichi et al., 2023, Grbin et al., 1996) and a new pilot testing of the 
selected concentrations to ensure that they cover a various range of thresholds. The 
concentrations of ATHP will be provided in solutions in small vials. 1 vial shall be added to 
one 75 cl bottle of wine 2 hours before the tasting (the wines will be spiked in the Wine 
Monopoly’s premises in Oslo where the tastings are held). Whereas the pilot study 
combined ATHP and APY at each concentration step with no consistent dilution factor, a 
consistent dilution factor between the eight concentrations of the 3-AFC test will be 
applied in order to 1) calculate the Best Estimate of individual threshold (BET) for the 
subsequent analysis and 2) avoid a potential matrix effect between the compounds.  
• BET – measurements and criteria for correct answers: Lawless and Heymann (2010, p. 
131) defines BET as “the geometric mean of the first correct trial with all subsequent trials 
correct and the previous trial incorrect”. If all the answers of a participant are correct 
throughout the entire series it is the geometric mean of the lowest concentration and the 
next hypothetical lower concentration. At the top it is the geometric mean of the highest 
concentration tested and the next hypothetical higher concentration. Previous research 
such as Tempère, et al. (2011, 2012, 2019) and Wise, et al. (2008) on the other hand, have 
applied other criteria for a “correct answer” such as the geometrical mean between the 
lower concentration in three consecutive answers and the previous concentration. The 
quality of the data achieved for this experiment will determine the most suitable 
approach in this respect and will be explained and justified in the RP. 
 
Phase 2 : Approx. 2 weeks of individual sensory training period for half of the 
participants of the panel 
 
After completing the first test session, the participants assigned to the training group are provided with 
the following to last over the individual training period: 
• Materials: Small 2 oz glass bottles with wine spiked with the highest concentration of 
ATHP used in the 3-AFC tests and a set of identical bottles of a lower concentration used 
in the same tests. A small vial containing residues of the supraliminal concentration to be 
===== PAGE 70 =====
66 
 
nosed during the training period as it gives a clear aroma of the taint even without tasting 
it (due to the pyridines dissolved in ethanol). 
• Training period: Results from the pilot study revealed that some participants felt more 
insensitive to mousiness following the intensive training period. For the next experiment 
participants will therefore be asked to alternate training with the higher and lower 
concentration of ATHP during the 2 weeks. Each training session will last for max. five 
minutes and the participants will log on into EyeQuestion by means of a QR code on the 
samples to track their commitment to the training. Previous sensory training research on 
other compounds have included training periods running from five to sixty days with 
different intensities (Tempère et al., 2012, Cain and Schmidt, 2002, Dalton et al., 2002). 
The 2 week-period proposed for this research sits in the middle of this range and 
appropriate length and intensity will be based on the pilot study.   
 
Phase 3: Test session 2  
Objective 1: Track any changes to the panel’s sensitivity to mousiness. 
Objective 2: Compare any differences in detection ability between the group of 
participants subject to training and the control group 
Objective 3: Obtain descriptors of ATHP from the participants to answer RQ3 
 
• The second tasting session will be organized in the same location and under the same 
circumstances as test session 1. 
• The participants will be exposed to the exact same eight ascending 3-AFC tests as in the 
first test session.    
• After completion of the 3-AFC test and a short break, the participants are asked to 
describe a lower and a higher concentration of ATHP by means of two separate glasses 
 
Base wine: The same base wine will be used for the pre-screening, both test sessions and 
the training period (Laroche Chardonnay L 2022). 
 
Statistical methods:  
Linear regression with the proportion of assessors detecting the mousy-sample as the 
dependent variable and the concentration levels (on log scale) as independent variable, 
will be applied to test if there is i) a significant difference at the panel level for the two 
rounds of test, and ii) if the difference between the two rounds differs between the 
training and the control group. In addition, individual BET-estimates for the two test 
rounds will be compared for the training and control group using parametric and non-
parametric tests. In all analyses the significance level will be set to alpha=0.05. All data 
analysis will be performed with R (R-project20).

Taints and off-flavours represent a major concern in the wine industry and mousiness is no exception to 
the rule. Up to now, most research on the subject has been devoted to understanding the causes of 
mousiness and recently about quantification of the compounds involved (Kiyomichi, et al., 2023, 
Hayasaka, 2019). The most referenced research to date on detection rates of mousiness (AWRI, 2011), 
however, concluded that 25 % of wine professionals could not detect the taint. Although this result is 
commonly explained by tasters’ different composition of saliva and pH on the tongue’s surface, no 
research has so far explored whether repeated exposures to one of the compounds responsible for the 
taint may influence wine professionals’ detection ability. If so, repeated exposures may potentially 
represent a tool to both reduce the lack of consensus among wine professionals and increase their 
abilities for sensory quality control in wine under normal wine tasting conditions.
