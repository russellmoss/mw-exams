Abstract  Acidity is a key structural element in wine, yet there is little scientific understanding of how people use descriptors to communicate acidity. This study investigates the relationship between language use, chemical composition, and temporal sensory perception in Riesling and Chenin Blanc, two grape varieties known for high acidity. The research aims to address three questions: (1) how wine experts describe the quality of acidity in these wines and whether variety-based differences exist, (2) how the temporal profile of acidity is affected by grape variety and chemical composition, and (3) how the interplay of chemical, perceptual, and psychological factors influences the use of acidity descriptors. Six wines were analysed chemically, including four from established regions (Alsace and Clare Valley Riesling; Vouvray and Swartland Chenin Blanc) and one of each variety from Marlborough. Time Intensity (TI) analysis, measuring the evolution of acidity in the mouth, revealed that Riesling wines reached peak acidity more quickly and with greater intensity than Chenin Blanc wines. An online questionnaire assessing experts’ mental representations of the four established styles identified eight key descriptors used in a subsequent in-person blind-tasting: racy, crisp, fresh, steely, bracing, sharp, zesty, broad. While experts clearly differentiated descriptor use by wine style in the questionnaire, during blind tasting, all terms except broad were more strongly matched with Riesling. Multivariate regression then revealed that descriptor use was significantly predicted by both acid and non-acid components (e.g., ethanol, CO₂), temporal dynamics, and olfactory access (e.g., wearing a nose clip led to decreased use of racy and bracing). 
===== PAGE 5 =====
 2  
Taken together, the use of acidity descriptors is shaped by wine composition, temporal sensory perception, and cognitive expectations. Experts drew on clear mental constructs between wine styles and descriptor associations in a conceptual setting, but these distinctions largely collapsed during blind tasting, where descriptor use became more constrained and perceptually driven, being shaped by acid and non-acid components as well as aroma availability. These findings challenge static notions of acidity and offer practical guidance for wine education and communication: for example, by incorporating acid-specific reference tastings (e.g., malic vs. tartaric), structured sip-hold-spit exercises to capture temporal dynamics, and training that encourages students to articulate perceptual nuance rather than rely on rote descriptors. Acknowledging and embracing the multisensory complexity of acidity can thus help wine professionals describe wine with greater precision, clarity, and consistency.

1. Introduction Acidity is a key structural component of wine, but there exists limited scientific support for how people use descriptors to communicate acidity. Although people may intuitively use specific acid descriptors, such as broad for Chenin Blanc and steely for Riesling, it is unknown what lies behind such multisensory associations. The aim of this research is to link language use with sensory perception and chemical composition (Fig. 1) to investigate how wine experts describe acidity and how these descriptors relate to perception and chemical composition. From a theoretical perspective, it contributes to the study of wine cognition by uncovering implicit drivers behind descriptor choice. Practically, this research offers new ways to train students in perceiving and articulating acidity, with the goal of improving how it is described and communicated.

2. Background and context 2.1 Describing wine acidity  Tasters are taught to assess acidity in different ways. Students from the Wine & Spirit Education Trust (WSET) learn to evaluate the level of acidity from low to high (WSET, 2023), while MW students are also encouraged to state the quality of acidity (IMW, 2021; IMW, n.d.). The quality of acidity is conveyed via descriptors, and these adjectives often blend sensory judgement with multisensory metaphors, such as those referring to vision (e.g., bright acidity), space (e.g., broad acidity), shape (e.g., sharp acidity), or touch (e.g., hard acidity). For example, Tim Wildman MW teaches that Riesling acidity tends to be crisp, racy, and steely, while Chenin Blanc tends to have broad, firm, and tart acidity (winetutor.tv). Nick Jackson MW, in his book Beyond Flavour (Jackson, 2022), takes acidity description one step further by proposing the concept of acid shape, which describes the temporal evolution of acidity level in a single sip, from attack to the finish. According to Jackson, each grape variety has a signature shape that tends to remain constant regardless of origin.  Despite these existing educational references, no scientific research has offered any support for, or explanations of, any consistent use of acidity descriptors for different grape varieties. There is, however, a wide body of literature on the use of multisensory metaphors or crossmodal correspondences that have been consistently applied to describe wine (e.g., Crichton-Fock et al., 2023; Heatherly et al., 2019; Spence et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2024). For example, Wang et al. (2024) found that, when 50 novice participants evaluated the same wine using pairs of multisensory metaphors, they were able to agree at a statistically significant level, on scales of round-angular, fast-slow, and loud-quiet. This suggests that, far from random, there 
===== PAGE 9 =====

may be certain aspects of the wine's chemical composition or taste experience that are driving the consistent use of these metaphorical descriptors. 2.2 Acid composition in grapes  Acidity is a crucial component of grape composition, with tartaric and malic acids being the primary acids present (Jackson, 2020). Tartaric acid is the most abundant and remains stable throughout grape ripening, while malic acid decreases as grapes ripen, due to metabolic respiration processes within the berries (Coombe, 1992). This balance between tartaric and malic acids significantly influences the acidity level in the resulting wine (Kliewer, 1967; Coombe, 1992). Other organic acids, such as citric acid, occur in smaller amounts but still contribute to the overall acid profile of the grape. Differences in acid composition can be attributed to several factors, including climate, viticultural practices, and grape variety. Cooler climates tend to produce grapes with higher acidity, partly because slower ripening helps preserve malic acid. In warmer regions, however, rapid sugar accumulation during ripening has a greater impact on acidity loss than malic acid degradation alone. As sugars accumulate and water content increases, the resulting dilution effect lowers the concentration of organic acids in the grape, contributing significantly to the drop in total acidity (Jones & Davis, 2000). Vineyard management practices like irrigation and canopy control can also play a role in influencing acid levels (Lopes et al., 2011; Reynolds et al., 1996). For instance, irrigation can affect the rate of berry development, and shading from the vine canopy can reduce sugar accumulation and limit the degradation of malic acid, allowing for higher acid retention in the grape (Crippen & Morrison, 1986). In addition to climate and viticultural practices, hereditary factors can affect grape acid content (Kliewer et al., 1967). For example, while Riesling and Chenin are 
===== PAGE 10 =====
 7  
both high-acid varieties, Riesling is inherently higher in tartaric acid, whereas Chenin Blanc is proportionally higher in malic acid (Kliewer et al., 1967). Therefore, it is possible that any differences in variety-specific acidity descriptors could feasibly come from differences in acid composition.  Finally, it should be acknowledged that winemaking methods can have a significant impact on the overall level as well as the acid composition of the final wine (Bird & Quillé, 2022). For example, acidification can be performed via biological (e.g., specialty yeast strains), chemical (e.g., calcium sulphate in Jerez), or physical means (e.g., blending with higher acid must). Similarly, deacidification can also be carried out via biological (e.g., using lactic acid bacteria in malolactic conversion), chemical (e.g., calcium carbonate), and physical means (e.g., ion exchange column). In general, these techniques are employed to achieve desired flavour profiles or to compensate for variations in grape acidity caused by environmental or varietal factors (Jackson, 2020).  2.3 Acidity perception  Acidity perception is the sum of contributions from the wine and the taster. Wine contains a matrix of different organic acids, which is determined by grape variety as well as harvest ripeness and winemaking methods (Jackson, 2020). From the perspective of the taster, acidity perception can be ascribed to a combination of physiological (e.g. sour taste receptors, salivation) and psychological factors (e.g., taste-taste and aroma-taste interactions) (Keast & Breslin, 2003; Auvray & Spence, 2008).  Physiologically, the detection of acidity is mediated by sour taste receptors, primarily located on the tongue's taste buds, which respond to the presence of hydrogen ions in acids (Chandrashekar et al., 2006; Smith & Margolskee, 2001). 
===== PAGE 11 =====
 8  
Acidity also stimulates salivation, as the presence of acids prompts the body to produce saliva in order to help neutralise it (Helm et al., 1982). This salivary response is one way to assess acidity, as higher salivation is often associated with a higher level of acidity in the wine (Schuster, 2000). Moreover, acidity perception can also be enhanced by the presence of dissolved CO2, as CO2 leads to the activation of sour-taste sensing receptors, and carbonic acid forms when CO2 dissolves in liquid, producing a sharp, tingling sensation (Chandrashekar et al., 2009). Psychologically, acidity can be influenced by both taste-taste and aroma-taste interactions. Taste-taste interactions occur when the presence of one taste suppresses or enhances the perceived intensity of another taste; medium to high concentrations of sweetness suppress acidity (“masking”), whereas low concentrations of saltiness and bitterness enhance acidity (Keast & Breslin, 2003). Aroma-taste interactions also influence acidity perception, as retronasal smell and taste combine to create the perception of flavour (Auvray & Spence, 2008). Lifelong associative learning links specific aromas and tastes, so those aromas commonly associated with sour foods, such as lemon, are perceived as 'smelling sour'. As a result, adding such sour-smelling aromas to food can enhance its perceived sourness (Wang et al., 2024).  Recent research has shown that different descriptors are associated with different acid solutions (Honoré-Chedozeau et al., 2024). In a blind tasting with 110 French oenologists, equal concentration solutions of tartaric, malic, citric, and lactic acid were tasted, and experts could select from a list of twelve descriptors to describe each solution. Correspondence Analysis revealed that tartaric acid was most associated with astringency and metallic; lactic acid with roundness, bold, and lactic; malic acid with apple and freshness; and citric acid with lemon. These results suggest 
===== PAGE 12 =====
 9  
that some of the differences in the use of acidity descriptors for different wines could reflect the acid composition of grapes.  2.4 Temporal methods in Sensory Science In Sensory Science, the level of acidity in food and beverages can be measured either by its absolute intensity or by its temporal evolution in the mouth (Lawless & Heymann, 2010). There is a large family of temporal sensory methods, including repeated ratings of sensory intensity at discrete time intervals (“Progressive Profiling”), continuous intensity tracking (“Time Intensity”), or simultaneous evaluation of several sensory attributes (e.g., “Temporal Choose All That Apply”) (Lawless & Heymann, 2010). Where there is a single sensory attribute of interest, Time Intensity (TI) is an appropriate method because it offers a complete picture of how the attribute’s intensity develops over time as the product is processed in the mouth (Peyvieux & Dijksterhius, 2001). TI has been widely used in sensory analysis of wine to evaluate both in-mouth and aftertaste sensations (e.g., Goodstein et al., 2014, Wang et al., 2017), as it captures subtle changes in taste and flavour intensity over time: differences that are often missed by static assessment methods. Put together, this project aims to investigate factors behind wine experts’ use of acidity descriptors in Riesling and Chenin Blanc, two of the most common high-acid white grape varieties, using both instrumental/analytical and psychological/sensory methods.

3.3 Sensory Analysis 3.3.1 Methods A sensory panel of 13 people was recruited to perform the study from the staff and students at the University of Copenhagen (number of participants in line with a typical trained sensory panel, Heymann & Ebeler, 2017). To capture the fine-grained shifts in acidity intensity, participants were trained on the Time Intensity (TI) method over three 1-hour sessions. The TI method involved participants evaluating the intensity of perceived acidity continuously over time (Goodstein et al., 2014), first for 45 seconds while holding a mouthful of wine, followed by 30 seconds after spitting to assess acidity on the finish. These time periods were determined based on both prior literature (Peyvieux & Dijksterhuis, 2001; Sokolowsky & Fischer, 2012) and pilot testing with two experienced sensory panel leaders. Each 25 mL wine sample was served at room temperature (20°C) in a small plastic cup labelled with a three-digit random code. Participants were instructed to taste the entire sample to maintain procedural consistency. During the first session, participants first calibrated acidity ratings by training on two tartaric acid and water solutions at 1.25 g/L (low concentration) and 3.0 g/L (high concentration). Then, they practised TI methodology on iPads (Compusense software, Guelph, Canada) by rating perceived visual brightness intensity of a fireworks video. In the second session, participants evaluated the six wine samples in the sensory booth. During the third session, each participant first had a warm-up sample of a different wine, and then evaluated the six wine samples in triplicate in random order.  3.3.2 TI data processing In line with standard TI analysis, the curve itself is not directly assessed; instead, inferential statistics is to be conducted via extracted metrics: Area under the

curve (AUC), maximum intensity (Imax), and time to Imax (Tmax) (Peyvieux & Dijksterhuis, 2001; Goodstein et al., 2014). AUC measures total perceived intensity over time, Imax

3.4 Online Questionnaire 3.4.1 Methods To identify common acidity descriptors for Riesling and Chenin Blanc to be used in the expert tasting study, an online questionnaire was launched. Participants were asked to list typical acidity descriptors for dry Riesling and Chenin Blanc from four representative regions identified earlier (Table 1). Notably, wines from Marlborough were not included in the questionnaire, as they represent a relatively unknown region for the target grape varieties. After all, the aim of the questionnaire was to produce a final list of eight adjectives based on the most common descriptors for each variety, to be used in the expert tasting.  MWs and MW students were recruited from the candidate’s network and MW and MW student newsletters. To minimise cohort bias, participants were recruited from at least three continents, and with MWs graduating both before and after 20205. Recruitment was guided by a power calculation (alpha = .05, power = .8, and large effect Cohen’s W = 0.5 with 5 degrees of freedom) suggesting 52 participants.  3.4.2 Descriptor data processing The most often used descriptors for each grape variety and each wine type were tabulated after all descriptors were converted to their stemmed forms. Stemming is a common text preprocessing step to reduce various morphological variants of a word to a single root form (e.g., zesty and zestiness would both become “zesti”).

This scale was chosen for its intuitive alignment with percentage-based reasoning and was displayed with visual guidance at 10-point intervals. Each wine was served at room temperature (20°C) in 25 mL portions in ISO glasses and labelled with a random 3-digit ID to avoid biasing participants (Fig. 7).

5. Discussion  This section explores the implications of the findings from each research question, analysing how they contribute to both theoretical understanding of wine cognition and practical applications in tasting and education.  5.1 RQ1   How do wine experts use adjectives to describe the quality of acidity in Riesling and Chenin Blanc wines, and are there consistent variety-based differences?  In summary, the findings from section 4.1 demonstrate clear variety-based differences in how wine experts describe the quality of acidity in Riesling and Chenin Blanc. Results from the Online Questionnaire revealed that descriptors such as zesty, racy, and steely were more frequently associated with Riesling, while bracing and crisp were more commonly used for Chenin Blanc (Table 4). As seen from the Correspondence Analysis (Fig. 10), experts from the online questionnaires tended to use different acidity descriptors with each of the four representative wine styles, showing that even within the same variety, different origins can result in different descriptor tendencies. Results from the Expert Tasting partially supported Online Questionnaire findings in that different acidity descriptors were more often associated with specific varieties in a blind tasting setup. In this case, broad was more likely used with Chenin Blanc and all other descriptors (bracing, racy, steely, sharp, crisp, zesty, fresh) with Riesling (Fig. 11).  5.1.1 Theoretical Implications Based on the online questionnaire, the consistent use of descriptors like zesty and racy for Riesling, and broad for Chenin Blanc, suggests that wine experts draw upon stable, variety-specific language to describe acidity. This consistency reinforces the idea that certain perceptual qualities of acidity are closely tied to the chemical and 
===== PAGE 44 =====
 41  
sensory properties of the grape variety. These findings align with previous research on crossmodal correspondences, where shape, colour, texture, and sound are associated with taste, suggesting that tasters use metaphorical associations to articulate complex sensory experiences (Spence et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2024). The difference between the clear separation of descriptors between wine styles in the online questionnaire (Fig. 10), versus the similarity in actual usage during blind tasting (Fig. 12), highlights a difference between conceptual representation and perceptual ratings of acidity descriptors. While online experts had a clear mental separation of the stereotypical wine styles in the descriptor space, when it came to the in-person tasting, the choice of acidity descriptor usage was much more simplified. In cognitive terms, this reveals a gap between schema-driven expectations and sensory-driven decisions (Parr et al., 2002; Spence & Wang, 2019). This discrepancy speaks to a broader challenge in wine cognition: mental models are neat, but perception is messy. Blind tasting, therefore, exposes the instability of our sensory judgments when detached from context. This is precisely why blind tasting is so difficult, not just because perception is complex, but because our internal expectations often outpace what the glass actually delivers. 5.1.2 Practical Implications The practical implications of these findings are particularly relevant for wine education and communication. Based on the online questionnaire, the consistent use of acidity descriptors for specific varieties, such as zesty and racy for Riesling and broad for Chenin Blanc (Table 4), suggests that those involved in the MW program already have some preconceived ideas for style-specific acidity descriptors. That said, Correspondence Analysis (Fig. 10) also showed that other descriptors, such as piercing, bright, and firm, seem to be used equally likely for all four more established 
===== PAGE 45 =====
 42  
wine styles (Alsace and Clare Valley Riesling, Vouvray and Swartland Chenin Blanc). This suggests a nuanced preconception of acidity descriptors, with some acidity descriptors being variety or style-specific, while others are used for a wider range of wines.  When comparing the online questionnaire with actual expert tasting results, there was a difference in the distribution of descriptor usage. For example, while bracing was more associated with Chenin Blanc in the online questionnaire, it was associated more with Riesling in the blind tasting. This points to the difference between mental constructs of wine styles and real-world experience. After all, not all wines in real life taste like idealised mental representations. This suggests that the participants were in fact blind-tasting and describing their sensations of acidity, rather than guessing the grape varieties and then applying the associated descriptors.  One of the key challenges in teaching and evaluating acidity is the lack of a sensory reference standard for metaphorical descriptors. Unlike measurable traits like pH or residual sugar, terms such as racy or steely have no fixed calibration; who is to say what level or quality of acidity qualifies as steely or racy? This ambiguity can lead to inconsistency in how such terms are used. A related risk is that tasters may rely too heavily on variety-specific expectations: in blind tastings, tasters may identify a wine first, then retroactively apply the “appropriate” acidity descriptors they associate with that grape or region. This reverse reasoning prioritises identification over genuine sensory analysis and may obscure how acidity is actually perceived in the moment.  To support more consistent and perceptually grounded language use, tasting education should go beyond rote memorisation of “typical” descriptors. Structured training exercises could instead isolate and manipulate specific elements in a mixture, such as residual sugar, ethanol, or acid composition, allowing students to experience firsthand how these variables modulate acidity perception, regardless of grape variety.

5.2 RQ2 How is the wine’s temporal acidity profile affected by grape variety and chemical composition? Results from section 4.2.1 revealed significant differences in the temporal profiles of acidity between Riesling and Chenin Blanc, suggesting a “crescendo” description of Chenin Blanc acidity, which takes a longer time to arrive at maximum intensity. However, correlational analysis (Table 6) offers a different interpretation, pointing to a significant positive association between ethanol level and Tmax. This suggests that, rather than variety-specific differences, higher ethanol levels may delay the perception of peak acidity. In other words, temporal aspects of acidity perception are not only influenced by grape variety but also closely tied to the wine's chemical composition. 5.2.1 Theoretical Implications Riesling's faster rise to peak acidity and higher intensity (Table 3) suggest a more immediate, sharper sensation of acidity, which may explain its frequent association with descriptors like racy and crisp. In contrast, Chenin Blanc's slower build-up to peak intensity aligns with descriptions like broad, reinforcing the idea of an extended, more gradual rise in acidity (Table 3). Taken at face value, these differences support the notion of "acid shape" proposed by Nick Jackson MW, where Riesling is often described as having a vertical or immediate acid profile, while Chenin Blanc is perceived as having a more crescendo-like evolution (Jackson, 2022). However, statistical analysis section 4.2 indicates that these differences may not be inherently variety-driven. Chenin Blanc wines in this study had, on average, higher ethanol levels than the Rieslings (Table 2B), which may account for the delayed perception of acidity. A further argument against variety-specificity is that Riesling and Chenin Blanc wines 
===== PAGE 47 =====
 44  
from Marlborough received similar acid descriptor ratings, while the other four “classic” wines were more distinct (Fig. 12). The inclusion of this third pair of Marlborough wines therefore proved valuable in revealing regional or producer influences may exert a greater impact on perceived acidity than grape variety alone. The significant correlation between ethanol content and time to maximum intensity (Tmax) raises important questions about how chemical interactions in the mouth, particularly the masking effect of ethanol, may delay the perception of acidity. This aligns with recent research suggesting that higher ethanol concentrations can reduce the intensity of sourness (Thibodeau & Pickering, 2021), providing a possible explanation for the slower build-up of acidity in Chenin Blanc wines with higher ethanol levels. In other words, it remains to be seen whether perceived acidity evolution reflects variety-based factors (Jackson, 2022) or chemical composition.   5.2.2 Practical Implications As these results suggest, students learning to evaluate wine should focus not only on the intensity of acidity, but also on how acidity evolves over time. Incorporating temporal elements into sensory training can help students enhance their ability to describe and identify wines more accurately, for example, during exams and professional tastings. In particular, educators could introduce a “15-second rule” tasting protocol by asking students to evaluate acidity twice, once immediately upon tasting, and once after 15 seconds, to train awareness of dynamic acidity evolution in the mouth. As Figure 5 shows, the initial increase in acidity takes place within the first 15 seconds.  This exercise would lead to a more nuanced assessment capturing both attack and mid-palate, and help students improve tasting consistency by standardising sip-hold-spit behaviour. This aligns with standard practices from Sensory Science (Lawless & Heymann, 2010), where panellists are routinely given very specific eating 
===== PAGE 48 =====
 45  
instructions, and would help avoid the variability introduced by inconsistent mouth movements. Another important finding was the role of ethanol in delaying perceived peak acidity (Tmax). This has practical relevance for both winemakers and tasters, as it highlights the need to consider ethanol as a factor when assessing the balance of a wine's structure. Wines with higher alcohol may require tasters to wait longer to fully perceive the acidity. This reinforces the importance of consciously extending evaluation time, especially in professional settings, to avoid prematurely judging a wine as flabby. For winemakers, this means that achieving freshness in high-alcohol wines may require elevated acidity or structural counterpoints such as CO2 or bitterness.  5.3 RQ3 How does the relative relationship between chemical, perceptual, and psychological factors influence an expert’s use of adjectives to describe acidity? The results from section 4.3 reveal that the relationship between chemical composition, sensory perception, and the use of acidity descriptors is complex, with only a portion of the variance in descriptor-matching scores explained by chemical and sensory factors. Multivariate regression analysis (Fig. 13) revealed that the choice of acidity descriptors is significantly correlated with both different types of acidity as well as non-acid components, temporal evolution, and aroma availability.  5.3.1 Theoretical Implications First, significant regression correlations in the models do support the fact that language use is indeed shaped by the wine's chemical composition. For instance, zesty and fresh are significantly associated with higher levels of malic acid and lower levels of lactic acid. Moreover, the involvement of non-acidic compounds, such as 
===== PAGE 49 =====
 46  
ethanol and CO2, in shaping descriptors like zesty, steely, and fresh challenges the traditional view that acidity descriptors are solely driven by acid content. These findings suggest that wine experts rely on a broader set of chemical cues when describing acidity, reinforcing the idea that cognitive representations of wine acidity are influenced by multiple interacting sensory and chemical factors.  Secondly, these results reveal some intriguing parallels and contrasts with recent findings regarding the associations between specific acids and acidity descriptors (Honoré-Chedozeau et al., 2024). In both studies, malic acid was consistently linked to descriptors that evoke freshness; in the present study, fresh was strongly correlated with malic acid levels, and Honoré-Chedozeau similarly found apple and freshness associated with malic acid. Similarly, tartaric acid is associated with a metallic character. In this study, steely and racy were most closely associated with tartaric acid, while Honoré-Chedozeau found tartaric acid more frequently described as astringent and metallic. Finally, lactic acid is associated with a lack of angularity or sharpness, with significant correlations with broad in this study and roundness by Honoré-Chedozeau. Overall, the results of both studies confirm the idea that different acid types have inherently different taste expressions, which can lead to a consistent use of metaphoric language.  The role of aroma in shaping how tasters describe acidity is an important theoretical result from this research. Evidence from the blind tasting conditions comparing noseclip versus full retronasal access showed that the absence of aroma significantly reduced the use of descriptors bracing and racy. This suggests that these descriptors are not purely based on gustatory or trigeminal cues, but may reflect learned aroma–acidity associations. In other words, tasters may choose certain acidity descriptors not in response to the taste itself, but because the aroma primes them to 
===== PAGE 50 =====
 47  
expect a particular taste sensation. This finding reinforces prior research on multisensory integration, showing that flavour perception is a cognitive construct where aroma, taste, and trigeminal inputs converge (Spence et al., 2013). It also suggests that acidity descriptors may be less objective than they appear, being shaped not just by the wine’s chemical composition, but by the associative memory of the taster. That said, the relatively low predictive power of the regression models, which explained only about 10-15% of the variance, suggests that acidity descriptors are influenced by more than just the chemical, sensory, and psychological factors included in the models. This implies that other chemical components, such as the aroma/volatile components, as well as perceptual and psychological factors, including cognitive associations and individual tasting experiences, likely play a significant role in how descriptors are selected, supporting the idea that wine communication is inherently multisensory and subjective. 5.3.2 Practical Implications The findings from section 4.3 have significant implications for how people assess and communicate acidity in wine. For wine education, the fact that different organic acids are associated with different descriptors (Fig. 13A), such as malic acid with fresh, suggests targeted training to familiarise students with their individual qualities. For example, students can taste reference solutions of different acids at different intensity levels and learn to generate descriptors that reflect each acid’s unique profile. This would train not only sensory sensitivity but also the ability to use language to describe their sensory experiences, which is crucial in both blind tasting and professional communication (Croijmans et al., 2024). Moreover, results showing the importance of non-acid compounds in acidity description (Fig. 13B) suggest that 
===== PAGE 51 =====
 48  
students should be taught to consider the full sensory experience when evaluating acidity. For example, educators can set up tasting exercises where the same wine is compared before or after carbonation, or with varying levels of sugar or aroma added.  From a technical perspective, these findings challenge the assumption that acidity descriptors can be accurately inferred from chemical data alone. This means that lab-reported values such as TA and pH, while informative, may not be sufficient predictors of perceived acidity. Winemakers, for example, should therefore validate acidity levels through human sensory evaluation, rather than relying solely on analytical results.  5.4 Limitations and future work  While this research has provided valuable insights into the relationship between acidity perception, chemical composition, and (English) language use in wine tasting, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was restricted to a relatively small sample of six wines, which limits the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, there were minor setbacks in terms of wine acquisition, such that the full intended wine selection criteria (Section 3.1.1) could not be met in full. For example, while there was an attempt to control for vintage variation, the two wines from Marlborough are from 2023, while the rest of the wines are from 2022. This was due to very limited supplier availability (lack of producers who made both Riesling and Chenin Blanc from the same region and in the same style) and constraints of a tight purchasing deadline. Additionally, chemical analysis (Table 2) revealed that not all the wines met the intended criteria of having strictly <5 g/L residual sugar. Specifically, Reducing Sugar measures were around 6 g/L for the Vouvray and Marlborough Chenin Blanc, and 10 g/L for the Marlborough Riesling. This discrepancy was discovered only after the chemical analysis was conducted and the wines had already been sourced. 
===== PAGE 52 =====
 49  
Since Total Acidity in all cases was no more than 2 g/L lower than the sugar content, the rest of the studies were conducted under the assumption that the wines were perceptually dry (Noordeloos & Nagel, 1972). Future studies could therefore benefit from a larger dataset with a broader range of wines, including different grape varieties, regions, and winemaking styles.  In terms of population sampling, the majority of wine experts from the Online Questionnaire and Expert Tasting were MW students and MWs, which introduces potential bias. These participants may have been more familiar with certain acidity descriptors due to their training, possibly limiting the findings to a specific cohort of English-speaking wine professionals. That said, the decision to focus on MWs and MW students, especially for the Online Questionnaire, was due to their increased familiarity with using acidity descriptors. The results of this project are intended to support MW students in refining their use of acidity descriptors to communicate with greater precision and consistency.  In addition, participant selection for the expert tasting study was limited to those who could physically come to the study in Copenhagen or London, which limited geographical representation. While the online questionnaire surveyed MWs and MW students, the expert tasting study recruited those who have WSET Diploma certifications, who may or may not have been involved in the MW programme. Ideally, both the online questionnaire and expert tasting study would have drawn from the same population sample, but this was not possible due to logistical constraints and sample size requirements. In the expert tasting, therefore, wine education level and familiarity with specific terms may have influenced the choice of descriptors. As shown in Figure 8, participants were relatively less familiar with terms such as racy, broad, and bracing. The eight descriptors used in the expert study were drawn from the most 
===== PAGE 53 =====
 50  
common terms in the online questionnaire, suggesting a possible discrepancy in language use between WSET Diploma holders involved in the MW programme and those who are not. As the MW programme encourages the use of acidity descriptors, some terms familiar to MWs and MW students may be less familiar to others. Another potential limitation was the experimental design for the expert tasting, where all participants first wore nose clips before moving to the full retronasal condition. This sequential approach could have introduced learning effects, as participants may have developed expectations about the wines during the first round of tastings. Future studies could counterbalance the order of tasting conditions or introduce more diverse sensory conditions to reduce this potential bias. Looking ahead, future work should consider including more detailed chemical analyses, such as gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), to identify specific flavour compounds that influence acidity perception. This could help explain the relatively low predictive power of the models used in this study, as certain key aroma compounds might not have been accounted for. Exploring how these compounds interact with acidity could shed light on the olfactory contributions to the perception of acidity.

6. Conclusions This research challenges conventional thinking about how acidity is perceived, described, and communicated in wine. Results demonstrated that acidity is not a fixed structural trait, but a multisensory and dynamic experience shaped by time, chemical composition, aroma integration, and the cognitive lens through which tasters interpret the wine. Descriptors like racy and broad were shown to vary systematically across grape varieties, while the temporal evolution of acidity and the influence of matrix components, such as ethanol and carbonation, further influenced how acidity is described. Furthermore, while expert tasters demonstrated clear mental constructs linking wine styles to specific acidity descriptors in the online survey, these distinctions largely collapsed during blind tasting, where descriptor use became more perceptually grounded and less driven by stylistic expectations.  Tasting education must therefore move beyond static definitions of acidity and train tasters to observe how acidity unfolds over time. Teaching exercises should include holding the wine in the mouth, noting the timing of peak acidity, and using standardised mouth movements to ensure consistent evaluations. Exercises using single-acid solutions (e.g., malic, tartaric, lactic), comparisons of wines with altered single components (e.g., sweetness, carbonation), and structured timing protocols (e.g., “15-second rule”) can help build both sensory accuracy and linguistic consistency.   Ultimately, this research calls for a more perceptually grounded, temporally aware, and chemically informed approach to wine tasting. Rather than treating acidity as a single metric, we must engage with it as a time-based, multisensory phenomenon. Doing so is essential for improving the quality of tasting education, the precision of

 Acidity descriptors Acidity is a key structural element in wine. There are different ways that tasters are taught to assess acidity. WSET students evaluate the level of acidity (low to high), while both level and quality of acidity should be stated for the MW exam. The quality of acidity is often conveyed via multisensory metaphorical descriptors referring to vision (e.g., bright), space (e.g., broad), shape (e.g., sharp), or touch (e.g., hard). For example, Tim Wildman MW teaches that Riesling acidity tends to be crisp, racy, and steely, while Chenin Blanc tends to have broad, firm, and tart acidity (winetutor.tv). Nick Jackson MW in his book “Beyond Flavour” (2020) takes this one step further by proposing the concept of acid shape – the temporal evolution of acidity levels in a single sip from the attack to the finish. According to Jackson, each grape variety has signature shapes that remain constant regardless of origin. Despite these common educational references, there has been no scientific research to support or explain any consistent use of acidity descriptors for different grape varieties. There is, however, a wide body of literature on the use of multisensory metaphors or crossmodal correspondences that have been consistently applied to describe wine (e.g., Crichton-Fock et al., 2023).    Acid composition in grapes While the factors behind the composition of different organic acids in grapes are complex, ranging from climatic influences to shoot vigor (Jackson 2020), hereditary factors can affect grape acid content (Kliewer et al., 1967). For example, while Riesling and Chenin are both high acid varieties, Riesling is inherently higher in tartaric acid whereas Chenin Blanc is proportionally higher in malic acid (Kliewer et al., 1967). Therefore, it is possible that any differences in variety-specific acidity descriptors could feasibly come from differences in acid composition.

 Acidity perception Acidity perception is the sum of contributions from the wine and the taster. Wine contains a matrix of different organic acids which is determined by grape variety as well as harvest ripeness and winemaking methods. From the perspective of the taster, acidity is due to a combination of physiological (e.g. sour taste receptors, salivation) and psychological (e.g., taste-taste and aroma-taste interactions) factors. In sensory science, the intensity of acidity in food and drink can be measured either by its absolute intensity or by its temporal evolution in the mouth using Time Intensity (e.g., Goodstein et al., 2014).   Put together, this study aims to investigate factors behind wine experts’ use of acidity descriptors in Riesling and Chenin Blanc, two of the most common high-acid white grape varieties, using both instrumental/analytical and psychological/sensory methods.
