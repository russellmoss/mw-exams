Theory exam chair report, Beverley Blanning MW

The gradual return to a post-Covid world has coincided with an improvement in the quality of answers in the theory exam this year, where we saw more high-quality papers achieving a passing grade than in 2021. This report provides question-by-question examiner feedback on the 2022 exam, complied in each case by the paper chairs.

It is clear that a few key issues are preventing promising candidates from scoring sufficiently high marks to pass. I am focusing on these here, in the hope that candidates can better understand what is required to pass the theory exam.

1. A basic, but important, point to remember is that this is an examination of communication in writing and this should always be front of mind when approaching these questions.

2. Candidates should always answer the question asked, not the question they wish had been asked, or one prepared earlier on a similar topic. If a question is in two parts, both parts must be addressed fully. Candidates frequently lose marks by focusing on the initial 'what' and omitting the subsequent 'why' part of a question. The MW exam is a test of knowledge but, more importantly, a test of understanding. We are looking for evidence of understanding that goes beyond rote learning and description. We expect accurate facts as a minimum, but the questions are designed to elicit a demonstration of mastery of the material being examined.

3. Many candidates reproduce the same examples as their peers, presumably gleaned from residential seminars. While there is no problem with using these examples, if this is all we see from an individual candidate, it is not an encouraging sign. There are two points to

1

===== PAGE 2 =====
remember with these examples: first, they need to be specific; and second, they need to be accurate. Candidates should bear in mind that the examiners will be reading the same thing multiple times and for the same example, the facts and figures we see can vary considerably from one candidate to the next. Examiners do check facts and inaccurate facts do not inspire confidence.

4. It is in candidates' interest to assess critically any and all information given, and this includes within the environment of a study programme seminar. Simple repetition of facts, regardless of the quality of the source, does not constitute analysis. Examples need to be applied to the question asked. Some candidates contrive to use the same example across multiple essays and multiple papers, often regardless of its relevance to the question.

5. Facts and assertions need to be attributed appropriately and, if quoting a study, it is appropriate to give the name and nature of the study, and when and where it was published. "According to Wine Intelligence" or "Studies show" do not constitute supporting evidence for a claim or point of view. Quoted opinions should be attributed to individuals, not to companies: "Santa Rita says…" is insufficient (and indeed implies the candidate thinks Santa Rita is a person).

6. For every scientific study, there is very often another that comes to a different conclusion. It is important that candidates show they understand differences in points of view and have gone the extra mile to demonstrate their own mastery of the subject matter.

7. If invited to give a personal opinion in a "what would you do?" or "what do you consider?" type of question, candidates need to show their informed opinion, based on their own experience, research and knowledge. There are few marks to be gained by writing something like "Glyphosate has been shown to be carcinogenic" with nothing more to back it up.

8. If candidates choose to write in English, they need to ensure that their English is sufficiently fluent for communication at the high level required to pass this exam. Success in the theory exam relies on an ability to communicate ideas, argue clearly and write in complete, correct sentences. Small errors of grammar, punctuation and spelling are tolerated, but a persistent misspelling of wine names, people and wine terminology is not. This year some candidates submitted (typed) scripts that were barely intelligible and certainly not at the standard of communication required to pass. This feels like a wasted effort, and one has to imagine these candidates would have fared better writing in their own language.

Many of these points are echoed in the Paper Chair reports below, which contain invaluable commentary on all of the questions featured in the 2022 exam. I hope it is a useful tool in preparing for success in the exam.

Paper one report 2022: Paper chair, Rhys Pender MW

General comments

The general issues that appeared in the answers are consistent with examiner comments in previous years, namely being too descriptive without analysis, only demonstrating surface-level knowledge, inaccurate examples, or a focus on just a few examples without analysing the bigger picture. Another issue that cost some students a passing grade was the omission of a key component of a question in an otherwise very well-written paper.

Q1. Which diseases of the vine are the greatest threat to vineyards around the world today, and why?

Question 1 had the highest average mark of all Paper 1 questions and answers were of a generally high standard. Examiners were looking for a good understanding of disease threats around the world showing global knowledge and analysis as to why and how they are a threat.

The main negative comments for this question were that many students, while showing good knowledge of the different diseases of the vine, failed to analyse why they were a threat. Candidates were required not only to show a knowledge of the diseases, but to provide detail on the nature of their threat: economic cost, spread, and impact on the long-term success of vineyards. A lot of examples were devoted only to how producers are managing diseases without analysis of the threat. Better-known diseases like mildews and trunk disease were generally well covered but often other diseases like Red Blotch, Pierce's Disease and Flavescence Dorée were omitted.

Most impressive was when candidates combined the textbook knowledge of how the diseases work and what is being done to manage them with a discussion of how and why they are a threat to vineyards, thus showing the critical thinking needed to pass the MW exam. The less successful candidates just described various diseases.

Q2. What effects do vineyard pests have on grape quality and how do wine grape growers control them?

For this question examiners were looking for a broad understanding of the different vineyard pests and how they are controlled, linking the impact back to grape quality.

While most candidates showed knowledge of a number of vineyard pests and control options, too often the link to grape quality was weak or non-existent. Some candidates discussed wine quality rather than grape quality and others missed making the link altogether. There were many misunderstandings relating to the basic biology of plants, insects and pathogens, vine physiology, insects, disease impacts and vectors.

Nearly every candidate missed considering some of the key pests, so despite some generally good essays, there were often holes that were too big to warrant a passing grade. There are many different vineyard pests and they have different impacts in different regions of the world. To be successful a student needed to show this global understanding and show the different control options in a range of situations. Many essays just read as a list of pests with one possible

Q3. To what extent does the geology of a vineyard affect the way it is managed?

This question required candidates to take a broad look at all aspects of vineyard management to identify to what extent the geology of a vineyard impacts, for example, drainage, irrigation, CEC, nutrition, weed control, vigour, fertilizers, vineyard equipment, vine trellising, erosion, tilling and cover crops.

There was a tendency to write either about geology, soil, or management, with few candidates linking it all together as required by the question. Some candidates seemed to be confused about how a soil's texture differs from its geological makeup and how these things have different impacts on management.

The passing papers considered "to what extent" well, and touched on a broad variety of management situations.

Q4. Glyphosate use is coming under increasing scrutiny. Should it be banned in modern viticulture? What are the alternatives?

Glyphosate is always a controversial topic and many people have strong opinions on it. This question was designed to test a candidate's ability to be able to consider all sides of the issue in a non-biased way, yet clearly come down with an opinion on whether it should be banned. This skill is paramount in passing any MW paper.

Some candidates clearly let their personal opinions be known without showing proper unbiased consideration of the pros and cons, often referencing only headline statements without going any deeper. Many answers were full of examples and information but completely one-sided. This question was a chance to show a considered opinion and build an argument towards a conclusion, citing examples along the way. The best answers did just that, discussing the pros and cons objectively, showing a broad understanding of alternatives and considering financial and logistical implications, as well as the long-term health of the vineyard.

Q5. As a viticulturist, what factors would influence your approach to growing cover crops in a vineyard?

This was the most popular question amongst candidates, but answers were mixed. For many candidates, this quite complex question, touching on many areas of grape growing, highlighted only a top-level understanding of viticulture. In many cases, essays were filled with lots of examples of what viticulturists/producers do regarding the use of cover crops without much critical analysis of why.

Successful candidates showed an understanding of the purpose of using a cover crop, the limiting factors (such as climate, irrigation, soil type, planting density, microclimate, humidity/temperature, and vine details such as age, vigour, health, etc.), the cover crop options and timing of when to use them. Importantly, different cover crop options may work in one situation but not in another and this needed to be analysed and explained, supported by a broad range of global examples.

Cover crops can also have simultaneous risks and rewards, and the best papers showed an understanding of this. The less successful papers showed only one side of the equation or were too top-level, lacking sufficient detail. To be successful, candidates needed to consider things like soil water, soil temperature, drainage, frost, nutrients, pests/IPM, humidity, vigour, erosion, compaction, equipment access, cultivation and financial aspects.

Q6. What factors affect the timing of pruning in vineyards around the world?

The second most popular question, this tested an understanding of different pruning considerations globally based on unique situations in vineyards across the world. This could be potential winter damage in extreme regions, rainfall in others, frost risk somewhere else or labour logistics, for example.

Timing of pruning and its potential influence on bud break is crucial. Trunk disease risk and attempting to hedge against unpredictable climate needed consideration, along with both dormant and green pruning. A few candidates failed to mention green pruning at all, reducing the potential for higher marks in otherwise strong papers.

Most candidates demonstrated a good understanding of dormant pruning and its timing but fewer made the link with green pruning and how its timing might influence growth, ripeness, pests and diseases and wine style.

A lot of essays drifted off into method of pruning (which was relevant only if linking things like mechanical pruning to speed), or into topics such as frost management which was not helpful to answer the question. Some candidates seemed to think bud break happens at the same time everywhere in the world and that fires and hail happen on a regular schedule that you can prune around.

Many papers seemed only to have top-level knowledge of pruning considerations and some candidates seemed to be just parroting back information they had heard from producers without understanding why, or when such actions may or may not be appropriate.

Paper two report 2022: Paper Chair, Richard Kershaw MW

General comments

The examiners on paper two noted two significant areas that could enhance answering the questions. The first is transcribing knowledge learnt into a cohesive answer. Many candidates appear to have garnered knowledge from a wide-ranging global base, which is to be credited and helps elucidate answers with pertinent examples. The issue, however, is that they struggle to adapt this knowledge, either through a flawed interpretation or because they lack sufficient technical details. A suggestion for students would be to put that gained information into something coherent that can be drawn upon later - perhaps asking the producer more detail behind the question.

Secondly, candidates need to ensure that their example supports their argument. There were some cases when candidates gave an example, but it was unclear what the purpose of the example was. Better scripts explained the argument and then supported it with an example. Remember that Paper two is technical and needs technical metrics to do justice to all the questions thoroughly. The top papers showed a good range of original examples, but the lesser papers used several stock repetitious examples that appeared to have been gleaned from a residential seminar as they were seen in numerous essays. A few candidates used the same producer as an example throughout the essay or stayed within a specific region or country. This displays limited experience in the field.

Candidates would benefit from spending a small amount of time planning their essays and a short amount of time re-reading them. This time would iron out typos and confusing statements, making for a potentially better essay mark. Even under time pressure, the correct spelling of key words, names, and techniques is vital as errors convey a shoddy overall impression. In addition, typos break up the flow of the answer, and examiners often struggle to determine what is stated – is it "bitter" or "better"? Poor grammar is also a common problem. If an answer is incoherent, it is difficult to award marks. Candidates who are not confident in their written English are encouraged to have their scripts translated, as it would help them enormously.

Q1. Explain the process of malolactic fermentation in winemaking. How, why and when is it employed?

Candidates needed to demonstrate a detailed knowledge of the malolactic process and the technical skill to explain how, why and when it is employed. The question was widely attempted and achieved the highest pass rate. The strong passes displayed a thorough technical understanding of the process and backed this up with relevant metrics, using a range of pertinent examples that clarified the points being made. Lesser papers contained vague, unconnected examples that did not apply to the process stated or were too general, e.g., "X winery does MLF on reds"!

The question called for a definition or reference to Lactic Acid Bacteria. Several papers failed to mention the essential bacteria, Oenococcus Oeni, and even fewer cited Lactobacillus or Pediococcus. The core answer is divided neatly into a framework that many candidates used to address the points discussed. Poorer scripts rambled, making the points they were addressing unclear.

Some technical detail was required to do justice to the question. For MLF to occur, the general metrics of pH, alcohol, temperature, and SO2 should all have warranted discussion. However, many papers only touched on a couple of these. Also a problem was that the values ascribed were poorly understood except by the top candidates. "pH influenced MLF" means nothing without clarifying that low pH inhibits MLF, and preferably giving a value of, say, 3.2. Whilst many commercial LAB can survive higher alcohol levels, most candidates failed to mention alcohol as a limiting factor, especially relevant since many assumed MLF usually takes place naturally.

Overall, most candidates were able to answer the "why" MLF is employed part and thus scored some valuable marks. The "how" required technical detail, and many candidates lacked the accuracy or understanding required.

Q2. Examine the role of yeast lees during wine maturation.

This question called for a focus on the effects of yeast lees in maturing wine post-fermentation and the breadth of styles that lees impact, including sparkling and still wines, red and white, oaked or unoaked. A reference to "natural" wines, skin contact and fortified wines would also have been beneficial.

This question covered a fair amount of ground. Despite some good answers, it was the least answered question on the paper – perhaps many saw it as too technical. This issue was undoubtedly true of a few candidates, as many failed to show more than a basic understanding of the role of yeast lees during maturation.

The answer required a solid definition of what yeast lees comprise, their formation, and their overall roles. A reference to 'sur lie' maturation would have been helpful. The essay could have been structured into the overall role that lees play, from their protective and stabilising qualities in enhancing wine quality to the role in dealing with negative impacts such H2S. Answers could have covered the effect lees have on flavour and referenced particular styles affected by lees. The examiners did not expect candidates to cover every last point but to have an overall understanding of the prominent, salient roles of yeast lees.

Most candidates were able to describe yeast lees and their critical role in developing flavour and texture. Whilst examples focussed on wine styles where maturation on lees is required, e.g. Champagne, Burgundy and Muscadet sur lie, few seemed aware of the role of lees in red wine maturation.

Better answers showed technical understanding of the compounds released during maturation on lees, how they are managed practically in the winery, and how they impact wine development, texture, aroma, and protein and tartrate stability. Whilst the role of yeast lees as an oxygen scavenger was covered effectively by most, few candidates discussed the negative aspects or risks of ageing on lees, such as reduction/H2S formation. Many answers did not even comment on bâtonnage. Only a handful considered the benefits of lees in terms of stability, reducing the need for SO2 and colour retention.

Q3. What are the options available for pre-and post-fermentation maceration in winemaking? What factors inform their use?

This was a popular question, requiring a clear understanding of maceration and how this extraction process contributes to wine style, quality and longevity. Whilst the question appeared red wine focussed, other wine styles also needed to be covered.

Many of the essays lacked definitions, leading to confusion about which extraction method the candidate was addressing. A sensible approach would have been to split the two practices into pre- and post-fermentation maceration. Pre-fermentation practices include cold soaks, carbonic, heat extraction and skin contact for whites. Post-fermentation should have identified extended skin contact, along with risks and benefits. The pre-fermentation maceration section was often covered better, but many students failed to answer the part of the question referring to post-fermentation maceration.

Stronger candidates included a wide range of relevant examples to support their points regarding the chemical processes that impact the changes in wine style. Some answers, however, failed to include basic technical details such as the length and temperature of maceration, and confused extraction methods during fermentation as pre- or post-maceration.

Lastly, a few mentioned cold stabilisation and mixed-up crushing and pressing (as in doing skin contact after pressing), suggesting a lack of experience and understanding. Some also discussed cap management during fermentation, showing how easy it is to fail an essay when not reading the question correctly.

Q4. How can a winemaker ensure consistency in a wine's style over a number of years?

Despite this question's popularity, it was the poorest answered on the paper. It provided plenty of scope for wide-ranging discussion. Candidates were not expected to cover every possible nuance but instead focus on those practices that were most relevant, and the better candidates were able to show their knowledge of the topic and demonstrate their depth of experience from wineries around the globe.

Spending time planning here was essential in order to do justice to the question. The best candidates thought about how to structure the answer and provided clear definitions for style and consistency. For example, one approach was to begin by identifying those elements that work against a wine style (e.g., vintage variations, unfavourable weather effects, etc.) and thus consistency before moving on to how techniques can be employed to help achieve a consistent wine style, such as blending, pH, management of alcohol and residual sugar, flavour profile and maturation choices.

The better answers noted the main types of wine where consistency was needed and explained why, then addressed options available to achieve this, using examples that were varied and valid. Poorer answers usually omitted to discuss why or how a procedure could help achieve consistency, often stating that doing the same thing every year (same grape sources, same yeasts, same temp, blend recipe, etc.) could be relied upon to achieve consistency. In certain circumstances this may be true, but if the production method irons out vintage creases, e.g. in fortified, this needed explaining.

Several answers had rather sloppy examples, e.g. “X likes to do this with his wines” without explaining how and why that procedure could work or by contrasting with another producer who chooses to do something else. It appeared the candidates relayed back information they had been told without comment or question and therefore did not adequately support their argument.

Q5. Describe what a winemaker can do when faced with each of the following situations:
    a. Stuck fermentation
    b. Oxidative characters, including mouse taint
    c. Early Brettanomyces infection

This was not a popular question, and was also one of the least well-answered. It appeared that candidates felt the stuck fermentation of Part a) was the 'banker' and, whilst all the candidates recognised Brettanomyces, the question did not ask for 'everything I know on Brett'. Part b) seemed to cause problems, and many candidates struggled to show any understanding of mouse taint. Good technical knowledge was needed across all three parts to do well in this question. A number of candidates devoted too much of their answer to one or two parts and not enough on the other(s).
    a. We expected candidates to explain what parameters constitute a stuck fermentation and the issues that can arise if left untreated, followed by the steps a winemaker can take to rectify the problem. The better candidates gave a relevant list of how the situation could have arisen, followed by more straightforward fixes such as temperature and aeration before tackling the more laborious processes of mixing with successful ferments, yeast hulls or inoculation. Only one essay mentioned high levels of fructose as a potential issue. The poorer answers suggested that winemakers would only know that the fermentation had stuck once serious spoilage and off smells had set in; they often included some irrelevant suggestions, such as bottling wines with RS if they could not start it or how to get MLF going. Nonetheless, this section was answered the best.
    b. The question stipulated that mouse taint should form part of the answer but we expected all forms of oxidative characters to be covered. Very few scripts managed to address this answer with any technical knowledge. Several candidates got muddled between the types of oxidation without clearly identifying the causes; others just gave a scattergun approach to remedies without considering relative effectiveness, cost or any threat to the quality of the solution. Equally, very few answers suggested checking how the oxygen got into the wine in the first place; how refrigeration can minimise it, along with inert gas and good SO2 practices. On that last point, fewer answers recognised that SO2 efficacy is pH related. When mouse taint was addressed, candidates correctly looked at the rise of natural wines but there was scant knowledge regarding how to combat the problem. Whilst it is a challenging taint and possibly cannot be overcome completely, ideas to consider might include sterile filtration, pH management and removal of lees.

Q6. Analyse the options available to producers of high-quality Chardonnay for reducing the risk of premature oxidation in bottle.

This was a highly focused question and, if unpacked correctly, was a chance to score well. The expectation was that candidates analyse those winemaking options that can reduce the risk of premature oxidation in bottles. It was surprising that although the question does not mention Burgundy, two candidates did not reference it at all. Two papers included references to Champagne which is not a style of high-quality Chardonnay with “premox” issues. Candidates needed to show understanding of the chemistry behind each process and have the technical detail to correctly state metrics. Essays that talked at length about “Premox” examples through the ages were unlikely to pass.

Introductory definitions were needed here. There were many ways to structure the essay's core, but one approach would be a brief mention of vineyards before running through the winemaking process, discussing options that reduce the risk of premature oxidation at each stage. Unfortunately, many students wrote an essay on "how to exclude oxygen from the winemaking and packaging process" instead, which resulted in those candidates overlooking any discussion of pressing grapes and resulting phenolic extraction.

Candidates were expected to touch on pH and the impact on wine ageing, SO2 levels during maturation and addition rates. Many failed to do this or, if these were mentioned, they struggled to identify correct addition times and rates. As in question 5, the connection between pH and SO2 does not seem well understood, yet it is crucial to winemaking. Many scripts did not touch upon it. Answers should also have covered phenolic compounds and their effects, lees management, SO2 levels prior to bottling and styles of Chardonnay most affected. Looking at turbidity and glutathione levels made the top candidates stand out. It was important not to cover other grape varieties nor lower-quality Chardonnay.

There was some confusion about whether to mention packaging in this answer. Although this is a Paper 2 essay and not strictly speaking on packaging, it is still relevant to include some packaging discussion in Paper 2 answers where it is relevant - such as this question where corks and closures have been a fundamental part of the discussion of the problem of premature oxidation.

Paper three report 2022: Paper Chair, Caroline Gilby MW

General comments

Paper three is a technical paper and the examiners expect candidates to apply their knowledge to situations that might be encountered in the real world. There is no substitute for visiting wineries, laboratories and bottling lines to understand how processes and topics being tested work in practice, and this is a useful way of gathering material for use as examples too. Strong candidates demonstrated appropriate technical knowledge, supported by real-world evidence and examples. However, many lacked factual knowledge and failed to adapt their answers to the specific question being asked.

Success in Paper three requires accurate factual and numerical information. Factual errors may undermine confidence in everything a candidate has written and imprecise statements like “good SO2 management”, “correct SO2” or “lower oxygen” suggest the candidate does not know the required facts. Key knowledge for MW candidates includes: EU limits for SO2 use, and units (including organic wines), clarity about total versus free SO2, typical levels of free SO2 for various styles, pH, common wine additives (legal limits and levels used in practice), pore sizes for filtration, and so on. It is worth re-reading scripts to avoid errors such as SO2 stated at 250 g/l or a pH of 31, both seen in this year's papers. Inaccurate use of ‘sulphur’ instead of sulphites or sulphur dioxide was less of a problem this year than we have seen in the past.

In general, non-passing candidates wrote superficial answers that were descriptive rather than analytical, lacked examples and numbers, or failed to answer the question, simply writing around a topic instead.  Examples are still required in this paper and should be used to support arguments.  Candidates could usefully compare and contrast, as this is a good way of demonstrating critical analysis. Simply stating a winery name, or giving an anecdote, is not sufficient as an example; examples must support reasoning rather than standing alone.

Defining key terms from the question at the outset is always helpful - it is much harder to write a clear argument without definitions. And answering the question is crucial. There were a few candidates who wrote well but didn't answer the question so could not score good marks. In the words of one examiner, “It is very apparent that candidates are in general producing an answer which is ‘one I made earlier’… they make an attempt in the introduction to indicate they've understood the angle of the question, but they then proceed simply to reproduce an earlier essay written on the broad topic, which therefore doesn't answer the question.” It was also noted that conclusions often bore no relation to the question, which is not acceptable at MW level.

Another point made by an examiner is that many essays were purely descriptive, focusing on setting out technical knowledge rather than applying this knowledge to the question asked. A number of candidates submitted unfinished answers, suggesting that they didn't manage their time appropriately in the exam.

Q1. What are the main technical issues a wine producer should consider when evaluating a change from bottling still wines at source to shipping them in bulk for bottling in the destination market?

Candidates needed to show understanding that bottling at source involves the least distance travelled for a wine and the fewest steps in handling, and that all processes are under the direct

control of the producer. A switch to bulk shipping requires the producer to consider distance travelled, time in transit, travel conditions (e.g., extreme temperatures), the potential for oxygen ingress or contamination, and additional handling/pumping of the wine. The key technical issues address how to maintain the quality and stability of the wine during the extended journey to the new point of bottling, and how this might be checked through sampling and records.

Answers needed to consider appropriate cellar stabilisation treatment depending on wine style prior to loading; microbial stability, ensured by appropriate preservative and filtration treatment; chemical stability, ensured by appropriate preservative treatment and loading process; QC of wine on loading and again on arrival with samples taken pre- and post-shipment.

Examiners expected candidates to show good technical understanding of key QC analyses and QA treatments that the producer should carry out pre-shipment, and also the method of shipment (tanker/flexi/ISO) with implications for management of ullage and temperature stability.

A lot of candidates slipped into discussion of bulk wine shipping in general rather than considering the change in procedures as required by the question. Some candidates wrote about off-topic issues such as bulk shipping container options, or market perception – these were not the “technical issues” which were clearly asked for in the question. Examiners also noted that some candidates saw this purely as a question on bulk wine transport tanks. Overall, many scripts were very light on QC, QA and required treatments and at what point they should occur. The key QA aspect of sampling, and the importance of samples, were often missed. Many candidates stated the percentage of wine shipped in bulk every year, but numbers varied quite widely and sources were not often cited. Weaker candidates were able to write a fair script on some aspects of bulk wine shipment, but could not achieve a passing mark as they did not answer the question asked.

Q2. Outline the winemaker's key considerations when deciding whether or not to filter each of the following in preparation for bottling:
        a) white wine with 180g/L residual sugar
        b) ruby port
        c) Bourgogne Pinot Noir

Filtration should be viewed as part of the overall process of wine clarification. In each case, candidates needed to consider whether not filtering is an option and why i.e. consider how vulnerable the product is to post-bottling risks, most notably fermentation in bottle by yeast (S. cerevisiae as well as spoilage yeasts like Brett) or malolactic bacteria.
Areas to cover included: impact of filtration on wine quality; clarification before filtration to save time, filter wastage and money; understanding of the style of wine to determine the correct filtration process and then the type of filtration itself; filtration options and pore sizes; risks of microbial contamination; re-fermentation.
    a) This would likely be a late harvest or botrytised wine with the presence of complex long-chain polymers that can make filtration difficult, thus the use of glucanase enzymes would be appropriate, followed by a filterability test. 180g/L residual sugar implies the risk of re-fermentation by yeast. Malic acid may also be present and an additional risk. The winemaker would always consider sterile filtration which is 0.45 micron. The winemaker would likely also have a high (approximately 40ppm) FSO2 at bottling.
    b) Fermentation is arrested by grape spirit leaving around 100 g/l residual sugar and alcohol 19%-20%.  Fortification reduces the risk of refermentation in bottle. Basic Ruby

Q3. ‘Post-maturation wine stabilisation practices should be used only on low-end wines.’ Discuss.

The purpose of stabilisation is to counter physical, chemical and microbiological changes in wine. It ensures wine won't form hazes or deposits, re-ferment or deteriorate in colour or flavour once bottled and reaches the consumer in the intended condition.

Some methods of stabilisation may have undesirable effects on wine quality, so the choice of whether or not to stabilise, or which method to use, will depend on the style and quality of the wine, and how it has been made and matured in the cellar. Candidates should have discussed the potential detrimental effects of stabilisation e.g. stripping of colour, flavour or aroma. Candidates also needed to define a ‘low-end’ wine - likely to be a high volume, entry-level priced wine produced over a short timescale, including all styles (fortified, sparkling, sweet etc).

Main areas to consider included reducing risk of physical and chemical changes such as tartrate formation, colour deterioration and haze formation. Options might include contact process, refrigeration, short-term products like metatartaric acid, CMC in whites, and the option of prolonged natural settlement at cool cellar temperature. Most producers at all levels are likely to want to avoid haze formation and therefore use protein stabilisation. Colour stability should have been considered too, including oxidative changes. Candidates were expected to understand the potential impacts of 'active' stabilisation treatments on quality, hence why it may be thought they should be reserved for low-end wines only. However, many of these instabilities would themselves be thought of as impacting quality and so stabilisation practices are relevant to wines of all qualities and price points: it is the method for achieving that stability that may differ. Natural wines might have been mentioned as an exception.

This proved a difficult question for many. Several candidates discussed why stability might be necessary rather than how it might be achieved and thus wrote less technical answers than

Overall, most candidates covered tartrate, protein and microbial stability as areas for discussion. Very few candidates included any discussion of colour stability. Examiners noted that the use of examples could have been better to support points. When candidates use an example that isn't quite relevant, or there are plenty of other better options, it reveals weak understanding.

This question could have been well-supported by compare and contrast examples, especially where candidates were focussing on discussion rather than technical detail.  One examiner noted, “Like a lot of technical questions, it tests the candidate's practical knowledge, and it quickly becomes apparent whether they have actually visited wineries, seen how different wine styles are made.”

Paper four report 2022: Paper Chair, Simon Thorpe MW

General comments

In common with the other papers, there are certain requirements for a successful outcome on paper four. First, it is vital to answer all elements of the question; there is often more than one component to these questions, so it is worth taking time to plan answers accordingly. It is also vital to use accurate data to back up examples and provide credibility to answers.  Perhaps most important of all is to ensure that the points being made are correct. Relevant names should be correctly spelled. This year, the examiners noted a very high number of spelling mistakes.

The other key point to be made is that in this paper there is often a need to structure answers in a way which shows a deep understanding of the subject.  A good definition at the beginning of an answer can really help manage the examiners' expectations in a positive way, and this might also including defining the scope of the answer to follow.

Q1. How has the global coronavirus pandemic impacted consumer wine purchasing behaviours? Use examples from at least three significant markets to illustrate your answer.

This was a popular and generally well-answered question.  The examiners were looking for a clear understanding of consumer purchasing behaviours that were directly a result of Covid, so this excluded more general trends such as a move towards lower alcohol, or premiumisation.  We didn't want candidates to explore the logistical impact of Covid on the wine trade either, as this was a question about consumer purchasing behaviour, not trade. A good answer could have referenced some of the ongoing consumer trends in the market before Covid but the focus needed to be firmly on those which have been accentuated by the pandemic, or directly triggered by it. Some of these behaviours relate to the direct impact on consumers of the new constraints or norms that came with the pandemic (closing down of the on-trade, for example), while others are more notional – the preference for buying local, supporting small businesses, or a greater willingness to experiment, for example.

Areas that could have been addressed, depending on the wine markets chosen, include:
online shopping; sustainable living; higher sales of entry-level wines and higher-end wines, often leaving the middle range behind; lower sparkling wine sales; rise of bag-in-box sales; secondary markets; the explosion in consumer interest in learning about wine, Zoominars etc; expanded interchange between consumers, restaurants, retailers and producers, and purchase of wines specifically for online events. As always, it was important to have correct and properly attributed data to back up arguments.

Some candidates really excelled at this question. Good scores could be gained by covering all the bases of behavioural changes. Great scores could be gained by looking at whether the changes have continued since the opening up of the markets and even some future-gazing to assess which trends could be yet longer lived.

This question had two distinct parts. The first part is simple marketing theory as applied to the wine category. The second part required more understanding of the role of a brand manager –

As this is a classic question which covers a lot of ground, it needed good structure and a clear introduction, with a definition of what constitutes a wine brand. Good answers illustrated that the key performance indicators might be different in brands with diverse aspirations or positioning – a mass-market mainstream brand might measure success via market share, average selling price and penetration, whilst a luxury brand might look more to prestige listings, secondary market value and reviews. Again, numbers were key when giving examples.

The second part of the question required examples of the management of wine brands. There are obviously many ways of measuring a brand's strength, but these must relate to the purpose or brand essence (there is very little point measuring distribution or share of the market if the brand is a luxury offering with limited volume). This seemed to be a question where there was a good understanding of the theory required in the first part of the question, but less knowledge or perhaps experience in the second part. It was important for candidates to address ‘most important’ and to structure a concise and well-argued answer while covering all the bases and not just giving a list of categories. Answers needed to show analysis of the importance of the influencers as well as just what they are.

This was not a popular question and received the lowest number of passing marks. Paper five report 2022: Paper Chair, Ken Mackay MW

General comments

This paper is your golden opportunity to be expansive in your thoughts and to really open and display your full tail feathers, after so many years of preening them, while you have studied, worked and observed the world of wine. Here are a few words of advice, after reading this year's essays, to hopefully help you get the recognition you deserve for all of your efforts.

The examiners are looking for clear evidence of "mastery" in your awareness of the contemporary world of wine. The suggestion of complete insight this term gives is a great clue as to the approach you should take with your essays. Balanced answers, which cover as much breadth of the topic as you can squeeze in, are essential. In most cases, you should aim, as far as possible, to cover all the broad aspects of the industry, reflecting an awareness of their different priorities. In particular, never shy away from the volume end of the wine business - it's not our dirty secret, but what the majority of consumers engage with, so answers that fail to address it will inevitably come across as out of touch.

Where you can, you should also take occasional opportunities to illustrate some of the depth of your knowledge. This can be achieved through being highly critical in your analysis of opinions, approaches, common assumptions or generalisations; and by using well-chosen examples with specific detail, to support your argument. Although fluency of language is crucial for this paper, to show your individual voice your arguments should always be grounded in reality and supported by some clear evidence, as opinions are not convincing on their own: sales figures, consumption trends, approximate costs, survey statistics, research findings, yield differences, etc. are the best tools you have to strengthen your answers.

This year, as in previous years, some candidates spent a lot of time on background knowledge of a topic, instead of getting on with directly answering the question. Try not to fall into this trap. Grapple with the question immediately: clearly define any terms and immediately get into your logical, hopefully flowing, but more importantly structured, balanced and critical answer.

Finally, although it is important to cover the "obvious" points you are sure everyone will come up with, try to dedicate some space in your answer to developing more original thoughts where you can. This is further evidence of your distinctive understanding and mastery.

The specific comments for this year's questions, below, are a synthesis of the perspective of the examiners. I hope they serve useful for future students preparing for success on this paper.

Ultimately a clear, reasoned answer was needed as to whether a legal definition of natural wine

A good answer required some consideration of the 2020 INAO definition of Vin Méthode Nature and the existence of multiple organisations with their own unofficial definitions and guidelines, such as Vini Veri, Vinnatur, the Natural Wine Association, RAW wine and others. The better answers considered how a legal definition might be more, or less, strict, and how that might be more, or less, useful, and critically examined whether wines in this category are already effectively controlled through the guidance of some or all of the different organisations, as well as the existing restrictions on certified organic and biodynamic producers.

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a relatively new issue for the world (and wine industry) to understand and talk authoritatively upon. Where AI begins and ends is up for debate and that was not being examined here: a good answer to this question did not require deep technical detail. The question offered a great opportunity for candidates to illustrate a broad knowledge of the global wine industry and to future-gaze in a thought-provoking way. The better answers had a clear definition of AI, reflecting capabilities we typically expect would require human intelligence (such as intelligent autonomous operations, sophisticated interpretative abilities, integrated decision-making, and the ability to adapt) and diverse examples of current and possible future applications, to illustrate a broad knowledge of the global wine industry.
