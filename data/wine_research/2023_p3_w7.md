# Mateus Rosé, Sogrape, NV

## Quick tasting profile
Pale salmon-pink, low-alcohol (11%) and lightly pétillant, with simple red-berry fruit and a soft, off-dry palate cut by moderate acidity. Blind, this should read as an inexpensive, mass-produced commercial rosé rather than a serious gastronomic wine — the point of the exercise is recognizing category and production method, not chasing complexity (source: https://eng.sograpevinhos.com/marcas/Mateus/Mateus%20Ros%C3%A9%20Original.pdf).

## Technical specs
- Grape varieties: Touriga Franca, Baga, Rufete and Tinta Barroca (no fixed blend percentages published) (source: https://eng.sograpevinhos.com/marcas/Mateus/Mateus%20Ros%C3%A9%20Original.pdf)
- Sourcing: fruit drawn from multiple Portuguese regions; Sogrape cultivates vineyards across Alentejo, Bairrada, Bucelas, Dão, Douro/Porto and Vinho Verde, with Bairrada (Beiras) — specifically the large Sogrape winery at Anadia — identified as a key production hub for Mateus rosé (source: https://wine-guide.wein.plus/wine-regions/bairrada-doc). No official document discloses an exact regional percentage split. Source needed for precise sourcing shares.
- Vinification: "white-wine method" rosé — grapes crushed and pressed with no skin contact, fermented in temperature-controlled stainless steel at roughly 16°C, then matured on fine lees for about two months before fining, cold stabilization, filtration and bottling (source: https://eng.sograpevinhos.com/marcas/Mateus/Mateus%20Ros%C3%A9%20Original.pdf)
- Effervescence: the wine carries light natural CO2/spritz; historically the fizz came from residual carbonic gas retained through the production process, a signature trait of the brand since the 1940s (source: https://decanter.com/magazine/mateus-249844; https://vinepair.com/articles/wine-101-rose-history-pt-3). Official lab documents for the Original give differing CO2 figures across published sheets, so exact carbonation level is Source needed.
- ABV / RS / TA: 11% ABV (±0.5) / approx. 15 g/L / approx. 6 g/L tartaric; pH approx. 3.2 (source: official Sogrape lab sheet, https://eng.sograpevinhos.com/marcas/Mateus/Mateus%20Ros%C3%A9%20Original.pdf)

## Vintage character
Non-vintage: Mateus Original is blended across harvests for a consistent house style rather than to express a single year, so there is no vintage report to cite. The relevant exam-facing point is brand consistency and industrial-scale production rather than vintage variation (source: https://eng.sograpevinhos.com/marcas/Mateus/Mateus%20Ros%C3%A9%20Original.pdf).

## Producer style
Sogrape was founded in 1942 by Fernando Van Zeller Guedes and created Mateus that same year as an affordable, exportable rosé; its curved flask bottle, inspired by WWI soldier canteens, became one of wine's most recognizable packaging designs. Sogrape has since grown into Portugal's largest wine group, with vineyards across six regions and multiple wineries and bottling lines. Mateus is sold in roughly 100-120 markets, has moved tens of millions of bottles historically, and was reported as the top-selling foreign wine in France as recently as 2020 — making it one of the highest-volume rosé brands in the world (source: https://sogrape.com/article/mateus-rose-the-most-sold-foreign-wine-in-france; https://evaton.com/brand/mateus; https://vinepair.com/articles/wine-101-rose-history-pt-3). Facing the modern shift toward pale, bone-dry Provence-style rosé, Sogrape has broadened the range with drier (Mateus Dry, Dry Selection) and sparkling (Brut, Demi-Sec) variants alongside the classic medium-sweet Original, while keeping the Original's off-dry, lightly spritzy house style largely unchanged for exam-vintage purposes.

## Why it's in this exam
Wine 7 closes 2023 Paper 3, Question 2 (wines 5-7, "different methods of production"), sitting alongside a traditional-method Champagne rosé (Laurent-Perrier) and an ambitious skin-contact, oak-aged Provence rosé (Château d'Esclans' Les Clans). Mateus is the commodity-tier benchmark: candidates must recognize a no-skin-contact, stainless-steel, mass-market rosé at the opposite commercial and stylistic pole from the other two, and discuss its (limited) quality and (large) commercial potential accordingly.
