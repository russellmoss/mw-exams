## Scope and terms

Glyphosate is a non-selective, systemic, post-emergent herbicide that inhibits the shikimate pathway, a metabolic route present in plants and some micro-organisms but not in animals. In viticulture its dominant use is a narrow under-vine strip, one or two passes a year, to remove competition from the vine row. By **ban** I mean regulatory prohibition of its use in vineyards, not voluntary withdrawal by individual estates, which is already common. The question therefore has two parts: is the case for prohibition strong enough, and can viticulture actually manage the under-vine strip without it. I take them in that order and state my position at the end of each.

## The case for a ban

The human-health case is genuinely contested rather than settled. The IARC classified glyphosate as probably carcinogenic to humans in 2015, while EFSA and the ECHA reached the opposite conclusion and the EU renewed its approval for a further ten years in late 2023. Those bodies were answering different questions — hazard versus risk at realistic exposure — but the divergence itself matters commercially, because it is enough to sustain litigation, insurance risk and consumer suspicion regardless of which is right. The largest exposure falls on the operator, not the consumer.

The agronomic case is stronger than the toxicological one. Sustained bare-strip herbicide use removes the vegetation that builds organic matter under the vine, leaves the surface exposed to erosion and crusting, and encourages a shallow root system in the very zone that is drying out fastest under climate change. Resistance is documented and real: glyphosate-resistant annual ryegrass is widespread in Australian cropping, and vineyards adjacent to that pressure inherit it. And the market has partly decided already — organic and biodynamic certification prohibits it, several large retail and negociant supply specifications exclude it, and regions including Bordeaux and Champagne have made collective reduction commitments. A grower relying on it is exposed to a regulatory change they cannot control.

## The case against a ban

The strongest argument is that a ban does not remove the weed; it moves the cost. Glyphosate's translocation to the root makes it uniquely effective against perennial rhizomatous weeds — bindweed, couch grass, Johnson grass — which mechanical cultivation propagates by fragmentation rather than controls. Removing it forces repeated tillage, and tillage is not a benign alternative: it burns diesel, oxidises soil carbon, forms a plough pan, prunes surface feeder roots, severs drip lines and, on slope, is the single largest cause of erosion. Substituting an under-vine blade for a herbicide pass can mean four to six passes replacing one, which is a real climate and soil cost as well as a financial one.

The second argument is distributional. Mechanical alternatives require capital equipment, tractor hours and skilled operators. A large, flat, mechanisable estate absorbs that; a smallholder on a terraced slope cannot, and on the steepest sites there is no machine that can do the work safely at all. That is precisely why France set out to end glyphosate use but retained it where no viable alternative exists, notably on steep slopes and terraces. Third, prohibition risks substitution by older contact herbicides applied more often, or by grazing and mulching regimes that carry their own risks. A ban that pushes growers to a worse tool is a bad ban.

## The alternatives, and what they cost

**Under-vine cultivation** — rotary hoes, blades, finger weeders — is the mainstream substitute: effective on annuals, capital-intensive, slow, and damaging to soil structure and surface roots if overused. **Permanent under-vine cover or a sown sward** works well where water is not limiting, and doubles as a devigoration tool in fertile, high-rainfall sites; in a dry-farmed Mediterranean vineyard it competes for the water the vine needs and is not viable without irrigation. **Mulching** with straw, bark, compost or prunings is the best outcome for soil and water retention but the bulk-material and spreading logistics make it expensive at scale. **Thermal, steam and electrical weeders** avoid soil disturbance but are slow, energy-hungry and mostly top-kill, so perennials return. **Grazing** — sheep in New Zealand and Australia over winter — is nearly free where flocks exist and impossible where they do not. **Contact bio-herbicides** such as pelargonic acid are permitted in some organic schemes but need repeated applications. Finally, **precision spot-spraying** with optical sensing can cut applied volume substantially without changing the tool at all. The practical answer for most estates is not one alternative but a combination matched to the block: sward in the wide mechanisable rows, mulch under young vines, and cultivation where the soil will take it.

## My position

No — glyphosate should not be banned outright, but it should be restricted rather than left as it is. I hold that position while accepting that the health evidence may yet move against it; if it does, the case changes and I would change with it. I would prohibit routine bare-strip use wherever the site is mechanisable and water permits a cover crop, and retain it under derogation for steep and terraced sites, for young-vine establishment, and for targeted control of perennial weed outbreaks. That captures nearly all the environmental benefit of prohibition while avoiding the erosion, carbon and equity costs of forcing tillage onto growers who cannot use it safely. An unconditional ban is a decision taken on the strength of the headlines rather than the evidence.
