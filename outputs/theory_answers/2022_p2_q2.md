## What yeast lees are, and how they form

Lees are the solid deposit that settles out of a wine after fermentation. Yeast lees specifically are the *Saccharomyces* biomass: whole dead cells, cell fragments and cell walls composed largely of mannoproteins and beta-glucans, together with cytoplasmic contents. In practice the deposit also contains grape solids, precipitated tartrates, phenolic material, bacteria and, in reds, seed and skin debris, so a distinction matters: **gross lees**, the coarse fraction that drops within a day or two and is usually racked away because it carries off-flavour precursors and reductive risk, and **fine lees**, the light yeast-rich fraction that a winemaker may deliberately keep.

The active process is **autolysis**. Once the cell dies, its own hydrolytic enzymes degrade the cell contents and then the cell wall from the inside, releasing mannoproteins and polysaccharides, amino acids and peptides, nucleotides, lipids and fatty acids, enzymes, and glutathione. This is slow — autolysis takes months to begin releasing material in quantity — which is why lees ageing is a maturation technique rather than a fermentation one, and why time, not merely contact, is the variable.

I read the question as asking what those released compounds *do*, across the range of styles matured on lees, and where lees are a liability rather than an asset. Four roles carry most of the weight: texture, aroma, redox protection, and stability.

## Texture and body

Mannoproteins and glucans are colloids; they raise viscosity and coat the palate, adding perceived weight without sugar or alcohol. They also bind tannin and reduce astringency, and they soften the perception of acidity. This is the reason an unoaked Chablis aged on fine lees in tank feels broader than the same juice racked clean, and why Muscadet *sur lie* — bottled directly off its lees without racking, retaining a light prickle of dissolved CO2 — has more mid-palate than its alcohol and extract predict.

## Aroma and flavour development

Released amino acids and nucleotides are precursors. In traditional-method sparkling wine, extended autolysis under pressure generates the biscuit, brioche and toasted-nut character that defines the style; Champagne requires a minimum of fifteen months on lees for non-vintage and three years for vintage, and prestige cuvées go far longer. In still whites, lees contribute savoury, bready and nutty notes and, importantly, *dampen* primary fruit — which is a stylistic decision, not automatically an improvement. Lees also adsorb and metabolise volatile compounds, absorbing some oak aroma, which is why lees ageing in new barriques gives less overt oak than the same barrel regime with the wine racked clean.

## Redox protection and reduced SO2 requirement

Yeast lees are a strong oxygen scavenger: living residual cells and released compounds, glutathione among them, consume dissolved oxygen and buffer the wine's redox state. This preserves fruit, colour and thiol aromatics, and it allows lower SO2 additions during élevage — a significant benefit for producers working with minimal sulphur, and part of why so-called natural and skin-contact wines lean on generous lees contact as a substitute for SO2. The same protective mechanism is the argument for keeping barrels undisturbed rather than racking repeatedly.

## Stability

Mannoproteins inhibit tartrate crystal growth, reducing the cold stabilisation burden, and they interfere with protein haze formation, reducing bentonite requirement and so reducing the aroma stripping bentonite causes. In red wine they combine with tannin-anthocyanin polymers and help hold colour in solution, and commercial mannoprotein preparations are sold precisely for these effects.

## Red wines, and the styles beyond the obvious

Lees ageing is not a white-wine technique. Red Burgundy and northern Rhône Syrah are commonly matured on fine lees post-MLF, gaining texture, colour stability and lower SO2 need. In fortified wine, the flor of biologically aged Sherry is a living yeast layer whose autolysis contributes directly to the character of Fino and Manzanilla, while Ports and oxidative styles are racked clean because oxidation is the objective. Skin-contact whites, where lees and skins mature together, and rosé and sparkling bases, all use lees deliberately.

## The risks, which are real

Lees consume oxygen, and past a point the wine goes reductive: sulphur-containing amino acids are degraded to hydrogen sulphide and, if left, to mercaptans and disulphides, which are far harder to remove. Management is therefore active: *bâtonnage* stirs the lees to speed autolysis and introduce a little oxygen, but every stirring is an oxygen addition, and the modern retreat from aggressive bâtonnage in white Burgundy reflects concern that it accelerates later oxidative development. Alternatives are gentle rolling of barrels, or racking to dislodge sulphides. Lees also feed spoilage: *Brettanomyces* thrives on lees nitrogen in a warm, high-pH red, and gross lees left in place give bitterness and vegetal character. There is a cost dimension too — tank and barrel occupancy, labour, and delayed cash flow.

## Conclusion

Yeast lees are best understood as a slow-release reservoir of colloids and antioxidants whose value is entirely time- and style-dependent. Where texture, savoury complexity and low-SO2 protection are wanted, they are the most efficient single tool available; where primary fruit and clean reduction-free aromatics are the point, they are a liability. The skill lies in choosing the fraction, the duration and the degree of disturbance.
