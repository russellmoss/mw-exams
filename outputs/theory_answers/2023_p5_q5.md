## Ageing is not the same as keeping

Three different properties get bundled into one phrase, and the argument cannot proceed until they are separated.

*Stability* is the wine's resistance to spoilage — microbial soundness, protein and tartrate stability, adequate sulphur, a sound closure. Every competently made wine has it, including a supermarket Pinot Grigio.

*Survival* is a wine's capacity to remain drinkable over years without deteriorating. Many wines have it and gain nothing from it.

*Ability to age*, properly understood, is the third thing: the capacity to become better than it was — for primary fruit to give way to tertiary complexity, for tannin to polymerise and soften while retaining structure, for esters to hydrolyse and aromatics to integrate, so that the wine at fifteen years is more interesting than at three. That is a rarer property, it depends on a scaffold of acid, tannin, extract or sugar sufficient to outlast the fruit, and it is what the question is really about. Answering on stability instead is the commonest way to get this subject wrong.

My position: ageability is of negligible practical importance to the overwhelming majority of wine and the overwhelming majority of drinkers, and of decisive importance to a small minority of wines whose influence on the whole industry is out of all proportion to their volume. Both halves of that are true simultaneously, and the interesting question is why the second half survives the first.

## The commercial reality

Start with the fact that most discussion of this topic avoids. The great majority of wine bought is drunk within a day or two of purchase, most often the same evening, and the buyer neither knows nor cares whether it would have improved. For those wines ageability is not merely unimportant — it is undesirable, because tannin and austerity are barriers to immediate enjoyment. Beaujolais, Provence rosé, New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc and Prosecco are all engineered against the property, and are none the worse for it. A producer of any of them who chased ageability would be destroying value.

The economics point the same way. Ageing is a working-capital problem: stock held is cash not received, cellar space occupied, insurance paid and risk carried. A producer required to hold wine for five years finances five vintages at once, which is a formidable barrier to entry for a new estate and a considerable advantage to an established one. Consumers face a parallel constraint that has grown sharper — declining home ownership among younger drinkers means fewer cellars, storage costs money, and a culture of immediate gratification makes buying now to drink in 2040 an eccentric act. On present trends, fewer people each decade are able or inclined to age wine themselves.

## The answer is style-specific

Ageability is not one property distributed evenly. It is category-defining in some places and irrelevant in others, and a serious answer has to move beyond still light wines.

Among *still reds*, classified Bordeaux, Barolo, Northern Rhône Syrah and traditional Rioja are built for it, and are undervalued when drunk young. Among *still whites*, Mosel Riesling, white Burgundy and Hunter Valley Sémillon transform rather than merely survive.

*Sparkling* wine makes the point most sharply because ageing is legislated into its quality hierarchy: vintage Champagne must spend a minimum of three years on its lees, against fifteen months for non-vintage, and that time is what generates the autolytic character the category sells. Prosecco, by contrast, is defined by the absence of it.

*Sweet* wines are the most ageworthy of all in relation to their alcohol, because sugar and acidity together preserve them: Sauternes and Tokaji Aszú are routinely superior at twenty years. *Fortified* wines split the argument neatly in two. Vintage Port is bottled after about two years and does its ageing in the bottle, while tawny does its ageing in wood; Madeira is effectively indestructible; and then fino Sherry provides the cleanest counter-example in the wine world — a wine of the highest quality whose defining virtue is freshness, which deteriorates in bottle, and for which ageability is a positive defect.

The differentiating point that most candidates miss is that regional law codifies this. Rioja Gran Reserva must spend a minimum of five years ageing, at least two of them in oak; Barolo requires around thirty-eight months before release, with a substantial part in wood; Brunello di Montalcino requires five years. Ageing here is not an emergent property of a good wine — it is a legal condition of using the name, which means whole appellations have chosen to make ageability the basis of their claim to quality.

## It matters to different people for different reasons

For the *producer*, ageability is a pricing mechanism. A wine that improves justifies a release price above its immediate drinking pleasure, and it makes the estate's back catalogue an asset. Against that it is a cash-flow burden, and for most producers the calculation comes out against.

For the *retailer and restaurateur*, it is mostly a cost. A supermarket wants stock to turn; a restaurant with a deep list is financing an inventory that may take a decade to sell, and charges for it accordingly. Only the specialist fine-wine merchant makes money from time itself.

For the *critic and journalist*, ageability is close to a professional necessity. It is what makes scores meaningful beyond the season, what makes vintage variation worth reporting, and what makes the vertical tasting — the industry's most powerful demonstration format — possible at all. A wine that does not age leaves no record for a writer to build a reputation on.

For *consumers*, three distinct groups. The everyday buyer is indifferent. The engaged enthusiast values it as an aspiration more than a practice — they buy for ageing more than they successfully age. The collector and investor treats it as the whole point.

## The economy that ageability sustains

There is a substantial labour market resting on this property, and it deserves weighing. Brokers and the auction departments of the major houses exist because old wine trades. Bonded warehouses, cellar builders and temperature-controlled storage services exist because it must be kept. Authenticators and provenance specialists became a profession after the counterfeiting scandals of the 2000s, precisely because old bottles are valuable and hard to verify. Fine-wine critics, indices and en primeur campaigns all price a future. None of this would exist if wine did not improve.

Culturally, ageability is what gives wine its unusual standing among drinks: it links a bottle to a specific year and a specific place, it makes wine a medium for memory and anniversary, and — as has been observed with some insight — fine wine happens to age on a roughly human timescale, so a bottle can be older than the person drinking it. That coincidence does far more emotional work than any tasting note.

Two cautions. Fine wine represents a very small share of world wine production by volume, so the economy above is built on a narrow base. And much of what the market prices as ageability is really rarity, brand and *perceived* ageability rather than the demonstrated thing — a great many wines are bought to age and never tested.

## Conclusion

Ageability is not important to most wine, most drinkers or most producers, and it is becoming less so as storage, space and patience all become scarcer. To say otherwise is to mistake the trade's preoccupations for the market's.

But it is important to the industry out of all proportion to the volume it touches, because it is the property that sustains fine wine's price structure, its critical apparatus, its ancillary economy and its cultural claim to be more than a beverage. Its importance, in short, is symbolic and structural rather than practical — and structural importance is not a lesser kind.
