## Sustainability, and the test I am applying

Sustainability means meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs, and in general business use it rests on three pillars: environmental, social and financial. Wine discussion routinely collapses it into the first, but a vineyard that sequesters carbon while paying its pickers badly, or a certified estate that loses money every year, is not sustainable in any meaningful sense. I assess every sector below against all three.

"Doing the most" needs a test too, or the answer becomes a list of admirable behaviour. Mine has three parts: **reach** (how much of the industry's volume or vineyard area is affected), **depth** (whether the sector acts across all three pillars or only the easy one), and **leverage** (whether it can compel others to change). A small producer doing everything perfectly scores on depth and nothing else.

## Producers and their certification associations

Individual wineries generate the innovation, but national and regional certification bodies are what convert it into reach, and they are the sector doing most on depth. Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand covers the large majority of that country's producing vineyard area; Certified Sustainable Wine of Chile, Sustainable Winegrowing Australia, the California Sustainable Winegrowing Alliance and SIP Certified operate at comparable scale in their regions. Crucially their standards are not environmental-only: they audit labour practice, health and safety, community relations, and — unusually and importantly — business viability, because a scheme that bankrupts its members fails on its own terms. South Africa's WIETA is the clearest case of the social pillar leading, having been created to address farmworker conditions rather than emissions.

Above them, appellation bodies add regulatory force. The Comité Champagne has committed the appellation to a collective carbon reduction target and banned herbicides across the region, which is enforcement rather than encouragement.

## Large multinational and family producers

Big producers matter because their supply chains are long. International Wineries for Climate Action, founded by Familia Torres and Jackson Family Wines, is significant precisely because it requires members to verify and reduce emissions rather than to announce intentions. Groups of this size also move the social pillar in ways small estates cannot — health and safety systems, permanent rather than seasonal employment, training — and they are financially robust enough to fund a twenty-year transition. Their weakness is that scale invites greenwashing, and a corporate report is not an audit; the test I apply is whether the claim is third-party verified and whether it covers the supply chain rather than the estate alone.

## Retail, monopolies and the supply chain

This is the sector with the most leverage, and my judgement is that it is doing the most overall. A producer changes one estate; a buyer changes every supplier at once. Systembolaget requires its suppliers to sign a code of conduct covering labour and human rights and audits them against it, which is a social-pillar intervention reaching across dozens of countries. The Canadian provincial boards and the major UK and northern European multiples exert the same force on packaging: shipping in bulk and filling in market, together with lighter glass, removes a large share of a bottle's carbon footprint, and it happened because retailers set specifications, not because producers volunteered.

Beneath them, packaging and logistics suppliers — glass makers cutting bottle weight and furnace emissions, closure and carton manufacturers — deliver environmental gains across the whole industry at once.

## The financial pillar, and who is undermining it

Here the same retailers score badly. Sustained deep price promotion and own-label price pressure make growers financially unsustainable, which is why vineyards are being pulled out in Bordeaux, Australia and California. Producers cannot invest in cover crops or solar out of a negative margin. The sectors genuinely advancing this pillar are those securing long-term grape contracts and transparent pricing, and the certification schemes that make economic viability an audited criterion.

## The laggards

Hospitality and the on-trade do least: restaurant lists reward provenance and scarcity, not practice, glass and food waste in the sector are largely unmeasured, and no sommelier is asked to justify a listing on labour standards. Wine media and competitions are close behind, still scoring the liquid alone. Consumers, despite stated preferences, rarely pay a premium for sustainability, which is why the agenda has to be driven by those with buying power rather than by demand — and why measuring sectors by the change they compel, rather than by the commitments they announce, is the only test that separates them.

## Conclusion

Producer certification associations are doing the most in depth — they are the only actors auditing all three pillars simultaneously and at regional scale. But retailers and monopolies are doing the most overall, because they can compel an entire supply chain to move at once, and have already done so on packaging and on labour standards. The qualification is that the same buying power, used on price, actively undermines the financial pillar. Until retail treats grower margin as a sustainability metric rather than a cost line, the sector with the greatest leverage will keep advancing two pillars and eroding the third.
