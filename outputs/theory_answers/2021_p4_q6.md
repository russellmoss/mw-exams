## What counts as a new product, and how I will judge importance

By new product development I mean anything that puts a genuinely new proposition in front of a consumer: a new brand, a new style within an existing brand, a new format or pack, or entry into an adjacent category. I exclude repackaging and vintage-to-vintage change, which are maintenance.

My answer is that importance varies enormously, and it varies on one axis: whether a producer's demand depends on being chosen from a shelf, or on being scarce. Where the consumer chooses between substitutable options, new product development is close to existential. Where demand exceeds supply, it ranges from unnecessary to actively harmful. I will take three tiers of producer in turn, then the motivations, then the failures.

## Why producers do it

The motivations are worth separating, because the wrong motivation is the usual cause of failure.

The strongest is **distribution**. Retailers ration shelf space and review ranges annually, and newness is the currency in which listings are won and defended. A brand that brings nothing to a range review is a brand that gets cut.

The second is **consumer recruitment**. Wine is losing occasions to spirits, beer, cocktails and hard seltzer, particularly among drinkers under thirty-five, and the category cannot recruit them with the same product it sold their parents.

Third is **margin**: a new tier or a new pack is the least resistant way to raise a realised price without repricing the existing range.

Fourth is **occasion**: single-serve, outdoor and lower-strength formats reach moments a 75cl cork-finished bottle cannot.

Fifth, and least respectable, is **surplus** — developing a product to absorb volume the existing range cannot sell. That motivation is where most failures come from.

## Mainstream branded producers: very important indeed

For a producer selling several million cases into grocery, new product development is not a choice. Volumes in mature markets are flat or declining, so growth has to be bought with newness.

The most instructive recent example is Treasury's 19 Crimes brand running a Snoop Dogg 'Cali Red' collaboration around 2020: the wine itself is unremarkable, but the collaboration recruited consumers who were not buying wine at all. That Prosecco DOC approved a rosé category in 2020 is a category-level version of the same move — it captured the rosé consumer inside a trusted name and did so at a higher price than white Prosecco. Canned formats, no- and low-alcohol ranges and wine-based ready-to-drink lines all belong to the same logic: defending share against adjacent categories on their own terms.

## Regional producers and co-operatives: important, but as adaptation

For a co-operative or a regional body facing structural decline, new product development is survival rather than growth. The Douro built a serious dry red table wine business out of vineyards planted for Port; Jerez and Port developed mixed serves and lighter styles to reach a consumer who will not drink a fortified wine after dinner; Vinho Verde replaced a cheap semi-sweet spritz with single-varietal Alvarinho at three times the price. In each case the existing product had a shrinking audience, and the alternative to development was managed decline.

## Fine wine: mostly unnecessary

At the top, new product development is largely irrelevant, because the product is scarcity and consistency. The great domaines of the Côte d'Or have introduced essentially nothing in living memory, and their demand has never been stronger. A line extension here does not add a consumer; it borrows credibility from the grand vin and repays it slowly.

The genuine exception is the second wine, which is the most successful new product development fine wine has produced. Second labels monetise fruit that would otherwise be sold in bulk, recruit consumers at a reachable price, and do not touch the grand vin. But they only work where the parent brand is strong, and third wines and distant branded ranges show where the elastic runs out.

## Where it has not worked

Three failure patterns are visible.

**Proliferation.** SKU counts expand faster than shelf space, complexity costs are real, and retailers eventually delist both the extension and the original.

**Dilution.** Extending a premium name downwards is easy and almost impossible to reverse; the price the consumer will pay is set by the cheapest bottle carrying the label.

**Novelty without repeat purchase.** Blue wine, heavily flavoured wines and most of the first decade of no-alcohol products generated publicity and no second purchase, because the motivation was surplus or noise rather than an identified consumer need.

The cautionary case is Beaujolais Nouveau: a spectacularly successful piece of product development that then defined the region in the consumer's mind for thirty years and took a generation to escape. A new product changes what a producer is understood to be, and that is not always recoverable.

## Conclusion

How important is it? Very, for the great majority of wine by volume, where the alternative to development is delisting. Barely at all for the small fraction whose demand exceeds supply, where it is at best a distraction. The producers who get it wrong are rarely those who do too much or too little; they are those who extend a brand when they needed a new one, or invent a product when what they needed was a better version of the one they had.
