## How I am measuring "making money"

Revenue is not profit, and profit is not return. I judge each business below on operating margin and on the return earned on the capital it employs, because this industry's characteristic trap is a respectable margin earned on an enormous asset base — land, oak and slow-moving stock — that destroys the return. Two propositions frame the answer. First, money is made where there is either a structural barrier to entry or ownership of the customer relationship; it is almost never made by growing grapes. Second, my three examples sit deliberately at different points of the chain — an input supplier, a brand owner and a retailer — and each earns its money by a different mechanism.

## Inputs: Corticeira Amorim

Amorim is the world's largest cork producer, listed in Lisbon and therefore publicly accountable. It runs a high-teens EBITDA margin, on trade estimates, on annual sales in the hundreds of millions of euros, sustained through a decade in which its customers' own fortunes swung violently.

Three things explain that. Supply is structurally constrained: cork oak takes decades to reach first harvest and, under Portuguese conservation law, may be stripped only roughly once a decade, and the forest is concentrated in Portugal and Spain, so no competitor can buy its way into the position at any price. Demand is fragmented: Amorim sells to tens of thousands of wineries, almost none of which has purchasing leverage over it. And it answered the screwcap threat by investing in TCA remediation and then segmenting a commodity into a graded range with a premium technical tier, converting a price-taker into a price-setter.

This is the picks-and-shovels position, and it is the most dependable profit in wine: sell *to* the industry rather than *in* it and you are indifferent to which brand wins.

## Brand ownership: LVMH's Wines and Spirits division

Moet Hennessy reports a recurring operating margin of roughly 30 per cent — several times what a good branded still-wine business earns, and earned on Champagne and cognac rather than on any technical advantage.

The mechanism is legally guaranteed scarcity plus ownership of the route to market. Champagne's appellation area is fixed by law, so the raw material cannot expand with demand; LVMH owns a large vineyard holding, contracts much of the rest, and releases stock against demand rather than clearing it. It then owns distribution in most significant markets, so it captures the importer's and often the distributor's margin as well as the producer's, and it controls how the price is presented at the point of sale rather than surrendering that to a third party. Marketing spend at a scale no independent house can match defends the price. Return on capital stays high despite very heavy ageing inventory because the price per bottle carries it.

Costco is widely regarded as one of the largest wine retailers in the world by value, and it repays close reading because it does not make its money on the wine. It is widely reported to cap its mark-up in the mid-teens percentage range on branded goods and its own Kirkland Signature label - well below a supermarket's typical wine gross margin.

Wine is therefore a traffic-driving, basket-building category carried at a deliberately thin margin to make the membership renewal feel earned. Return on capital is nonetheless high, because a narrow range turns very fast against extended supplier payment terms: the wine is sold before it is paid for, so the supply chain's working capital funds the retailer. The commercial consequence for producers is severe — enormous volume against very few SKUs, a landed price dictated by the mark-up cap, and enough scale to commission own-label bottlings from suppliers who cannot afford to decline.

## Who is not making money

The counter-case proves the rule. The grower earns least: he carries the land, the capital and the vintage risk, sells into a market that publishes his price, and owns no brand. Mid-sized family estates are squeezed hardest — too small for scale economics, too large to sell out at the cellar door, carrying a winery that is idle for most of the year. Vineyard land in the most prestigious appellations returns very little against its capital value, and is bought for reasons other than yield.

## Conclusion

Money is being made by those selling inputs into a fragmented industry, by those owning protected scarcity and the distribution that monetises it, and by retailers large enough to treat the wine as a means to another end. What unites all three is that none of them is a price-taker. The businesses losing money are those that own the most capital and control the least of the final price — which is to say most of the people who actually grow grapes and make wine.
