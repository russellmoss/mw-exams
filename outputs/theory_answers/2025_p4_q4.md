## Defining the object, because the category is not uniform

A government wine monopoly is state ownership of one or more tiers of the alcohol trade — most often retail, sometimes wholesale and import as well — established for public-health reasons rather than commercial ones. That mandate is the analytical key, because every benefit below follows from a buyer that is not maximising profit, and every one weakens where it is.

The commonest failure on this question is to treat Sweden, Norway, Finland, Ontario and Quebec as the whole picture. The US control states are a materially different animal: seventeen or so US states control the wholesale tier for spirits, a smaller number also control wine retail — Pennsylvania, Utah, New Hampshire and Mississippi among them — and their mandate is substantially fiscal, operating inside a three-tier system whose other tiers are private. New Hampshire's state stores are sited to attract cross-border trade, which is the opposite of a harm-reduction posture. I take "benefit" as measurable commercial or welfare gain, address the three named groups in turn, and argue that the benefits are real, unequally distributed, and contingent on the health mandate rather than on monopoly as such.

## Producers

The tender process is the genuine benefit, and it is underappreciated. A monopoly publishes a specification — style, price point, volume, packaging, increasingly sustainability criteria — and buys blind or near-blind against it. A small producer in Chile or Portugal can win a national listing on the wine in the glass, without slotting fees, distributor gatekeeping or a marketing budget, which is close to impossible in any private retail market of comparable size. Running alongside it is a quality-assurance function: monopolies operate analytical laboratories, enforce specification compliance and audit supply, so a listing becomes a credential the producer carries into other markets, and the discipline forces improvements in consistency and packaging that outlive the contract.

The costs are equally structural. A single buyer is a monopsony: it sets the price the producer must meet and captures the gain from any cost reduction; delisting on rate-of-sale is mechanical and effectively unappealable; and advertising restrictions mean brand equity accrues to the shelf rather than to the brand. Service levels and volumes for a core listing exceed what many estates can supply. The small-and-niche debate cuts both ways and should be settled on evidence: the tender lowers entry barriers, but specification, order size and logistics requirements favour those with volume. The real accommodation for niche producers is the special-order channel — Vinmonopolet's and Systembolaget's order ranges, and private import through agents — which offers listing without shelf space, and therefore without the volume test.

## Retailers

Here the benefit is thinnest, because a full retail monopoly largely abolishes the group it might benefit. There is no independent wine merchant of scale in Norway or Sweden. What survives, and survives well, is the importer-agent, because the monopoly is an efficient national distribution platform — centralised buying, standard terms, uniform logistics, guaranteed payment — that a two-person agency can plug into for a fraction of the cost of building private distribution. Against that, the structure removes retail price competition, assortment differentiation and retail innovation, which is a genuine welfare cost even if the mandate does not weigh it. The US control states show the distortion in purer form: a state retail monopoly bordering an open state produces persistent cross-border leakage, which transfers retail activity rather than reducing consumption.

## Consumers

The benefits are substantial and routinely ignored by the trade. Range breadth is driven by tender criteria rather than by supplier margin, so obscure origins and small categories get shelf space a margin-driven retailer would not give them. Pricing is transparent and uniform nationally, so a rural consumer pays what an urban one pays — an equity outcome private retail never delivers. Staff have no incentive to upsell. Sustainability and packaging requirements are pushed down the supply chain with a force no single retailer commands, which is why lightweight glass and bag-in-box penetrated these markets first.

The costs are convenience and price competition: restricted hours and outlets, no promotions, slower assortment turnover. Two things are routinely conflated here and should be separated: high shelf prices in these markets are overwhelmingly a function of excise duty, which is the health lever, not of the monopoly structure. The privatisation experiments — Alberta in the 1990s and Washington State in 2012 — are the evidence to reason from: outlet numbers and convenience rose, and consumer prices did not reliably fall.

## To what extent

Consumers benefit most, on range, equity and transparency; producers benefit selectively, gaining a meritocratic route to a national listing but on monopsony terms and with no brand to show for it; retailers benefit least, because the structure substantially replaces them. All of it is contingent on a non-commercial mandate. Where a monopoly is run for revenue rather than harm reduction, as in several US control states, the range narrows, tender discipline weakens and the benefits to all three groups largely evaporate. That is the strongest evidence that it is the mandate, not the monopoly, doing the work.
