## Terms, and how I read the question

By **small-scale** I mean a winery under about 10,000 cases a year: one where lots are handled individually and by hand, where the winemaker tastes every vessel weekly, and where capital equipment — optical sorters, reverse osmosis, rotofermenters — cannot be amortised. Scale says nothing about quality: plenty of small wineries make ordinary wine and several of the world's benchmark reds come from estates producing far more than this. **High quality** I define as a wine that is balanced and concentrated without heaviness, complex, capable of improving in bottle, and legible as coming from its place — provenance is part of the definition, not a marketing add-on, and price is a consequence of quality rather than evidence of it.

Two readings I reject. This is not a question about whether to minimise intervention: every choice in a cellar is an intervention, and deciding not to destem is as active a decision as adding tannin. Nor is it about hygiene and fault-fixing; I take the wine as healthy and the question as being about which choices optimise quality. So the answer is not *how much* to intervene but *which* interventions the quality objective justifies. My position, argued below, is that interventions which preserve optionality and work with the fruit are strongly desirable, while those that overwrite site character are not — because at this quality level site difference is the thing being sold.

## Fruit selection and sorting

The most desirable intervention of all, and the one small scale makes uniquely available. Hand-sorting on a table at 3,000 cases costs a few hours; at three million it is impossible, which is why large producers buy optical sorters instead. Removing raisined and mouldy berries lifts quality more than any subsequent decision, because no cellar technique removes laccase-oxidised or dehydrated character afterwards. I would spend money here before anywhere else.

## Pre-fermentation choices

Cold soak, whole-bunch inclusion and saignée pull in different directions and cannot all be right. Cold soak extracts anthocyanin and primary aroma before alcohol appears, which suits a variety short of colour; whole bunch adds stem tannin, aromatic lift and cooler, slower fermentation but raises pH and risks greenness from unlignified stems. Compare the traditionalist Barolo school of long submerged-cap macerations in large Slavonian botti against the modernist school of short rotofermenter macerations and French barriques: both make serious wine, and the difference is not one of competence but of what each is willing to trade — perfume and tension against approachability and colour. Whole bunch is well-founded in Côte-Rôtie, where whole-bunch inclusion and co-fermentation with a little Viognier are long-standing, but transplanted to a warm site with short vegetative cycles it produces stalky wine. Saignée I regard as the least desirable of the three: it concentrates everything indiscriminately, including alcohol and any unripe phenolics, and is usually a correction for a viticultural failure.

## Yeast

Ambient fermentation is not a virtue in itself, and the argument that it expresses site is weaker than usually claimed — a resident cellar population is a winery signature at least as much as a vineyard one. Its real benefit is a slower, more complex fermentation from sequential non-Saccharomyces activity; its real cost is risk of sluggishness and VA. A cultured strain gives reliability and a predictable profile but narrows the aromatic range. At small scale I would ferment ambient on sound fruit, with a cultured strain held ready — the intervention being the contingency, not the default.

## Fermentation and cap management

This is where extraction is decided, and gentleness is worth more than power. Punch-downs are more selective for skin than seed phenolics; pump-overs mix and oxygenate efficiently; rack-and-return and rotofermenters extract hard and fast and, in my view, belong to a volume model rather than this one. Temperature is the greater lever: a peak in the high twenties builds structure, cooler preserves aroma, and small vessels allow the winemaker to run several regimes and blend. Extended post-fermentation maceration trades present colour for polymeric pigment and softer tannin, which is desirable for a wine intended to age and pointless for one that is not.

## Additions — tannin and acid

Acidification is desirable when it restores balance in a warm vintage and undesirable when it becomes a routine substitute for picking earlier, because the wine then tastes of two separate things. Enological tannin at crush is useful and under-used in the sense that it stabilises pigment; added late for grip, it is cosmetic and shows as a hard finish. The test I apply is whether the addition assists what the fruit already contains or supplies what it lacks.

## Oak and élevage

The intervention most often overdone. New oak supplies slow oxygen ingress and ellagitannins that help polymerise pigment — genuinely useful — but at high percentages it substitutes a purchased flavour for a grown one, and it is expensive at small scale. I would rather buy larger-format and older barrels and spend the difference on sorting. Fining and filtration protect the buyer against haze and refermentation; for a high-quality red bottled with reasonable free SO2 and sound hygiene, I would generally forgo both, accepting sediment as the cost.

## Conclusion

Interventions are desirable in direct proportion to how far they serve the fruit rather than replace it. Sorting, thoughtful extraction, temperature control and oxygen management are close to obligatory at this level. Saignée, heavy new oak, late tannin and habitual acidification are not, because each one narrows the very distinctiveness the wine is priced on.
