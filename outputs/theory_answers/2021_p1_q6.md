## Defining terroir, and stating my position

Terroir is the whole natural environment of a delimited site — geology and soil, topography of slope, aspect and altitude, mesoclimate, and soil biology — as it expresses itself through a particular plant material. The modern working definition is broader than the physical one: the OIV's 2010 definition treats terroir as the interaction between a physical environment and applied viticultural and oenological practices. That inclusion of human practice is what makes the question answerable at all for a category the trade rightly calls manufactured.

My position is that there is no single ideal terroir for sparkling wine, because the category contains styles with incompatible site requirements; but there is an ideal terroir for each style, and there is one generalisation that holds across all of them. The ideal site for sparkling wine is a marginal one — a site too cool to ripen fruit reliably for still wine — because the style's requirements turn that viticultural deficiency into an asset. For the autolytic traditional-method style specifically, the Champagne combination is the closest thing to an ideal that exists, though a large part of what makes it so is human and legal rather than physical.

## The physical brief, and why it differs by style

**Autolytic traditional method.** The base wine must be high in acid, low in potential alcohol, restrained in primary aroma so that autolysis has room to speak, and clean enough for whole-bunch pressing. That brief points to a cool climate at the edge of ripening — Champagne's northern latitude, Tasmania, the English chalk downs, and altitude as a substitute for latitude in Trentodoc, Limoux and the higher Penedes sites for Cava. It points to free-draining soils with a reserve of water: Champagne's chalk drains and yet stores moisture and reflects light, the Kimmeridgian marls of the Aube and Chablis do similar work, and limestone recurs from Franciacorta to Robertson. Lake or maritime moderation lengthens the ripening period without raising peak heat, which is the mechanism behind Franciacorta, Marlborough, Anderson Valley and the cooler South African wards used for Cap Classique. Sites within a region are not equivalent either: the Cote des Blancs, the Montagne de Reims and the Aube each contribute something different, which is an argument that no single terroir suffices even inside the ideal region.

**Aromatic tank method.** Prosecco reverses the brief. Glera is a relatively neutral, moderate-acid variety whose value lies in delicate floral and pear aromatics that a long autolytic ageing would destroy, so the site must deliver ripeness and aromatic retention rather than searing acidity. The steep hills of Conegliano and Valdobbiadene give the aromatic definition; the flat Treviso plain gives the volume. Asti has the same logic in stronger form: Moscato Bianco on the Piedmont hills, picked to preserve terpenes, fermented once and arrested. A site ideal for Champagne would produce poor Asti.

**Ancestral method.** Pet-Nat requires healthy fruit and a reliable residual-sugar and yeast population at bottling, and comparatively little else; its character is dominated by process decisions. It is the hardest style for which to argue an ideal terroir, and the honest observation is that it is made successfully almost everywhere.

## The manufactured element

Even within the traditional method, the largest determinants of the finished wine are human. Blending is the central technology, and it exists specifically to override site: multi-village, multi-variety assemblage smooths parcel differences, and the reserve-wine system is designed to erase vintage variation altogether — a deliberate suppression of what terroir would otherwise express. Press fractionation discards the phenolic later pressings; the decision on malolactic conversion determines the acid profile as decisively as the site does; time on lees creates the autolytic character that most consumers identify as the style; and dosage adjusts the final balance after everything else. A house style is engineered, and reproduced from fruit that varies enormously year to year.

The delimitations are human too. Champagne's boundary was fixed by a legal settlement in the 1920s after the grower riots, and Prosecco was reorganised in 2009, when the grape was renamed Glera and the denomination extended across a large area of the Veneto and Friuli — an explicit demonstration that the boundary of an "ideal" terroir can be redrawn by administrative decision. Conversely, the human element also creates terroir claims: the hills of Conegliano and Valdobbiadene were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2019 on the strength of their cultural landscape, and the grower movement and single-parcel cuvees in Champagne are an attempt to reassert site against the blending house.

## Weighing it

The physical case for a single ideal is genuinely strong for one style. Chalk, marginal cool climate, gentle slopes and a long, unforced ripening period do produce base wine of a quality that is very hard to match, and new regions consciously seek chalk or limestone at cool latitudes. But three objections defeat the claim of a universal ideal. Different sparkling styles want opposite things from a site. The processing decisions contribute more of the finished character than the site does, which is precisely what "manufactured" means. And the leading regions' boundaries are legal artefacts as much as geological ones.

## Conclusion

There is no ideal terroir for sparkling wine as a category. There is an ideal site brief for each style, and one principle common to all: choose a site whose climate is marginal for still wine, because low sugar and high acid are the style's assets rather than its faults. For traditional-method wine, Champagne remains the benchmark — but I would attribute that as much to three centuries of accumulated human technique and to the legal boundary drawn around it as to the chalk, which is exactly why Tasmania, England and Trentodoc can now approach it.
