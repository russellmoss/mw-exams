## Defining the target

Final sugar means residual sugar: the fermentable and unfermentable sugars left in the finished wine. Even a bone-dry wine holds roughly 1-2 g/L of unfermentable pentoses, so zero is not on the table. EU labelling defines dry as up to 4 g/L, or up to 9 g/L where total acidity is no more than 2 g/L below the sugar; then medium dry to 12 g/L, medium to 45 g/L and sweet above 45 g/L. Sparkling wine has its own ladder: brut nature under 3 g/L, extra brut under 6, brut under 12, extra dry 12-17, sec 17-32, demi-sec 32-50 and doux above 50. Those numbers matter because they are what the winemaker is actually aiming at, and because perceived sweetness is not the same thing — a 9 g/L Riesling at pH 2.9 tastes drier than a 4 g/L Pinot Grigio.

This is a winery question. Vineyard sugar accumulation — late harvest, botrytis, passerillage, cane-cutting — sets the raw material, and I treat it only as the starting condition. I work through the cellar chronologically: what arrives, what the ferment does, how it can be stopped, and what can be blended in afterwards.

## Setting the starting sugar

Enrichment raises must sugar: sucrose where chaptalisation is allowed, rectified concentrated grape must (RCGM) where it is not, or water removal by vacuum evaporation, reverse osmosis or cryoextraction. Sugar can equally be reduced before fermentation — glucose oxidase, membrane systems coupling reverse osmosis to a sugar-stripping resin, or water addition where permitted, as in Australia for high-Baumé fruit. These fix the potential alcohol, and so determine how much sugar there is room to leave.

## Fermenting to dryness — the ordinary case

Most wine is dry because the yeast was allowed to finish, and that is an active achievement, not a default. It needs a strain with sufficient alcohol tolerance, correct rehydration, YAN of the order of 200 mg/L for a normal must and considerably more for a high-sugar one, staged nutrient additions, temperature held in range, and oxygen during the growth phase to build yeast sterols. Where any of these fail, the wine ends with sugar nobody chose, and restarting a stuck ferment with an acclimatised culture is far harder than avoiding one. High-sugar musts — Amarone, a Barossa red at 16% potential alcohol — are the difficult case, because osmotic stress and ethanol toxicity make dryness the accomplishment. Running the logic backwards is a crude sweetening tool: pick a slow, low-tolerance strain and sugar remains, but the endpoint is unpredictable.

## Stopping the fermentation

Every precise method here removes or immobilises yeast at a chosen sugar level.

- **Chilling** to around 0-4C settles the yeast and halts activity — Mosel Kabinett, Loire demi-sec. It is reversible on warming, so it must be locked in by something else.
- **Sulphur dioxide** at mutage dose is the traditional sweet-wine tool. In Sauternes the ferment usually stalls unaided once alcohol and botrytis metabolites overwhelm the yeast; SO2 then fixes the result rather than causing it.
- **Physical removal** — centrifugation, cross-flow, then sterile filtration. Asti is the clearest case: one tank fermentation under pressure, chilled and filtered at around 7% abv with the sugar still in place. Prosecco DOC labelled Extra Dry sits at 12-17 g/L by the same route.
- **Fortification.** Spirit to about 15% abv upwards kills the yeast, and the timing sets the sugar. Port fortified at roughly 6-8% abv retains something like 100 g/L; a Vin Doux Naturel such as Muscat de Beaumes-de-Venise is arrested earlier and sweeter; Pineau des Charentes fortifies juice that never fermented at all.
- **Pressure.** Accumulated CO2 inhibits yeast, which contributes in tank-method sweet sparkling alongside the chilling.

## Adding sugar back

Blending after fermentation is the most controllable option, because the base wine is finished and the addition is dosed against an analysis rather than guessed at mid-ferment. Süssreserve — sterile unfermented juice — is used in Germany and increasingly in England, and brings acidity and grape aroma with it. RCGM does the same arithmetic without flavour, which is why it dominates at commercial scale. In sparkling wine the liqueur d'expédition at disgorgement sets the whole style, and is the reason one base wine can be sold as brut and as demi-sec. Sherry is the instructive fortified case: it ferments bone dry without exception, so every sweet sherry is a blend, sweetened with Pedro Ximénez or Moscatel wine, or with RCGM for commercial cream styles.

## Holding the sugar where you put it

Above roughly 4 g/L the wine is refermentable, so sugar control is only as good as the stabilisation behind it. Sterile filtration at 0.45 micron, molecular SO2 held on target, sorbic acid, capped at 200 mg/L in the EU and useless without SO2 because lactic bacteria turn it into geranium taint, or DMDC at 200 mg/L. Dealcoholised wine is the extreme: stripping the alcohol leaves a sweet liquid with no defence at all, hence back-sweetening followed by aseptic bottling.

## Conclusion

The options divide by how precisely they hit a number. Fermenting dry and blending back are the two accurate methods, and between them account for most of the world's wine; arresting a live fermentation is the imprecise one, tolerated where the arrest itself creates the style, as in Port and Asti. Choosing between them is a choice about how much control the style can afford to give up.
