## Three different words wearing one coat

The proposition contains two separate charges, and they need separating before either can be judged. Overuse is a claim about frequency and about the word being applied where nothing specific is meant. Misunderstanding is a claim about meaning — that the people using it, and the people reading it, do not have the same referent, and that the most common explanation given for it is untrue. I agree with both charges, and I nevertheless think the word should be retained. That combination is the position I defend.

The confusion begins with the fact that "minerality" is used for at least three unrelated things. It is used geologically, to mean rock minerals — the crystalline inorganic compounds of the bedrock, chalk, slate, granite, flint. It is used chemically, to mean nutrient minerals, the soluble ions a vine actually takes up: potassium, calcium, magnesium, sodium, chloride, sulphate. And it is used sensorially, as a descriptor for aromas and flavours redolent of wet stone, gunflint, chalk dust, iodine or salt, and increasingly for a textural sensation rather than an aroma at all. It is then routinely conflated with a fourth idea, terroir, so that calling a wine mineral becomes a way of asserting it tastes of somewhere without having to say how.

Any usable definition has to choose. Mine is that minerality is a sensory term only, and that the geological sense is a metaphor which the industry has mistaken for a mechanism.

## The scientific case against the word

The strongest objection is straightforward: the causal story almost everyone tells is wrong. Vines do not absorb rock. Roots take up ions in soil solution, and the geologist Alex Maltman has argued that the mineral ions actually present in wine occur at concentrations far below human detection thresholds — and that in any case they are colourless, largely non-volatile and taste of nothing at those levels. There is no mechanism by which Kimmeridgian marl transmits the flavour of Kimmeridgian marl.

The second objection is sensory. Sensory studies in Chablis and the Loire have found little consensus among trained tasters as to which wines are mineral or what the term denotes, and researchers have struggled to link the descriptor to any consistent chemical marker. A descriptor that expert panels cannot apply reproducibly is, by the standards of tasting vocabulary, not doing its job.

The third is historical, and it is the most telling. The word barely appears in English wine writing before the 1980s and becomes common only in the 1990s and 2000s. A quality supposedly intrinsic to great terroir went unnamed for centuries by the people who worked those terroirs. That timing coincides with the rise of a counter-movement to ripe, oaked, high-alcohol wine, which suggests the word was recruited to mean "the opposite of that" — a stylistic allegiance dressed as a geological observation.

## The cultural case for it

Against all of that: the perception is real. Experienced tasters do reach for the same word in front of Chablis, Santorini Assyrtiko, Mosel Riesling from slate, Muscadet on gneiss and Manzanilla from Sanlúcar — and they reach for it without prompting and across languages. Something is being perceived, even if the folk explanation of it is false.

Wine language is largely metaphorical and always has been. Nobody objects that a wine has no blackcurrants in it, or that "silky" describes no measurable property of a liquid. The demand that "mineral" be literally true holds it to a standard we impose on no other descriptor. Tasting notes are communication, not analysis, and a word that reliably conveys a cluster of sensations to an experienced audience earns its place even when the underlying cause is unresolved.

## Where the perception plausibly comes from

This is where most discussion of the subject stops short, and where the technical argument matters.

*In the vineyard*, the candidates are indirect but real. Poor, free-draining, low-vigour soils restrain yield and vegetative growth and delay sugar accumulation, giving wines picked at lower ripeness with high acidity and low fruit intensity — the sensory space in which minerality is almost always reported. Cool sites and high altitude do the same. Coastal and island vineyards can show measurably higher sodium and chloride, and there salinity may genuinely be a soil-derived contribution. Soil chemistry also acts on pH: potassium uptake raises must pH and softens a wine, so low-potassium sites make the taut, low-pH wines that get called mineral.

*In the winery*, the candidates are stronger still. Succinic acid, a normal fermentation product, is described as salty-bitter and varies with yeast strain and fermentation conditions. Benzenemethanethiol, a thiol associated with struck-flint and smoky characters, is linked to lees contact and low-oxygen handling — a reductive winemaking signature, in other words, produced by decisions made in a cellar and not by the rock beneath the vine. Lees ageing also contributes mannoproteins and a chalky textural impression.

*In maturation*, closure and oxygen management determine whether those reductive characters persist or dissipate. A wine bottled under a low-oxygen-transmission closure will hold flinty notes for years that would blow off under a more permeable one.

The honest summary is that minerality is most likely a composite: high acidity, low pH, restrained fruit, some salinity, some reductive sulphur chemistry, and a textural component from lees. Most of that is vineyard-conditioned but cellar-mediated, which is precisely why the geological story is both intuitive and wrong.

## Who uses it, and what it does for them

For the trade and the media the word is genuinely useful shorthand among peers who share a reference set. For consumers it is close to meaningless: research consistently shows most buyers navigate by grape, region, brand and price, and a term that expert panels cannot apply consistently will not help someone choosing in a supermarket aisle.

It is nevertheless commercially valuable, and any balanced answer has to concede this. "Mineral" signals seriousness, restraint, ageworthiness and site-specificity, and it lifts a wine's perceived standing and price. That is exactly why it is overused: it is a free claim to distinction, unfalsifiable, and it costs nothing to write on a website, a shelf-talker or a by-the-glass list. Overuse is not carelessness; it is incentive.

## Overused and misunderstood are two charges

Overused: yes, demonstrably, because the word carries commercial value with no evidential burden. Misunderstood: yes, but not by everyone equally. Experienced tasters use it with a fairly stable sensory referent and a false causal explanation; consumers have neither.

## What the word should mean, and how it should be used

I would keep it and discipline it. Among professionals, "mineral" should be treated as a sensory descriptor only, and never used alone — it should be qualified by what is actually perceived: flinty, chalky, saline, iodine, wet stone. It should never appear as an explanation, and the phrase "mineral because of the limestone" should be retired from trade communication and education entirely, because it teaches a mechanism that does not exist.

For consumers it should not be used at all. On labels, wine lists and websites, the plain sensory words underneath it — saline, smoky, taut, chalky — are more informative, more honest, and communicate the same wine to someone who does not already know what we mean.
