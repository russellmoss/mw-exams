## Scope, data and structure

I take "the past three years" to mean the 2015, 2016 and 2017 harvests and the trading years to spring 2018. My volume figures come from the OIV's annual production and consumption balance, which is published and replicable; my value and channel figures from IWSR and Nielsen. I identify three changes on the supply side and three on the demand side, and then give the second half of this answer to what they imply, because the implications are where the commercial argument actually lies.

## Supply: three changes

**The end of structural surplus.** 2017 was the pivot. April frost across France, drought in Italy and Spain, and a world crop the OIV put at roughly 250 million hectolitres, the lowest in over fifty years and around 8% below 2016. It did not stand alone: El Nino rains cut the 2016 Argentine and Chilean crops, and South Africa entered a Western Cape drought severe enough to shrink the 2018 harvest materially. The consequence is that the European surplus which set the terms of trade for a generation - the "wine lake" - has effectively gone.

**Supply is being re-regulated and rebuilt.** The EU replaced planting rights with the vineyard authorisation scheme on 1 January 2016, permitting new plantings of up to 1% of national vineyard area a year. At the same time China now has the second largest vineyard area in the world, and the United States, New Zealand and Argentina have continued planting into premium demand.

**Sourcing has become mobile.** Bulk wine now trades as a reported commodity, and blenders and own-label bottlers switch country of origin vintage to vintage. That flexibility is what absorbed the shortfalls without empty shelves.

## Demand: three changes

**Flat volume, rising value.** Global consumption has been broadly flat at around 240-250 million hectolitres for a decade while spend has risen. Premiumisation is the single most important demand change of the period: mature-market consumers are drinking less and paying more per bottle.

**The centre of gravity has moved.** The United States is the largest wine market by value, while France, Italy and Spain continue a long per-capita decline. Growth has come from the US, China and a cluster of smaller Asian markets, and Australia's export mix swung decisively towards China as the Australia-China free trade agreement phased its wine tariff to zero.

**Category and channel switching.** Sparkling wine, led by Prosecco, has taken share from still wine; Provence-style rosé has grown across markets; direct-to-consumer and online have grown faster than grocery. In the UK, sterling fell by roughly 15% against the euro after the June 2016 referendum, raising the landed cost of every imported wine.

## Implications: the "so what"

**Entry-level wine is the casualty, and this matters more than the headline figures.** Bulk prices in Spain and southern France rose steeply off their 2013-14 lows at exactly the moment sterling weakened. In a high-duty market the wine in a cheap bottle is a small fraction of the shelf price, so a doubling of bulk cost cannot be absorbed. Retailers must either reformulate to a cheaper origin, shrink the specification, or move the price point up. All three have happened, and the entry price ladder in the UK has stepped up accordingly.

**Producers have regained pricing power for the first time in a generation - but only branded ones.** Scarcity transfers margin to whoever the customer asks for by name. Growers selling anonymous bulk got a good two years; brand owners got a durable repricing.

**Capital is being redirected up the price ladder.** Premiumisation plus tight supply justifies planting, vineyard acquisition and consolidation at the premium end, and makes any business whose only advantage is volume structurally fragile. This is why the recent transactions have concentrated on premium brands rather than capacity.

**Trade policy and currency are now the largest single variables in a producer's P&L.** Australia's China concentration, the UK's import exposure and tariff-driven route-to-market change have all shown that a business can be undone by a decision taken in a capital city rather than by anything in the vineyard. Single-market dependence is the risk I would most want managed.

**For the consumer, and therefore for the industry's future, choice at the bottom has narrowed.** That is the uncomfortable implication: premiumisation is celebrated by producers precisely because it is profitable, but a rising entry price makes recruiting the next generation of wine drinkers harder, and beer, spirits and hard seltzer are not facing the same input squeeze.

## Conclusion

The most important change is the coincidence of two things: the disappearance of structural oversupply and a demand curve that is flat in volume but rising in value. Together they have ended a thirty-year buyer's market. The implication I would put first is not the price of a case of bulk Airen but the strategic one - the industry has spent three years enjoying higher prices while quietly making its own entry-level recruitment problem worse.
