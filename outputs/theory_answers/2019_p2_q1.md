## Oxygen is a tool and a threat

Oxygen is not a fault agent to be excluded. Managed, it polymerises tannin and stabilises colour, builds complexity, resolves reduction, drives flor and the whole oxidative family, and prevents stuck fermentations. Unmanaged, it browns phenolics, strips thiols and terpenes, produces acetaldehyde and ethyl acetate, and feeds *Acetobacter* and *Brettanomyces*. Management therefore means deciding, at each stage, whether oxygen is wanted, in what quantity, and then delivering or excluding exactly that.

The winemaker's levers are the same throughout - temperature, sulphur dioxide, inert gas, vessel choice, movement, and measurement - and one saturation of must or wine is roughly 8 mg/l at 20C, rising further at cooler cellar temperatures (toward 14 mg/l near 0C), which is why cumulative small pickups matter more than any single event. I take the process in order and note where styles diverge.

## Harvest and reception

Impact: damaged fruit oxidises before it reaches the press, and rot-affected fruit carries laccase, which oxidises phenolics even under SO2.

Management: pick at night or in the cool, keep loads shallow, avoid crushing in the bin, and move fruit quickly. Sulphur dioxide at the crusher inhibits tyrosinase; where laccase is present, heat or fining is needed instead because SO2 will not stop it. Machine-harvested aromatic whites are dosed with SO2 and sometimes ascorbic acid in the bin, and dry ice or CO2 blanketing displaces air in the hopper. The opposite decision is legitimate too: Champagne and traditional-method producers accept whole-bunch handling with minimal SO2 and let must phenolics oxidise and drop out.

## Pressing and juice handling

Impact: this is the stage of greatest exposure and the one where style diverges most sharply.

Management for aromatic whites — Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, Verdejo, Albariño — is **hyper-reductive**: inert the press with nitrogen or CO2, press whole bunches gently, keep juice cold, add SO2 and ascorbic acid early, and settle under a gas blanket, so that volatile thiols and terpenes survive. Management for barrel-fermented Chardonnay and for many Rhône whites is the reverse: **hyperoxidation**, deliberately saturating the must with oxygen with little or no SO2, so that oxidisable phenolics polymerise and precipitate with the lees and the finished wine is more, not less, stable to oxygen later. Both are oxygen management; they simply take opposite positions on the same variable. Reds are pressed without concern, since their tannin buffers oxidation.

Flotation, used for high-volume whites, uses gas to clarify and can be run on nitrogen where oxidation is a risk.

## Fermentation

Impact: yeast consume oxygen fast, so the ferment itself protects the must; but yeast need a small amount for sterol synthesis, and its absence causes sluggish ferments and hydrogen sulphide.

Management: deliver oxygen deliberately and early. An aeration at about one-third sugar depletion — by pumping over with splashing, a rack-and-return, or a measured oxygen dose — builds membrane sterols and reduces the risk of a stuck ferment and reductive off-odours. In reds, pump-overs, punch-downs, délestage and *pigeage* combine extraction with oxygen delivery, and open-top fermenters supply more than closed tanks. In aromatic whites the winemaker minimises this, fermenting cold in closed stainless steel with a CO2 blanket from the ferment itself. Barrel fermentation gives whites a slow, low dose that is one reason barrel-fermented Chardonnay resists later oxidation better than tank-fermented.

## Post-fermentation and malolactic

Impact: this is the most vulnerable moment in the wine's life — fermentation CO2 has gone, SO2 is still absent because MLF is pending, and yeast are no longer scavenging.

Management: keep vessels topped, blanket headspace with nitrogen or argon, avoid unnecessary rackings, and complete MLF promptly, then sulphur immediately. Where MLF is not wanted — most aromatic whites and most rosé — sulphur, chill and filter early, since a wine held unsulphured is exposed on both fronts at once.

## Maturation

Impact: slow controlled ingress builds structure and complexity; excess dries fruit and raises acetaldehyde and VA.

Management is largely a vessel decision, since each format has a characteristic oxygen transfer rate: stainless steel is effectively closed; concrete and large old foudres transfer very little; a 225-litre barrel transfers most, and a new one more than an old one. Racking, topping frequency and lees management then modulate it — gross and fine lees consume oxygen and are the winemaker's built-in reductant, so *sur lie* ageing with bâtonnage is an oxygen-management technique as much as a textural one. **Micro-oxygenation** delivers a metered dose in tank, achieving barrel-like tannin polymerisation in Malbec, Tannat and high-volume reds without barrel cost. And a whole family of styles is built on the opposite decision: Sherry under flor consumes oxygen biologically while oloroso and Madeira are matured deliberately oxidatively, vin jaune under voile, tawny Port in small pipes, and Rioja Gran Reserva through long barrel ageing. Here oxygen is the style.

## Preparation and bottling

Impact: fining, filtration, cold stabilisation and every pump and transfer pick up oxygen; and total package oxygen at bottling determines shelf life.

Management: use inert-gas-purged lines and tanks, minimise transfers, use gravity or gentle pumps, sparge with nitrogen, purge bottles and headspace, and measure total package oxygen rather than assume it. Free SO2 is set to the wine's pH — a Riesling at low pH needs far less than a high-pH red for the same molecular level. Closure choice then determines the ingress rate for the rest of the wine's life: screwcap liners are specified by oxygen transmission, and a reductive-prone Riesling and an under-extracted red want different ones. Sparkling wine adds disgorging, where oxygen pickup and dosage SO2 must both be controlled.

## Conclusion

Managing oxygen is a sequence of deliberate decisions, not an avoidance policy: exclude it where aroma is the asset, supply it where structure, ferment health or oxidative character is the asset, and above all measure it — dissolved oxygen at transfers, total package oxygen at bottling — because the impacts that ruin wine are cumulative and invisible.
