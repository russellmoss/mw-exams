## Scope

Pruning timing has two halves and the question covers both: **dormant pruning**, meaning where in the winter window the canes and spurs are cut, and **green pruning**, meaning the timing of disbudding, desuckering, shoot thinning, topping, leaf removal and crop thinning during the season. I deal with method only where the method itself dictates the timing, and I treat frost as a driver of pruning date rather than as a frost-management problem in its own right. The organising point is that pruning date is one of the few levers that shifts the vine's phenological calendar, so it is chosen against whatever the dominant local risk happens to be — and that risk differs completely from one region to another.

## Dormant pruning: bud break, and the risks it is timed against

The physiological lever is apical dominance. A vine left long and pruned late holds its basal buds back, so late pruning can delay bud break by roughly one to two weeks. Where spring frost is the dominant risk this is the cheapest tool available, which is why growers in Chablis, Champagne and the frost-exposed valley floors of Mendoza and Marlborough prune last, or double-prune — leaving long canes through winter and making the final cut once the frost window has passed. It is also why the block sequence follows variety phenology: early-budding Chardonnay, Merlot and Pinot Noir are left until last, while later-budding Cabernet Sauvignon, Riesling and Petit Verdot are pruned first.

**Winter cold injury** dominates in extreme continental regions and works the opposite way. In Ontario, the Finger Lakes, Michigan and inland China, growers deliberately delay until the coldest period has passed so that bud mortality can be assessed and compensated for by retaining extra buds — pruning early to a fixed bud number risks pruning to a crop that has already been killed. Where vines are buried under soil for winter in Ningxia, the calendar is set by the burial and unearthing operations rather than by the vine at all.

**Rainfall** sets the timing for a different reason: trunk disease. Eutypa and Botryosphaeria spores are released after rain and infect fresh pruning wounds, so the rule is to prune into a dry window with settled weather ahead, not before a forecast front. Late pruning helps here too, because wounds made close to sap flow occlude faster. It also drives block sequence within the estate: prune the young, clean blocks first and the old, symptomatic blocks last, so infected debris is not carried into healthy vineyards.

**Labour and logistics** are the constraint that overrides all of the above at scale. Pruning is the largest single labour operation of the year, and no large estate can complete a thousand hectares in the two-week window that is physiologically ideal. The window is therefore set by crew size, contractor availability and cost — and this is the one place method matters, because mechanical pre-pruning in midwinter followed by a hand pass compresses the labour requirement enough to bring the finishing cuts into the preferred period.

## Regions where the dormant calendar does not apply

It is a mistake to assume bud break occurs on the same schedule everywhere. In tropical and subtropical regions with insufficient winter chilling — the Sao Francisco Valley in Brazil, parts of India and Thailand — the vine has no true dormancy, and the pruning date **is** the start of the season. Growers there take two crops a year in Brazil and time pruning against the dry window and the market, not against frost. In warm temperate regions, deliberately late pruning after bud burst is being used as a heat-adaptation tool, pushing flowering and ripening into cooler autumn conditions.

## Green pruning: the timing that shapes the crop

Fewer candidates work this half, and it is where the quality decisions sit. **Desuckering and disbudding** should be done while shoots are herbaceous — early, when they detach cleanly and cheaply; delay it and the operation wounds a lignified trunk and creates an infection court. **Shoot thinning** at around ten to twenty centimetres allows the retained shoots to compensate; done later, the vine has already spent reserves on shoots that will be discarded. **Topping and hedging** timed too early stimulates lateral regrowth, thickening the canopy, raising humidity and increasing mildew and botrytis pressure; done after veraison, regrowth is limited but sugar loading may be constrained by the leaf area removed.

**Leaf removal timing** is the clearest link to style and disease. Removal before flowering reduces fruit set and produces looser, less compact bunches that resist botrytis, and it also raises phenolic and aromatic development; removal after veraison in a hot region risks sunburn and stripped aromatics, so it is confined to the morning side. **Crop thinning** at veraison is more accurate, because colour change identifies laggard bunches, but earlier thinning is more effective at driving compensation into the remaining fruit — earlier for concentration, later for evenness.

## Conclusion

Dormant pruning timing is a risk-management decision, and which risk dominates changes by region: cold injury in extreme continental climates, spring frost in marginal temperate ones, spore release wherever winters are wet, and pure labour logistics wherever the holding is large. Green pruning timing is a quality decision, traded against disease pressure and the vine's capacity to compensate.
