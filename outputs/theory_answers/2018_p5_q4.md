## What elitism is, and what it is not

Elitism is the belief that a select group - defined by knowledge, taste, wealth or birth - should hold privileged authority or access, together with the gatekeeping that belief produces. It is a claim about *who is entitled to judge and to obtain*.

It is not inequality. Inequality is an unequal distribution of resources and is an outcome; elitism is an attitude and a practice. Fine wine being expensive is inequality of purchasing power, not elitism. Nor is it discrimination by protected characteristic: excluding women from a tasting circle is sexism, and the wine trade has that problem too, but it is a different problem with different causes and remedies. Conflating the three produces an essay that never touches the question.

Two senses of the word matter here, and they behave differently:

- **Stratification** - the existence of a quality and price hierarchy. Every category of consumer good has one.
- **Gatekeeping** - the control of judgement, vocabulary and access by a credentialed group, so that participation requires the permission of insiders. This is elitism proper, and this is where the question lives.

My verdict, argued below: elitism is genuinely *inherent* to wine, for structural reasons that cannot be reformed away. It is *not* inherently a problem. It is a problem in exactly one place - at the point of recruitment - and a positive almost everywhere else.

## Where the hierarchies came from

Wine's hierarchies are old, formal and written down, which is unusual. The 1855 Medoc classification was drawn up by Bordeaux brokers from the prevailing market prices for the Paris Exposition Universelle - a ranking of reputation, made by the trade, that has proved almost immovable for well over a century. Burgundy's climat hierarchy was codified into law with the AOC system in the 1930s, and the German wine law of 1971 replaced a village-and-site hierarchy with one based on must weight. Each of these took a market judgement and gave it the force of law.

But the historical picture is more interesting than "wine was always elite", and this is where a comparison with present-day attitudes earns its marks. In producing countries wine was for centuries the ordinary drink of ordinary people - a daily agricultural staple, drunk with meals, of no social significance whatever. Its association with exclusivity is largely a phenomenon of *export* markets, where wine arrives without a food culture attached, priced by transport and duty, and is adopted first by the wealthy. Britain's relationship with claret and port is the model. So elitism in wine is not an ancient inheritance so much as a by-product of trade: the further wine travels from where it is grown, the more elite it becomes.

## What has changed

The present-day picture cuts both ways.

Elitism has been reinforced in the fine-wine tier. Allocation lists, en primeur, ballots, the 100-point score, investment funds and auction records have professionalised exclusivity, and the growth of Asian demand has pushed the top of the market further from ordinary consumers than at any point in living memory. The credentialed layer has also thickened: WSET, the Master of Wine and the Master Sommelier have made expertise formal and visible in a way it was not fifty years ago.

Elitism has simultaneously collapsed everywhere else. Discounters sell competent wine at commodity prices; supermarkets made wine a grocery item; online retail removed the intimidating merchant; and Vivino and CellarTracker aggregate consumer ratings, which transfers authority from the critic to the crowd. Natural wine built a following substantially on anti-establishment positioning. The result is a market that is more polarised than it was, not uniformly more elitist: the base has democratised faster than the apex has opened.

## Is wine worse than other categories?

This is the decisive test, and the answer is largely no.

Every category with heterogeneous quality and status value builds the same structures. Hermes allocates Birkin bags rather than selling them freely, and Rolex and Patek Philippe operate waiting lists more restrictive than any Burgundy domaine's. Contemporary art galleries place works with approved collectors and refuse others outright. Sneaker brands run ballots. Specialty coffee has adopted wine's own language of origin, varietal, altitude and process within twenty years. Whisky, cigars and classical music all have connoisseur hierarchies with their own vocabularies and their own bores. Gatekeeping is not a wine pathology; it is what happens to any product where quality is hard to verify and status is available.

Wine does have one genuine difference, and it is worth isolating precisely. The consumer cannot assess the product before buying it, cannot easily assess it afterwards either, and the number of distinguishable products runs into the hundreds of thousands. That combination makes expertise *functionally necessary* - somebody must select, and the buyer must trust them. A watch is a watch when you look at it; a bottle is opaque. It is this necessity, not snobbery, that generates wine's expert class, and it is why the class cannot simply be abolished.

## Who suffers, who benefits, and where it is actually a positive

The people harmed are new and casual consumers, who are deterred at the point of entry by vocabulary, ritual and the risk of ordering wrongly. That deterrence is measurable in the industry's flat recruitment. Harmed too are producers outside a recognised hierarchy - a good grower in an unclassified region carries a price ceiling imposed by geography - and, historically, consumers in emerging markets sold classification rather than quality.

The beneficiaries are the owners of classified land, the fine-wine trade, established critics and educators, collectors whose holdings appreciate, and sommeliers whose expertise is monetisable.

And there is a real positive case that should not be conceded away. The hierarchy is what funds quality: without a price ceiling sustained by reputation, nobody would drop yields, sort by hand or age wine for a decade. Elitism in the sense of insisting that some wine is better than other wine is simply the maintenance of standards, and a category that abandoned it would be a commodity. In several markets - China's gift culture, the auction market, luxury hospitality - exclusivity is not a defect but the product itself.

## Verdict

Is elitism inherent? Yes. Wine's value is substantially reputational, reputation requires ranking, and the product's opacity forces consumers to rely on experts. You cannot remove hierarchy from wine without removing what makes fine wine possible. Anyone proposing to abolish it is proposing a different industry.

Is it a problem? Only partly, and only at one margin. Between the beginner and the bottle sits a language, a ritual and an implied judgement of the buyer's taste, and that costs the category consumers it will not recover. That is a real and self-inflicted problem, and it is the one worth fixing.

But it is fixable without touching the hierarchy, because the two are separable. Wine can keep grand cru and lose the interrogation at the restaurant table. What the industry must stop doing is treating its hierarchy as a consumer-facing education syllabus - insisting the newcomer learn the ranking before being allowed to enjoy the drink. Elitism is inherent in wine's structure; it becomes a problem only when the trade makes it a condition of entry. That is a choice, not an inevitability, and it is the trade's to unmake.
