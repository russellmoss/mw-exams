## Three senses of "objective"

The question turns on a word that is doing three different jobs, and separating them settles most of the argument.

**Objective as mind-independent.** A property the wine has whether or not anyone tastes it, like its alcohol or its pH. In this sense "better" cannot be objective, and no amount of analysis will make it so. "Better" is a value predicate; no measurable constituent of wine entails it. Anyone claiming objectivity in this sense loses.

**Objective as intersubjective.** A judgement is objective when it is not arbitrary — when it is answerable to reasons, made on stated criteria, and reliably converged upon by people qualified to make it. This is the sense in which we call a medical diagnosis or a chess annotation objective. Wine judgement can be objective in this sense.

**Objective as fault-freedom.** A wine can be defective on criteria the whole trade agrees on: cork taint, oxidation, volatile acidity above threshold, reduction, refermentation. This is the narrowest and firmest ground.

## "Better" at what

"Better" needs a second definition, because it hides three claims.

**Preference** — I like this more. Personal, incorrigible, and not what the question asks about.
**Quality** — this wine is better made, more complete, more rewarding of attention. A claim that invites agreement and can be defended with reasons.
**Value** — better for the money, or better for this purpose. Objective once the purpose is fixed, and trivially so.

Only the second is interesting, and it carries an implicit clause: better *within a shared purpose*. A vin jaune and a Napa Cabernet are not competing at anything. "Better" is comparative within a style, a use, or an intention, and the question is ill-formed without one.

My position: **yes, one wine can be objectively better than another, in the fault-based and intersubjective senses, and no in the mind-independent sense.** Further, the claim is strong when the gap is wide and collapses when the gap is narrow — which is the opposite of where the trade spends its confidence.

## Hume, and why the standard sits in the judge

David Hume's "Of The Standard Of Taste" of 1757 remains the central treatment. Hume starts from the sceptical position — sentiment is not representation, so no sentiment can be false — and then observes that in practice we do not behave as though all verdicts are equal: nobody, he says, would rank Ogilby with Milton. Something must explain that.

His answer is that the standard lies not in the object but in the properly qualified subject. A true judge has delicacy of taste (detects components others miss), practice, comparison across the range, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. The joint verdict of such judges is the standard of taste. Hume then concedes what makes him honest: even among true judges, irreducible variation persists from temperament and from the culture of the age.

Transpose that and you have the wine trade exactly. We do not claim that quality is in the liquid. We claim that trained, practised, comparison-rich tasters converge, and we institutionalise the claim — through examinations, panels, appellation approval tastings and blind competitions.

## BLIC, and the analogy with fine art

The trade's working criteria are balance, length, intensity and complexity, with typicity and ageing potential as riders. Their status is instructive: each is a *descriptive* property, checkable by anyone trained. Whether a finish persists for three seconds or thirty is a matter of fact. Whether the alcohol protrudes from the acid and fruit is a matter of fact. Complexity is roughly countable. What is conventional is the *link* between those properties and the verdict "better".

The fine-art parallel is exact and worth making. Nobody thinks a Rembrandt is better than a hotel-lobby print in the sense that "better" is a pigment. But no one treats the comparison as mere preference either, because there are criteria — draughtsmanship, control of light, compositional invention, difficulty overcome — that trained viewers apply with substantial agreement. The criteria are conventional and not arbitrary: they track what repays sustained attention. BLIC does the same work, and inherits the same status.

## What can actually be measured

Chemistry measures composition and faults: alcohol, titratable acidity, pH, residual sugar, volatile acidity, TCA, Brettanomyces markers. It identifies defects reliably and quality not at all. There is no analyte that scales with quality, and there will not be one, because quality is not a substance.

Sensory science measures something more useful — reliability. Trained panels discriminate and rank reproducibly on defined attributes. That is the empirical basis for the intersubjective claim, and it is real. Its limits are equally real, which brings me to the objections.

## The four strongest objections

**Unreliability.** Published studies of a large American state-fair competition found that individual judges frequently scored replicate pours of the same wine differently within a single flight, and inter-critic agreement on the same wine is often weak. This is the most damaging objection and it must be conceded: it refutes the objectivity of any individual verdict. It does not refute the aggregate claim, because noisy individual measurements can still yield a stable mean — which is precisely why competitions use panels and why a single critic's score should never be treated as a fact.

**Context effects.** Experiments in which the same wine is presented at different stated prices have found that the higher price raises both reported enjoyment and measured activity in reward-related regions of the brain. Judgement is contaminated by information. The trade's answer is blind tasting, which is not a philosophical solution but is a working one, and its existence shows the profession already accepts the objection.

**Cultural relativity.** Criteria are formed inside a tradition. A Jerez taster's standard for a fino and a Burgundian's for a village Chambolle are not commensurable, and the global adoption of one set of criteria reflects commercial power as much as insight.

**Circularity.** True judges are identified by their agreement with the standard they themselves set. Hume saw this. My answer is that the circle is real but not vicious: it is the same circle in every skilled practice, and it is broken pragmatically, because training demonstrably changes what a taster can detect, and that change is testable independently of any verdict.

## Where the claim holds and where it breaks

**Faults.** Near-total agreement. A corked bottle is worse than a sound one, and no preference rescues it. Objectively better, in the plainest sense.

**Wide gaps within a shared style.** An industrial supermarket Chardonnay against a fine Chablis: trained tasters would be close to unanimous, the reasons would be statable in BLIC terms, and disagreement would signal inexperience rather than a different valid view. Objectively better, in the intersubjective sense.

**Close comparisons within a peer group.** Latour against Margaux in the same vintage. Here the claim fails. Rankings shuffle between tastings — the 1976 Paris tasting demonstrated it once and every re-run has confirmed it — because there is no fact left to discover. What remains is preference wearing the clothes of judgement.

**Across purposes.** Ill-formed until a purpose is supplied.

## Conclusion

Yes — but only within limits worth stating precisely. One wine can be objectively better than another where a defect is present, and where the quality gap is wide enough that qualified judges converge on stated criteria. That is objectivity in Hume's sense, not the physicist's, and it is enough to teach on, examine on and price on. It is not enough to settle the top of any peer group, and the trade's habit of asserting it there is the least defensible thing we do.
