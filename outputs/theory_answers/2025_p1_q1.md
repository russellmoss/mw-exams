## What counts as an agrochemical, and what is left when they go

An agrochemical is any manufactured chemical input applied to a vineyard to kill or suppress a pest, pathogen or weed. That includes synthetic fungicides, insecticides, acaricides and herbicides, but it also includes the mineral fungicides permitted in organic viticulture: copper in Bordeaux mixture and elemental sulphur are agrochemicals. An organic vineyard is therefore not an agrochemical-free vineyard — it is one running a narrower, mostly contact-acting chemical toolkit, and copper's persistence in soil is the reason its use is capped in the EU. This question is not about organic viticulture and I do not treat it as such.

Strip out the chemistry entirely and four categories remain: genetic (resistant varieties, rootstocks, clones), cultural (canopy, vigour, floor and nitrogen management, pruning, sanitation), biological (predators, antagonists, microbial antagonism), and physical or biotechnical (netting, fencing, hot-water treatment, roguing, pheromone mating disruption — itself a synthesised chemical, though not a toxicant). I assess each pest and disease group against those four, weighted towards the groups on which growers actually spend their spray budget, and give a verdict on each.

## Where non-chemical control is fully effective

Phylloxera is the outstanding case and worth leading with, because it proves the principle: the insect that destroyed European viticulture is now controlled worldwide by a purely genetic solution — grafting onto American rootstocks — with no recurring input at all. Nothing else matches it, but some come close. Spider mites are largely a chemically induced problem: withdraw broad-spectrum insecticides and predatory mites such as Typhlodromus pyri re-establish and hold them, so removing the agrochemical is itself the control. European grapevine moth is strongly suppressed by pheromone mating disruption; its limitation is that it demands adoption across a whole district rather than one estate, which is a coordination problem, not an efficacy one. Trunk diseases have no chemical answer since the withdrawal of sodium arsenite, so the non-chemical levers — later pruning into sap flow, small wounds, respecting flow paths, cutting back to healthy wood rather than replanting — are the only answer available, and they are moderately effective. Animal pests are almost entirely a physical problem solved by netting and fencing; I give them no more space than that, because they are not where growers rely on chemistry.

## Where it is partially effective

Botrytis and sour rot respond well to cultural control: loose-clustered clones, restrained nitrogen and vigour, fruit-zone leaf removal after set, and preventing the berry damage caused by powdery mildew and moth larvae. In a dry-ripening climate that is usually sufficient. In a wet September it is not, and the residual risk is then managed by picking early — which buys fruit health at the cost of phenolic ripeness, and is a real quality penalty rather than a solution.

Weeds are the clearest case of a control that works agronomically but costs elsewhere. Cultivation, mowing, mulching, under-vine cover crops and sheep grazing all replace herbicide. The bill arrives as tractor passes and fuel, compaction, erosion on slopes, water and nitrogen competition on dry sites, and labour that is increasingly hard to find at scale. Effective, but expensive rather than impossible.

Nematodes are handled by resistant rootstocks at planting; on a high-population replant site, fallowing and biofumigation are only partial. Flavescence dorée sits in the same band: roguing and hot-water treatment of planting material genuinely remove inoculum, but where the leafhopper vector is established, control is legally mandated by insecticide — and the organic option, pyrethrum, is an agrochemical.

## Where it does not work: the mildews

Downy and powdery mildew are the honest test, because they are the reason spray programmes exist and *Vitis vinifera* has no useful resistance to either. Open canopies, wider rows, devigorating rootstocks, shoot positioning and leaf removal all lower pressure, but in an Atlantic or humid-continental climate they do not substitute for a fungicide in a wet season; growers who attempted it in the 2018 downy mildew year in Bordeaux lost a large share of the crop even while spraying copper. The copper alternatives under trial — plant extracts, whey, bicarbonates, UV-C — are inconsistent and mostly unproven at commercial scale.

The one genuinely effective non-chemical answer here is genetic. Resistance loci from *Muscadinia rotundifolia* and Asian *Vitis* species are now stacked in crosses such as Souvignier Gris, Solaris and the INRAE Resdur series, and they cut spraying sharply. Three constraints qualify that: single-locus resistance breaks down, so durability needs pyramided loci plus a residual programme; appellation law admits the varieties slowly; and the market for them is unproven. The effective solution requires surrendering the variety, and most growers will not.

## Verdict

Effectiveness is conditional on three things in order. Climate first: in an arid low-pressure region — Mendoza, inland Australia, much of Chile — a near agrochemical-free programme is achievable today. In a wet maritime climate with vinifera it is not, and losing the crop is not a control strategy. Scale second: sanitation, mechanical weeding and hand passes are labour-heavy, so the model is far more affordable on a small estate than across a large commercial holding. Compromise third. Non-chemical control is fully effective against pests with a genetic or biological answer, adequate against rot in dry climates, workable but costly for weeds, and inadequate against the mildews for as long as the variety is fixed. Changing the variety is the only lever that changes that answer, and it is a commercial decision rather than a viticultural one.
