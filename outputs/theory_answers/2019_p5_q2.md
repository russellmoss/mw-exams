## Framing the two levers

Terroir I take in its working sense: the fixed attributes of a site — latitude, altitude, aspect, slope, proximity to water, soil depth and water-holding capacity — plus the human tradition codified around them. Variety I take broadly as plant material: variety, clone, massal selection and rootstock, since a grower choosing "Cabernet" is in practice choosing a clone on a rootstock.

The two are not symmetrical, and the asymmetry decides the question. Terroir is a given that can be re-read and re-selected but not altered. Plant material is a decision that can be re-taken, at cost, roughly once a generation. My answer, argued below, is that a changing climate places the greater emphasis on **choice of grape variety and plant material** — because it is the only one of the two levers a grower can actually pull, and because change devalues the historic mapping of variety onto site that terroir's authority rests on.

## What is actually changing

The trap in this question is to treat climate change as warming. It is not. Mean growing-season temperature is rising, and that part is predictable: earlier budburst, earlier and compressed harvests, higher potential alcohol, lower malic acid, and a decoupling of sugar ripeness from phenolic and aromatic ripeness. A grower can plan against a trend.

The consequences that hurt are the unpredictable ones — increased variability around the trend. Earlier budburst extends the frost-exposed window, so warming produces *more* frost damage, not less; the frost of 2016 and 2017 in Burgundy and Bordeaux made the point expensively. Hail is more violent and more localised. Drought and heat spikes arrive as events rather than as averages, and a single 45-degree afternoon at veraison does damage no seasonal average records. Fire and smoke taint have become a category-level risk in Australia and California. Rainfall is redistributed rather than reduced, so wet springs raise downy mildew pressure in regions whose summers are drying out.

This distinction matters for the question. Warming alone would favour terroir: it would simply shift the optimal band uphill and poleward, and the grower's job would be to find the right site. Variability favours plant material, because resilience to a range of unpredictable stresses is bred and grafted, not sited.

## The case for terroir

The strongest version of the terroir argument is that where regulation fixes the variety, site is the only lever left. Barolo permits only Nebbiolo; the grower's entire adaptive repertoire is therefore terroir-based — planting higher, choosing cooler north- and east-facing expositions that were historically unripenable, exploiting soil water-holding differences, and adjusting canopy and harvest date. The same logic runs through Chablis, Champagne, Rioja and most of appellation Europe. On this reading, a changing climate revalues the vineyard map: parcels once considered marginal become the best, and the *hierarchy* within an appellation is the thing being rewritten.

There is a second, better version. As conditions become more extreme, the attributes that buffer extremity — deep clay that holds water through drought, altitude that restores diurnal range, maritime influence that moderates heat spikes, slope that sheds frost — become worth more relative to the attributes that merely conferred ripeness. Terroir is not being devalued; the criteria for judging it are being rewritten. That is a real and important point, and any answer that ignores it is incomplete.

But both versions share a weakness. Terroir is a constraint, not an action. Very few producers can buy a vineyard three hundred metres higher, and those who can are usually large companies whose moves — into Tasmania, into the Uco Valley's upper reaches, into England — are capital allocation rather than adaptation. For the grower already holding a site, terroir explains the problem. It does not solve it.

## The case for variety

Plant material is where the actionable response lives, and the evidence is that this is where the industry is in fact moving.

Bordeaux moved to admit a short list of additional varieties, among them Touriga Nacional, Marselan and Alvarinho, precisely because it concluded that its historic encépagement would not hold. Champagne admitted the fungus-resistant Voltis on a trial basis, which is remarkable: the most conservative appellation in the world changed its plant material rules before it changed anything else. Rioja's admission of Maturana and Tempranillo Blanco followed a comparable logic. The Barossa's growers have planted Fiano, Vermentino and Nero d'Avola, and southern Italian and Iberian varieties are being trialled across warm New World regions, because a Mediterranean climate is best answered by Mediterranean plant material. Note what these have in common: each is an appellation or region choosing to loosen the varietal rule while leaving the site rules untouched. The institutions themselves have decided that variety is the adjustable lever.

Below the level of variety, the same is true and less remarked. Rootstock choice is the single most powerful drought tool a grower has, and the shift toward deeper-rooting, drought-tolerant stocks changes a vineyard's climate response more reliably than any canopy intervention. Late-ripening clones and massal selections retain acidity and delay harvest out of the September heat. Disease-resistant crossings answer the wet-spring half of the problem that terroir cannot address at all. And unlike a vineyard purchase, replanting is available to every producer at every scale, and is reversible within a generation.

The honest qualification is that variety change is slow, expensive, and constrained by law and by consumer recognition. A Barossa grower can plant Fiano; a Chablis grower cannot plant anything. Market recognition is the harder constraint of the two: a variety nobody has heard of is a commercial risk laid on top of an agronomic one, which is why the first movers have been co-operatives and large companies able to absorb a failed block. But the constraint proves the point rather than refuting it: where variety is available, growers use it first, and where it is forbidden, the pressure now falls on the regulator to permit it.

It is worth testing the argument against its hardest case. Sicily's growers on Etna have adapted almost entirely through site — moving up the mountain, working contrade at successively higher elevations with the same Nerello Mascalese — and have produced one of the most convincing adaptations in Europe without touching plant material. That is a genuine terroir answer. But notice that it was available only because the mountain supplied eight hundred metres of vertical range within a single denomination. Most regions have no such gradient, and where a grower cannot move, the vine is what changes.

## Conclusion

A changing climate places the greater emphasis on choice of grape variety and plant material. Terroir gains in interpretive importance — the map of good sites is being redrawn, and the qualities that make a site good are changing — but a grower cannot act on terroir except by buying land or by reading his existing site more intelligently. Variety, clone and rootstock are the levers that can be pulled on the land he already holds, and they answer the variability that is the genuinely damaging part of climate change, not merely the warming. The clearest evidence is institutional: it is the varietal rules of Bordeaux, Champagne and Rioja that are being rewritten, not their boundaries.
