## Who is a new consumer

The question is about recruitment, not about growth, and the two are routinely confused. A new consumer is someone who does not currently drink wine. Selling more wine, or dearer wine, to people who already drink it is a different exercise entirely, and I exclude it.

Defined that way, there are five distinct populations, and only one of them is young:

1. **Drinkers of other alcohol** in wine-drinking markets - beer, spirits, cider and now ready-to-drink consumers who have never found a reason to cross over. This is the largest and most accessible pool anywhere.
2. **Consumers in markets with no wine tradition** - much of Asia, Africa, the Middle East and Latin America. The largest pool in absolute terms.
3. **Legal-drinking-age entrants** in every market - the group everyone means when they say Millennials, and the one that gets disproportionate attention relative to its size.
4. **Older non-drinkers of wine** - people who arrive at wine in their forties and fifties through food, travel or changed income. New is not the same as young, and this group has both money and occasions.
5. **Lapsed consumers** - people who drank wine and stopped, for reasons of health, price, occasion or a bad experience. Recruiting them is cheaper than recruiting anyone else and the industry does almost nothing about it.

The context that makes this urgent: global wine consumption has been broadly flat for a decade at around 240-250 million hectolitres while the world's adult population has grown, and per-capita consumption in France, Italy and Spain has fallen for decades. Wine is losing share of throat in its historic heartlands, which means recruitment is not an ambition but a replacement requirement.

## Who is "the industry"

"The industry" cannot act, because it is not an agent. Six actors can, and they have different capabilities, different time horizons and largely opposed interests:

- **Producers**, who control product, format and price architecture but reach consumers only through others.
- **Generic and regional bodies**, the only actors with a pre-competitive mandate - and the ones most committed to defending origin, which is precisely the thing new consumers do not understand.
- **Importers and distributors**, who control which wines exist in a market at all.
- **Retailers and the on-trade**, who own the point of decision and, crucially, the data.
- **Educators and opinion leaders** - the qualification bodies, critics, sommeliers, and now platforms and social media - who control the language.
- **Regulators**, who are not part of the industry but determine what is possible in India, China, the Gulf and the monopoly markets.

Any proposal that does not say which of these is meant to act is not a proposal.

## What stands in the way

Before tactics, the obstacles, because they determine which tactics can work. Wine is *complex*: a category organised by place, in foreign languages, with no consistent taste description on the pack - a barrier no competing category imposes. It has a *taste* problem for new palates: tannin, acidity and dryness are acquired, whereas sweetness and carbonation are not, which is why seltzer and RTD recruit so easily. It has an *occasion* problem: 75cl of a perishable, alcoholic, room-temperature liquid suits a shared seated meal and almost nothing else. It has *cultural and religious* barriers in the highest-population markets. It faces *fiscal* barriers - India levies a federal customs duty on imported wine of 150%, which places imported wine beyond the reach of the middle class the industry says it wants. And it carries a *status* barrier: the sense that one may order wrongly.

## What I would do

**Producers: build for the occasion, not the bottle.** The single-serve, chilled, portable, lower-alcohol format is not a downmarket compromise, it is the entry ramp - and it must come as a purpose-built brand, not a line extension of an existing estate. My rule would be that a recruitment product must be drinkable cold, openable without a tool and buyable for the price of two beers.

**Retailers: reorganise the fixture.** Sorting wine by country of origin is an organising principle that serves the trade and defeats the beginner. I would arrange the shelf by taste and occasion and put the country in small type, and I would expect to be resisted by every supplier whose appellation is its only asset.

**Generic bodies: spend on the category, not the appellation.** The current allocation is close to the opposite of what recruitment needs - competing origins each explaining their hierarchy to people who have not yet decided to drink wine at all. I would pool a pre-competitive fund whose only remit is to recruit into the category, in the way the dairy and pork boards have done.

**Educators: unbundle the ladder.** Consumer education is currently structured as the bottom rung of a professional qualification. I would separate it entirely - short, free, taste-first, food-led, with no vocabulary requirement and no exam - because the beginner needs permission, not a syllabus.

**On-trade: make trial free of consequence.** By-the-glass and small-format pours, and the removal of the ordering ritual, do more for recruitment than any list. The moment of maximum deterrence in wine is being handed a list in a restaurant.

**Emerging markets: start from the local table.** Wine recruits through food. In a market with no wine tradition the entry argument is what to drink with the food people already eat, not the classification of a European region. That means generic bodies funding local culinary partnerships rather than exporting the appellation.

**Everyone: recruit the lapsed.** Retailers hold the data to identify consumers who stopped buying wine and no one uses it. A low-and-no proposition, an honest health conversation and a lower price point address most of the reasons they left.

## Where I would put the money

My position is that the industry has its priorities inverted. It spends disproportionately on young consumers in mature Western markets - a small, saturated, heavily contested pool - while the largest recruitable populations are middle-income consumers in Asia, Africa and Latin America, and older non-drinkers and lapsed drinkers at home.

And I would rank the interventions by leverage rather than by cost. The most valuable single change is not a product or a campaign but the removal of the language barrier. Wine is the only major consumer category that requires the buyer to learn a vocabulary before transacting. Fix that - on the label, on the shelf, on the list - and every other tactic works better.

## Conclusion

The industry can attract new consumers, but not as an industry; only as identified actors doing specific things for identified populations. The prize is real, because the recruitable pool is vastly larger than the drinking one. But it requires the trade to accept something it finds genuinely uncomfortable: that the origin-based hierarchy which gives wine its cultural value and its margin is the same thing that makes it hard to start drinking. Recruitment means being willing to set that aside at the entry point - and the industry's reluctance to do so is why the numbers have not moved.
