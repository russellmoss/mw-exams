## Where stems enter the process

Stems reach a wine at three points, and the argument differs at each. At the press, as whole uncrushed bunches so the rachis stays with the fruit. At the fermenter, as intact clusters under the cap or as a measured proportion of destemmed rachis returned to a crushed must. And after fermentation, as stems left with the skins during extended maceration or in a qvevri. I take the question across all three points and across white, rosé, red, sparkling and fortified production. The starting position matters: the mechanical destemmer made destemming the default, so the burden of proof sits with inclusion, not against it.

## The press house: whites, rosé and sparkling

Here the stem is a pressing aid, not a flavour. A lattice of rachis — roughly 2-5% of bunch weight but a much larger share of its volume — opens drainage channels through the cake, so juice runs at lower pressure over a shorter cycle. The advantage is cleaner, lower-solids juice with less phenolic pickup, less bitterness and less oxidative browning. That is why whole-bunch pressing is the norm for traditional-method base wine, where the fractionated cycle separates a low-phenolic cuvée from a firmer, more oxidisable taille: in Champagne, 4,000 kg of whole bunches yields 2,550 litres of legally usable juice, of which the first 2,050 litres are the cuvée. The same logic governs direct-press Provençal rosé, where pale colour and no grip are the entire brief.

The disadvantage is economic and non-negotiable. Whole-bunch pressing demands hand-harvesting into shallow crates, cuts throughput per press cycle, and requires press capacity sized for uncrushed fruit. Machine-harvested fruit arrives destemmed by definition. Below the mid price tiers that cost cannot be recovered.

## The fermenter: red wine

In red ferments stems do four things, each double-edged.

They buffer temperature and hold the cap open, improving drainage and making the pomace press out faster — worth having in a warm vintage or a deep vat, immaterial in a small one.

Intact berries within a whole cluster ferment intracellularly, generating carbonic-derived esters that read as aromatic lift, perfume and spice. Pushed too far, the same chemistry gives banana and confection that flattens varietal identity.

The rachis contributes tannin unlike seed or skin tannin: fine-grained, drying at the finish, and consistently read by tasters as heightened acidity and tension. Where fruit is soft and structurally light this is a real gain, which is why the advantage concentrates in Grenache, Syrah and Pinot Noir. It is a poor fit for Cabernet Sauvignon or Merlot, which already carry firm tannin and, in Cabernet's case, a pyrazine profile that stems reinforce rather than balance.

And stems absorb. They take up water and alcohol, so a whole-bunch ferment finishes marginally lower in alcohol, and they adsorb anthocyanins, so it finishes paler.

## The case against, which is why most wine is destemmed

Ripeness is the first argument. Stems lignify later than fruit, and a green rachis delivers herbaceous, bitter, stalky character and an aggressive finish. Only a fully brown, woody stem is usable, and in a cool or interrupted season that condition simply is not met — so the technique carries vintage risk that destemming does not.

Second, chemistry works against the sensory impression. Stems leach potassium, raising pH and lowering titratable acidity, which weakens colour, reduces the efficacy of a given free SO2 and increases microbial vulnerability — the opposite of the freshness the taster reports.

Third, capacity and yield. Stems occupy tank volume, absorb wine that is then lost to press, and lower the effective throughput of a fermenter that has been paid for by the litre.

Fourth, hygiene. Stems arrive with vineyard debris, spoilage yeasts and residues, and cannot be sorted as reliably as berries.

Fifth, control. Whole-bunch fermentation is slower to start, harder to homogenise and harder to reproduce vintage to vintage — a serious objection for any brand selling on consistency.

## Skin-contact whites, qvevri and fortified

Whites are routinely and wrongly left out of this discussion. In qvevri and modern skin-contact winemaking, stems are included deliberately to supply tannin that acts as an oxidative buffer during long unsulphured maceration, and to give the drying grip that defines the style. The disadvantage is severe bitterness where stems are unripe, and a wine whose texture excludes most of the market.

In fortified production the case is weakest. Port's maceration is cut short by fortification, so stem tannin is extracted harshly and incompletely; near-universal destemming there is a considered decision, not a default.

## Conclusion

Stem inclusion is justified in two narrow places: as a mechanical pressing aid for premium white, rosé and sparkling base wine, and as a structural and aromatic tool for soft, low-tannin red varieties in ripe vintages, where the rachis is lignified. Everywhere else the balance of pH, colour, ripeness risk, hygiene, capacity and cost explains why the industry destems. It is a technique whose upside is real but conditional, and the conditions are demanding.
