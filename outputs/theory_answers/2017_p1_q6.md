## Definitions and selection

**Pests** are animals that damage the vine — arthropods, nematodes, birds and mammals. **Diseases** are caused by fungi, oomycetes, bacteria, phytoplasmas and viruses. To **manage** is not to eradicate but to hold losses below an economic threshold, using prevention, monitoring and targeted intervention within the legal and philosophical limits the grower works under. **Principal** I define by three tests: global incidence, economic severity, and the absence of a cheap cure. That last test is what makes the modern list different from the historic one — the mildews are serious but routinely controlled, whereas trunk disease and the vector-borne viruses have no cure at all.

On those criteria I take eight: powdery mildew, downy mildew, botrytis bunch rot, the trunk-disease complex, Flavescence dorée, Pierce's disease, the leafroll and red blotch viruses with their vectors, and fanleaf with its nematode. Phylloxera I dismiss briefly: resistant rootstocks have made it a manageable historical problem everywhere they are used, and it now matters only where own-rooted vines survive — Chile, parts of the Barossa under quarantine, Santorini's sand.

## Fungal diseases: the routine but unrelenting group

**Powdery mildew** (*Erysiphe necator*) is the most universal. It needs no free water and is most active around 20-27C, so it strikes in dry Mediterranean and Australian seasons where growers may be least alert. Management is preventive: an open canopy through shoot thinning and leaf removal, sulphur — which is also the organic grower's principal tool — and, conventionally, DMI and strobilurin sprays rotated by mode of action to manage resistance.

**Downy mildew** (*Plasmopara viticola*) needs warm rain, so it dominates in Bordeaux, the Loire, northern Italy and humid New World sites. Copper is the organic answer and the organic problem: it is a protectant only, it washes off, and it accumulates permanently in soil, which is why the EU caps copper at an average of around 4 kg per hectare per year. Conventional growers use systemics; both depend on drainage, canopy openness and forecasting models.

**Botrytis bunch rot** is managed almost entirely by cultural means: loose-clustered clones, fruit-zone leaf removal at set, restrained nitrogen, and avoiding any wound — insect, powdery mildew scar or split — that admits it. Botryticides are a supplement, timed at flowering and bunch closure.

**The trunk-disease complex** — Esca, Eutypa and Botryosphaeria dieback — is in my view the most serious current threat, because it is incurable, chronic and worsened by the loss of sodium arsenite. Infection is through pruning wounds, so management is entirely preventive: prune late and in dry weather, make small wounds, apply wound sealants or *Trichoderma*, adopt double pruning, use clean and hot-water-treated nursery material, and practise remedial curettage on valuable vines.

## Bacteria and phytoplasmas

**Flavescence dorée** is a phytoplasma spread by the leafhopper *Scaphoideus titanus*. It is a notifiable disease in France and Italy, with compulsory insecticide treatment and rogueing in declared zones — a real conflict for certified organic growers, who must use pyrethrum with its poor selectivity or seek derogation. Hot-water treatment of planting material at the nursery is the other control.

**Pierce's disease** is caused by the bacterium *Xylella fastidiosa*, vectored in California by the glassy-winged sharpshooter. Management is area-wide vector control, removal of riparian host plants, quarantine on nursery movement, and — the only durable answer — resistant varieties from the long-running breeding programme at UC Davis. Cold winters remain the best natural control, which is why it does not threaten northern Europe yet.

## Viruses: the incurable group

Leafroll (particularly GLRaV-3) delays ripening and strips colour; it is spread by mealybugs and soft scale. Red blotch produces a similar ripening failure and is associated with insect transmission and, above all, infected planting material. Fanleaf degeneration is transmitted by the dagger nematode *Xiphinema index*, which persists in old roots for years. Since nothing cures a virus, management is threefold: certified virus-tested planting material, roguing and replanting infected vines before spread, and vector control — mealybug mating disruption, systemic insecticide where permitted, and for fanleaf a long fallow, thorough root removal and biofumigation with brassica green manure before replanting.

## Pests: damage versus transmission

The useful distinction is between pests that cause direct physical damage and those whose importance is as vectors. Deer, kangaroos, wild boar, rabbits and birds eat buds, shoots and fruit; they are managed by fencing, guards, netting and scaring devices, and the damage is proportional and visible. Nematodes, leafhoppers, mites and mealybugs cause modest direct injury but transmit the diseases above, so they must be managed at population thresholds far below the level at which their own feeding matters. That difference — treating a vector prophylactically, a browser reactively — is the heart of a sound pest strategy.

## Conclusion

The principal threats have shifted from acute to chronic. The mildews and botrytis are managed competently every year by canopy work and a spray programme calibrated to the grower's philosophy. What is not managed is the incurable group — trunk disease, leafroll, red blotch, fanleaf, Flavescence dorée and Pierce's disease — where the only effective controls are clean planting material, vector suppression and preventive pruning hygiene. Growers should be spending less anxiety on phylloxera and more on the nursery they buy from.
