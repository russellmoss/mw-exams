## Definition and approach

Skin contact is any period during which juice or wine is in contact with grape skins, and with the seeds and pulp fragments that accompany them, from the moment the berry is broken until the solids are separated by pressing or draining. It therefore covers pre-fermentation maceration, fermentation on skins, post-fermentation maceration, and the incidental contact that occurs inside a press. The first summary point worth making is that every wine experiences some skin contact: even a whole-bunch-pressed Champagne base has juice moving across skins under pressure. The question is not whether, but how long, how warm, how vigorous and how oxidative.

I examine it by wine category, because the extraction chemistry is broadly common but the risks and the commercial consequences differ sharply between colours and styles.

## White wines

The gains are aromatic and textural. Bound terpene precursors in Muscat and Gewürztraminer, and thiol precursors in Sauvignon Blanc, sit in the skin, so a short cool maceration of six to twelve hours before pressing raises aromatic intensity and adds mouthfeel and body — routine in Marlborough and for Pinot Gris in Alsace.

The costs are real. The same contact extracts phenolics that read as bitterness, raises potassium and therefore pH, picks up colour, increases protein and so the bentonite requirement, and exposes the must to oxidation and, on any rot-affected fruit, to laccase. It also slows the press cycle at the busiest point of vintage. The judgement is variety-specific: what flatters Gewürztraminer would coarsen Chablis.

## Skin-contact and amber whites

Taken to weeks or months — Kakheti in qvevri, Collio and Brda in a range of vessels — skin contact converts a white wine into a structurally tannic one. The pros are genuine: tannin and oxidative stability let these wines be made with very little SO2 and age far longer than their fruit alone would allow, and they are commercially distinctive. The cons are bitterness, loss of primary fruit, elevated volatile acidity and Brettanomyces risk under long unprotected maceration, and a narrow market.

## Rosé

Here skin contact is the whole design. Direct pressing gives the palest, most delicate wines of the Provençal type; a short maceration of a few hours gives more colour and more phenolic weight; saignée bleeds off a red fermentation, giving the deepest rosé but subordinating it to the red it was drawn from. The con is that colour and phenolics move together, so a producer chasing extra flavour risks both a shade the market has moved away from and a bitter finish.

## Red wines

Red winemaking cannot be waved through simply because skins are always present, because the whole craft lies in how much contact and of what kind. Skin contact delivers anthocyanin, tannin, flavour and the polymeric pigment that gives colour stability and ageing capacity. Different routes reach the same place: gentle punch-down in a small open vat, pump-over in a tall tank, and a rotary fermenter can each arrive at a soft, well-coloured wine, the last in two or three days rather than ten. The same technique can serve opposite ends — punching down infrequently and gently for a delicate Burgundian infusion, or hard and often for maximum extraction from the same fruit.

The risks are equally specific: over-extraction of hard seed tannin once alcohol is high, acetic bacteria and ethyl acetate on an exposed cap, Brettanomyces during a long post-fermentation maceration, potassium-driven pH rise, and the plain capital cost of tank time.

## Sparkling, fortified and sweet wines

In traditional-method sparkling the object is to minimise contact: whole bunches, gentle low-pressure pressing and discarded later fractions, because phenolic bitterness is merciless in a bone-dry, low-dosage wine and because black grapes must give white juice. The exception, Champagne rosé by saignée, shows contact used deliberately for colour.

In Port the extraction window is tiny — everything must be pulled from the skins in two or three days before spirit arrests fermentation — which is why lagares, foot treading and robotic treaders exist. In Tokaji, macerating aszú berries in must for of the order of twelve to thirty-six hours is skin contact under another name, extracting sugar, flavour and phenolics; in Sauternes the opposite choice is made, whole bunches pressed gently to keep laccase-borne oxidation and bitterness out. Two sweet wines of comparable stature, and opposite answers to the same question — which is the point about context.

In Sherry the fruit is pressed quickly and lightly, because Palomino's phenolics would coarsen a wine that must then spend years exposed to oxygen or to flor; extraction here would be the enemy of a style built on what happens after fermentation.

## Conclusion

Skin contact is the winemaker's principal lever on structure, and its pros and cons are the same phenomenon read in two directions: everything it adds — phenolics, colour, aroma precursors, potassium, ageing capacity — it adds together, along with exposure to oxygen and to microbes. The notable trend of the past decade is that whites have moved towards more extraction and reds towards less, which suggests the profession now treats skin contact less as a default setting and more as a dial to be set per wine.
