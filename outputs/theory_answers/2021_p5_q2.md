## What is a style, and what would count as a threat

By **style** I mean the recognisable sensory identity of a wine — the product of variety, site, climate, viticulture, winemaking and maturation, expressed as a package a taster could name blind. I will also argue that price and quality tier are part of style, because a €6 and a €60 wine from the same appellation are different products, not the same product at two prices.

By **threat** I mean something narrower than change. A threat is a force that reduces the number of distinguishable styles *reaching consumers*, on a timescale that matters. That distinction is the crux: styles that exist but cannot be bought are, commercially, extinct, and styles that vanish from one place while appearing in another have moved rather than died.

My position is this. **Diversity of sensory style is not under threat; it is being redistributed, and on balance it is increasing.** What is genuinely under threat is diversity of *price and quality tier* — the mid-priced, regionally specific wine that carries most of the information — and because that is where most stylistic difference is actually purchased, the loss matters more than the headline picture suggests.

## The forces of convergence

**Consolidation** is the strongest case for the prosecution. Production, distribution and retail have all concentrated, and the narrow point of the funnel is the retailer: a large UK supermarket lists a few hundred wines at a time, out of hundreds of thousands in production. Listings are won on specification — a price point, an ABV, a flavour brief — and specification buying manufactures sameness far more efficiently than any winemaker can.

**Cellar technology** makes the specification achievable. Cultured yeast selected for a flavour outcome, enzymes, tannin and oak additions, micro-oxygenation, reverse osmosis and spinning-cone alcohol adjustment, acidification and deacidification: the toolkit lets a producer hit a target profile from a wide range of raw material. Technology is style-neutral in principle and convergent in practice, because it is deployed to meet a brief.

**Viticulture** is where convergence is least discussed and most real, and it goes well beyond variety. A handful of Chardonnay and Pinot Noir clones account for a very large share of new plantings worldwide, narrowing the genetic base within a variety. Rootstock choice is made for phylloxera, nematodes and vigour, and a small number of rootstocks dominate. Vertical shoot positioning has replaced local training systems across most of the world because it suits machinery, and machine harvesting in turn requires uniform row spacing, canopy height and fruit-zone geometry — so the machine specifies the vineyard. Higher cropping levels dilute site character; herbicide and fertiliser regimes flatten differences in soil biology. Every one of those decisions is defensible individually, and their sum is a global vineyard that looks and yields more alike than it did.

## The forces of divergence

Against that, the last two decades have produced more new styles than any comparable period I can point to. Skin-contact whites, pét-nat, low-sulphur reds, seriously made rosé, dealcoholised wine, canned and kegged formats, and traditional-method sparkling from a dozen countries that had none. Old varieties have been recovered and commercialised across Iberia, Italy, Greece and Eastern Europe, and disease-resistant crossings are creating genuinely new sensory territory.

The distribution channel is the reason these survive. **E-commerce and social media** cut the cost of reaching a scattered audience to near zero, which is precisely the condition a niche style needs. A producer of two thousand cases can now build a viable global customer base without a distributor's permission — an impossibility in the supermarket-dominated world of the 1990s. **Premiumisation** funds the same effect, because difference is what a consumer paying above commodity prices is buying.

## Climate change: which way does it cut?

Most answers assume it destroys diversity. I think the net effect is closer to neutral, with an important asymmetry.

Against: it collapses styles defined by marginality. Light, low-alcohol Mosel Kabinett, the taut base wines of Champagne, brisk Muscadet, delicate Loire Cabernet Franc and Sherry's flor ecology in a hotter Jerez are all defined by conditions that are moving away from them. Worse, appellation law fixes them geographically — a style whose defining balance has migrated 300 kilometres north cannot follow it.

For: warming has created viable production in England, Denmark, Belgium, Tasmania and at altitudes in Argentina and Chile that were previously too cold, and it has made late-ripening varieties workable in places that could never finish them. Every one of those is net-new style.

My verdict: climate change produces net *change* rather than net loss, but a real transition-period deficit, because established styles disappear in a decade while new ones need a generation to acquire an identity anyone recognises.

## Regulation, health and the demand side

Political and regulatory forces cut both ways. Appellation rules preserve styles and simultaneously prevent adaptation — Bordeaux's admission of a short list of new varieties, capped at a small share of plantings and of the blend, is preservation and obstruction in one act. Conversely the UK's 2023 duty reform, which taxes wine by alcoholic strength, is a rare instance of regulation actively *creating* stylistic diversity by making lower-alcohol wines cheaper to sell.

Health and anti-alcohol pressure, from warning labels to lowered consumption guidelines, principally shrink volume rather than variety — though they push producers towards the no-and-low category, which adds styles.

The generational argument needs care. The claim that younger cohorts have abandoned wine rests largely on national market-tracking surveys and self-reported consumption panels; those are useful for direction but weak on magnitude, they rarely follow the same people over time, and they cannot separate a cohort effect from a life-stage one. What the data support is that younger drinkers spread spending across more categories — spirits, RTDs, craft beer, no-alcohol — not that wine's stylistic range is contracting. Competition takes occasions, not styles.

## The part that is genuinely under threat

Diversity of quality and price is the weak point, and it is the element of style least often examined. Costs of glass, freight, labour and energy have risen fastest at the bottom, retail margin has not moved, and the entry tier is being squeezed towards a smaller number of very large brands. Premiumisation protects the top. The casualty is the middle: the €10–20 wine of specific origin, made in commercially meaningful quantity, which is where most consumers ever encounter a regional style at all. Lose that and stylistic diversity survives as a connoisseur's museum while the mass market gets a shorter menu — a hollowing that Prosecco overtook Champagne in global volume in the last decade already illustrates at category level.

## Conclusion

No, the diversity of wine styles is not under threat; it is greater now than a generation ago and the digital route to market is what has secured it. But that answer is only true of the styles that exist. The diversity a consumer can actually reach is narrowing, because the mid-priced tier that carried it is being squeezed out between commodity brands and luxury. The threat is not to the range of wine. It is to the range of wine anyone can buy.
