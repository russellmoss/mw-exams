**To:** Technical Director and Wine Buying Board
**From:** Head of Quality Control
**Subject:** Implications of reducing post-fermentation SO2 additions across own-label wine
**Purpose:** To set out the technical consequences, the risk to the business by style, and a recommended policy

## 1. What post-fermentation SO2 does

SO2 added after fermentation performs three distinct functions, and reducing it withdraws all three at once.

It is an **anti-oxidant**, but indirectly: it scavenges hydrogen peroxide generated by the oxidation of phenolics, and it binds acetaldehyde, the product of that reaction, removing the bruised-apple character and freeing the wine to age reductively. It is an **anti-microbial**, inhibiting spoilage yeasts including *Brettanomyces*, lactic acid bacteria and acetic acid bacteria. It is **anti-enzymatic**, inhibiting polyphenol oxidase and, less effectively, botrytis laccase.

Only free SO2 works; bound SO2 does not. Because binding to acetaldehyde, sugars and carbonyls is continuous, free SO2 falls throughout a wine's life, faster where oxygen ingress is higher.

## 2. Molecular SO2, pH and the legal ceiling

The active species is molecular SO2, and its share of free SO2 is governed by pH. As a working rule, at pH 3.0 roughly 6% of free SO2 is molecular, at pH 3.5 roughly 2% and at pH 3.8 under 1%. The industry targets are 0.8 mg/L molecular SO2 in white and at least 0.5 mg/L in red to be considered secure against microbial change of state.

The consequence for us is that a total or free SO2 figure alone is a meaningless specification. Achieving 0.8 mg/L molecular requires about 40 mg/L free at pH 3.5 but only some 15 mg/L at pH 3.0. A supplier reducing SO2 in a high-pH warm-climate wine is taking a far greater risk than one reducing it in a cool-climate wine of low pH, and our specification must be written in molecular terms with pH recorded.

The legal frame is a ceiling, not a target: EU maxima are broadly 150 mg/L total for dry red and 200 mg/L for dry white and rosé, rising for sweet wines; organic wine reduces those ceilings by roughly 30 to 50 mg/L. Separately there is the labelling threshold of 10 mg/L, above which contains sulphites must be declared — a genuine commercial driver, since only a wine below it can drop the declaration.

## 3. Implications by style

**Aromatic whites (Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Riesling), screwcap.** Highest exposure. Volatile thiols are lost rapidly once free SO2 falls, and colour browns. Our current 30 to 35 mg/L free should not be reduced; reduction here costs the varietal character the shopper bought.

**Off-dry and medium whites.** Residual sugar plus reduced SO2 is our worst refermentation risk. Reduction is not acceptable without compensating sterile filtration and validated packaging.

**Rosé.** Colour is the whole proposition; oxidation shifts pink to orange within months, and pinking risk rises. Treat as aromatic whites.

**Commercial reds for early drinking.** Most tolerant. Phenolics provide intrinsic antioxidant capacity, and a modest reduction from our 25 to 30 mg/L free is defensible provided pH is below 3.6, since *Brettanomyces* and volatile acidity are the risks and both are pH-sensitive.

**Premium reds intended to age.** Reduction shortens the useful life and raises *Brettanomyces* risk during barrel maturation, when free SO2 is topped between rackings; the saving is not worth it.

**Sweet and botrytis wines.** Highest sugar, laccase present, refermentation catastrophic. Our 45 to 55 mg/L free is a floor, not a target for reduction.

**Sparkling and low-alcohol/de-alcoholised.** Both are microbiologically fragile and low-alcohol wines lack ethanol's protection; SO2 reduction here needs an alternative preservation system, not a smaller dose.

## 4. Implications for handling and for the business

Reducing SO2 transfers the burden onto oxygen management. Every step must be tightened: dissolved oxygen and total package oxygen measured at fill, inert gas on tanks and fillers, closure oxygen-transmission specified rather than assumed, and bottling scheduled close to despatch. Sterile filtration and hygiene become critical rather than routine, and dimethyl dicarbonate to the EU limit of 200 mg/L can sterilise at bottling but gives no ongoing protection. Cold-chain shipping and warehousing, and shorter code life with faster rotation, follow.

The commercial pressure is real: consumer sensitivity to sulphites, the growth of natural and low-intervention wine, and organic listings all push in this direction. But the risks land on us, not the supplier. Oxidised or refermenting own-label wine means customer complaints, returns, potential withdrawal, the cost of replacement stock and, most damaging, loss of trust in a label carrying our name. A shorter shelf life also breaks our planning assumptions for seasonal and promotional volumes.

## 5. Recommendations

1. **Specify molecular SO2, not total.** Set 0.8 mg/L molecular for whites, rosés and sweet wines and 0.5 mg/L for reds at bottling, with pH stated on every certificate of analysis.
2. **Permit reduction only in commercial reds below pH 3.6**, and only against evidence, not aspiration.
3. **Prohibit reduction in off-dry whites, rosé, sweet wines, sparkling and low-alcohol** without a validated alternative preservation and packaging system.
4. **Make reduction conditional on oxygen control**: measured TPO at fill, specified closure OTR, and accelerated shelf-life trials with a tasting panel before listing.
5. **Where reduction is approved, shorten code life accordingly** and re-tender annually, treating any low-SO2 line as a higher-risk listing with tighter retained-sample monitoring.

The reduction is achievable, but only as a differentiated policy backed by oxygen control — not as a single number applied across the range.
