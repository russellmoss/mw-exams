## Two measures, not one

Titratable acidity is the total concentration of titratable protons, expressed as tartaric acid; pH is the concentration of free hydrogen ions, and it is set by the ratio of acids to their salts — above all potassium bitartrate. The two move together but not proportionally, and the gap between them is where most adjustment decisions live. A ripe Californian Chardonnay can show 6 g/L titratable acidity and still sit at pH 3.8 because potassium uptake has half-neutralised its tartaric acid. Adjusting acidity means deciding which of the two numbers is the problem.

## Why acidity is adjusted

**Balance and flavour.** Acidity provides the structural counterweight to alcohol, sugar and tannin. A wine short of it tastes flabby and short; a wine with excess tastes green and hard. Low-pH wines also read as fresher and show primary fruit more clearly.

**Microbial stability.** This is the technically decisive reason. Sulphur dioxide is only antimicrobial in its molecular form, and the molecular fraction collapses as pH rises: the same free sulphur dioxide that protects a wine at pH 3.3 is largely ineffective at pH 3.8. High pH therefore invites *Brettanomyces*, lactic acid bacteria and acetic acid bacteria, and correcting pH is a spoilage-prevention measure before it is a stylistic one.

**Colour and longevity.** In red wine, low pH shifts the anthocyanin equilibrium toward the coloured flavylium form, so the wine is brighter and more stable; high-pH reds look dull and brown early. Low pH also slows oxidation and helps a wine age.

## Raising acidity

**Tartaric acid** is the standard. It is the strongest of the grape acids, is not metabolised by yeast or bacteria, and is native to wine, so it is the only addition that lowers pH durably. Its drawback is that some of it precipitates as potassium bitartrate, so part of the addition is lost and the wine's tartrate stability shifts. A working rule is that roughly 1 g/L of tartaric acid moves pH by about 0.1 units in a lightly buffered white, and materially less in a high-potassium red — buffering capacity, not the dose, decides the outcome, which is why bench trials with a pH meter matter more than the arithmetic.

**Malic acid** raises titratable acidity with a sharper, greener taste and less pH movement. It is only sensible in wines that will not undergo malolactic conversion, since bacteria would convert it straight back.

**Citric acid** is the weakest lever and the most conditional: it is metabolised by lactic acid bacteria to acetic acid and diacetyl, so it must not be used in a wine at risk of a bacterial pass. It is capped at roughly 1 g/L in the finished wine, and is used more for its iron-chelating effect and for lifting a finished white than for real correction.

## Lowering acidity

**Malolactic conversion** is the routine biological route: converting dicarboxylic malic acid to monocarboxylic lactic acid typically costs 1 to 3 g/L of titratable acidity and lifts pH by 0.1 to 0.3, while adding texture and buttery diacetyl. It is standard for red wine and elective for white, and it is the reason a cool-climate Chardonnay can be softened without any chemical addition.

**Chemical deacidification** uses calcium carbonate or potassium bicarbonate. Both neutralise tartaric acid; around two-thirds of a gram of calcium carbonate neutralises a gram of tartaric acid. Potassium bicarbonate is preferred late because the resulting bitartrate can be cold-stabilised out, whereas calcium tartrate is slow and unpredictable to precipitate and can throw a deposit in bottle months later. Both raise pH, which is often the opposite of what a high-pH, high-acid wine needs.

**Double-salt deacidification** treats a portion of must with excess calcium carbonate so that calcium tartromalate precipitates, removing malic acid as well as tartaric, then blends it back. It is the tool for genuinely unripe, malic-dominated musts in marginal vintages. Ion exchange and electrodialysis offer pH manipulation where permitted, but their legality varies.

## What decides the call

Climate is the primary factor: warm regions acidify as a matter of course because malic acid is respired away in the heat and potassium accumulates, while marginal regions deacidify in cool vintages. Variety matters — Palomino and Grenache arrive at low acidity and high pH, Riesling and Chenin Blanc rarely need help. Style decides magnitude: a wine sold on freshness needs a lower pH than a broad, oxidatively handled one. And the law constrains everything: the European Union sets acidification and deacidification limits by wine-growing zone and forbids enriching and acidifying the same wine, whereas Australia and the United States allow acidification essentially freely. A producer in Jerez adds tartaric acid to low-acid Palomino must as standard practice; a producer in Burgundy is far more constrained.

## Timing, from must to pre-bottling

Acidifying the must is best: the acid integrates through fermentation, tartrate equilibrium is reached before stabilisation, and the low pH protects the must and improves sulphur dioxide efficiency from the start. Additions to fermenting wine are avoidable but workable. Post-fermentation additions taste separate and need time and a cold-stability check before bottling; small pre-bottling corrections are legitimate for final polish but should be blended slowly and given settling time. Deacidification is best done early on must, except for potassium bicarbonate, which is a late, small-scale corrective.

## Beyond still red and white

Sparkling base wines from warm regions are routinely acidified to preserve the high acidity the second fermentation and long lees ageing demand. Botrytis-affected sweet wines need acidity to balance high residual sugar without compounding already-elevated volatile acidity. In fortified production, acid additions protect low-acid musts through a short fermentation before spirit arrests it.

## Conclusion

Acidity is adjusted because pH governs microbial safety, colour and ageing before it governs taste. The competent decision is made on pH rather than titratable acidity, taken as early as the must where possible, chosen with an eye to whether the wine will see malolactic conversion, and executed inside the local law.
