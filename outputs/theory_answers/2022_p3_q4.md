## Scope and the governing principle

Two things must be right before wine can be sold in the EU. It must have been made using oenological practices the EU authorises, and it must be labelled and documented as EU law requires. This applies equally to wine made inside the Union and to wine from a third country: origin outside the EU is not an exemption, it adds a certification step. I read the question as covering the analysis needed to demonstrate compliance and to populate the label, and the mandatory label particulars themselves. The framework sits in the common market organisation regulation and its delegated and implementing acts on labelling and on oenological practices, overlaid by general EU food law on allergens, contaminants and lot marking.

## Analysis

Analysis serves two purposes: proving the wine is legal, and supplying the figures the label must carry.

The certificate-of-analysis parameters normally required for a third-country wine are actual and total alcoholic strength, total dry extract, total acidity, volatile acidity, total sulphur dioxide, residual sugar, and — for sparkling wine — pressure. Those figures must be capable of showing compliance with the legal limits. The ones that most often catch producers are:

- **Volatile acidity**, capped at roughly 18 milliequivalents per litre for white and rosé and 20 for red.
- **Total sulphur dioxide**, capped at broadly 150 mg/L for dry reds and 200 mg/L for dry whites and rosés, with higher ceilings for sweet wines and for certain PDOs.
- **Alcoholic strength**, with a minimum of around 8.5% vol for wine and a ceiling for unenriched wine, and a labelling tolerance requiring the declared figure to sit within 0.5% vol of the analysed figure and to be stated in half-percent steps.
- **Contaminants** under general food law, ochratoxin A and lead in particular.
- **Allergen thresholds**: total SO2 above 10 mg/L triggers the sulphite declaration, and detectable residues of milk- or egg-derived fining agents trigger theirs.

Use an accredited laboratory — ISO 17025 — and analyse the bottling lot itself rather than a tank sample taken months earlier. Sulphur dioxide in particular moves between tank and bottle, and it is the bottled figure that will be tested at the border.

## Documentation

Wine entering the EU from a third country must be accompanied by certification from the competent body in the country of origin, historically the VI-1 document with its analysis bulletin. That regime has been simplified more than once in recent years, and several countries operate under lighter arrangements by bilateral agreement. Precisely because it has changed, the current requirement for a given country of origin must be confirmed rather than assumed.

## Mandatory label particulars

For a still wine: the product category, or the PDO or PGI name; actual alcoholic strength expressed as "% vol"; an indication of provenance ("Wine of…", "Product of…"); the name and address of the bottler; for imported wine, the EU-established importer; the nominal volume; the lot number; and the allergen declaration. Sparkling wines add a sweetness term, from brut nature through to doux, and name the responsible producer or vendor.

The allergen declaration is a distinct obligation and is where confusion is common. "Contains sulphites" — or an equivalent wording in the language of sale — is required wherever total SO2 exceeds the threshold above, which in practice means almost every wine. Milk and egg must be declared separately where residues of casein, milk protein, egg white or lysozyme are detectable, which is a question about fining agents and their removal, not about SO2.

Mandatory particulars must appear in an official language of the member state where the wine is sold, and the core particulars must be legible and grouped in the same field of vision.

Since December 2023 two further particulars apply: an ingredients list and a nutrition declaration. The energy value must appear on the physical label; the full nutrition declaration and the ingredients list may be given electronically through a QR code, provided the linked page carries no marketing content, collects no user data, and is available in the relevant languages.

Optional particulars are regulated too. Vintage and grape variety may only be claimed where the minimum percentage rule is met and, for wine without a geographical indication, only under a certification scheme. Organic claims require certified production and the control body's code.

## How to be sure

No producer should work from memory here, and the examiners' real test is whether one knows where the answer lives. The reliable route is: the competent authority in the destination member state, which is the body that will actually challenge a label; the national export organisation at home, which usually maintains market-by-market label guides; the EU's own market-access database; and the EU importer, who carries legal responsibility and holds current artwork templates. Have artwork pre-checked before print, keep reference samples with full lot traceability, and re-check whenever the regulation moves.

## Conclusion

Compliance rests on three things: making the wine by authorised oenological practices, proving its composition by accredited analysis against the legal limits, and carrying every mandatory particular — category, strength, provenance, bottler or importer, volume, lot and allergens, now with ingredients and nutrition — in the right language and the right field of vision. The details change. The obligation to verify them with the destination market's competent authority before the label is printed does not.
