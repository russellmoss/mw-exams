## Scope: is, not should

The question asks whether France's system *is* a model for the rest of the world, which is a descriptive question about observed adoption, not a normative one about merit. I therefore judge it by what other countries have actually built, and treat the merits of the French system only where they explain why it was or was not copied.

My answer is that the extent is large but uneven, and the unevenness is the whole point. France has exported its **architecture** — the place-based, rule-bound, three-tier appellation framework — almost universally. It has exported its **hierarchies** — the ranking of vineyards and estates within a region — almost not at all. And the architecture's grip is loosening as climate and market change expose what it cannot do.

## France does not have one classification system

Weaker discussion of this topic treats "the French system" as a single thing. It is two, and they work quite differently.

The **national framework** is a pyramid of three tiers: Appellation d'Origine Protégée, Indication Géographique Protégée and Vin de France. The INAO was created in the 1930s and the AOC system codified from 1935, and AOC became AOP under the EU's PDO framework following the reforms of the late 2000s. What makes it distinctive is that it regulates far more than origin: an AOP cahier des charges fixes the delimited area, permitted varieties, maximum yields, minimum ripeness, pruning and training, ageing requirements, and submits the wine to a tasting for approval. IGP relaxes most of that and permits varietal labelling; Vin de France removes it entirely.

The **regional hierarchies** sit inside that framework and are mutually inconsistent. Burgundy ranks *land*: Grand Cru and Premier Cru are legal appellations attached to parcels, with the Grands Crus standing as appellations in their own right. The 1855 Médoc classification ranked merchants' brands by their trading prices and has been altered only once, so it ranks *names*, is unrevisable in practice, and floats free of the vineyard. Saint-Émilion revises its classification roughly every ten years, ranking *estates* and admitting the possibility of demotion — which is why it is periodically litigated. Alsace has just over fifty Grands Crus, ranking land but added piecemeal over decades. Champagne's échelle des crus ranked *villages* for grape pricing and has lapsed as a commercial mechanism.

That heterogeneity matters for the question. A country wanting to copy France must first decide which France it is copying, and the four options contradict each other on what is being ranked and whether the ranking can change.

## Where the architecture has been copied — extensively

Inside the EU, adoption is near-total, partly by law. Italy's DOCG/DOC/IGT, Spain's DOCa/DO/VdlT with the estate-level Vino de Pago, Portugal's DOC/Vinho Regional and Greece's OPAP-derived tiers are all recognisably the same shape: a top tier with binding production rules, a permissive regional tier, and a table-wine floor. Germany's 2021 wine law reform moved the country towards an origin-based pyramid, deliberately displacing the ripeness-based Prädikat logic that had been Germany's own contrary model since 1971 — the single clearest recent evidence that the French architecture wins arguments.

Outside the EU the shape recurs without the rules. The United States has American Viticultural Areas, Australia has Geographical Indications under the Label Integrity Programme, South Africa has the Wine of Origin scheme, New Zealand only legislated for geographical indications in the 2010s, Argentina has DOs with Luján de Cuyo and San Rafael as its controlled appellations, and Chile, Japan and China have all built GI systems in the last thirty years. The EU's trade agreements and the TRIPS framework have pushed the same template further still, since protecting a GI abroad requires having one at home.

So on architecture the answer to "to what extent" is: to a very great extent, and increasingly.

## Where the hierarchy has not been copied — almost anywhere

The New World schemes are delimitation-only. An American Viticultural Area requires 85% of the grapes to come from the named area and imposes no rule on yield, variety, ripeness or method; Australian and South African GIs are similar. They copied the map and left the code behind — which means they adopted the least French part of the French system.

The ranked-vineyard idea has essentially two serious imitators. Germany's VDP built a Burgundian pyramid of Grosse Lage and Erste Lage, but as a private members' association, not a state instrument. Barolo and Barbaresco's MGA scheme delimits named vineyards without ranking them — a conscious decision to take Burgundy's geography and refuse its hierarchy. Beyond those, Hungary's historic Tokaj vineyard classification and the Douro's vineyard scoring system, which grades sites by points for the allocation of Port production rights, both predate or parallel the French models rather than imitating them.

## Why the hierarchy did not travel

The architecture is administrable: draw a boundary, write rules, audit compliance. Any competent ministry can do it in a decade.

A hierarchy is not administrable in the same way, because it requires an agreed history. Burgundy's crus encode centuries of observed differences between adjacent parcels under near-identical management and a single variety — conditions no young region can synthesise. Rank vineyards in a region planted forty years ago and you are ranking the winemakers, which is contestable, litigable and immediately obsolete. Saint-Émilion's periodic disputes show that even in France ranking estates is barely sustainable, and the 1855 classification's immunity to revision shows the alternative is a hierarchy that has stopped tracking reality.

## The disadvantages, and the counter-model

The French system's costs are real. It guarantees origin and process, not quality, so a poorly made Grand Cru outranks an excellent IGP on the label. It is rigid against change: varieties, yields and methods are fixed by rules that take years to amend, precisely when a warming climate requires flexibility. It drives its most inventive producers out of the system — the Vin de France declassification route, and Italy's Super Tuscan precedent, are both innovation escaping classification. And it is illegible: several hundred AOPs, named after places most consumers cannot locate.

Meanwhile the genuinely dominant global model is a different one. Most wine is sold on variety and brand, and where a hierarchy exists it is usually the producer's own — a bin- or tier-ladder within a single brand, offering the consumer a legible quality ranking that requires no knowledge of geography. On volumes sold, that model has been imitated far more widely than anything France has produced.

## Conclusion

To a considerable extent, and decreasingly. France's national three-tier appellation architecture is the template for the classification systems of most wine-producing countries and remains the default answer to the question "how should origin be regulated". Its internal hierarchies — the part the trade actually reveres — have almost no imitators outside the private sphere, because they cannot be manufactured. And in the market rather than the statute book, the brand-and-variety ladder has out-competed both.
