## Scope, and the one variable that dominates

Once a wine is packed it stops being a winemaking problem and becomes a physical asset with a shelf life, held in a warehouse, a distributor's facility or a retailer's back room. I am concerned here with static storage and deliberately not with transport, which raises its own issues of transit temperature and handling. Six issues matter: temperature, humidity, light, taint, closure behaviour over time, and stock management. They are not equal, and I will argue that temperature is the dominant one and that light and taint are the two most commonly neglected.

## Temperature

Temperature acts twice over. Chemically, wine ageing is a set of oxidative and hydrolytic reactions whose rates follow the Arrhenius relationship — as a working rule, reaction rates roughly double for every 10 degree Celsius rise. A wine held at 25 degrees is therefore ageing at something like four times the rate of the same wine at 5 degrees, losing fruit, gaining aldehydic and cooked-fruit notes and browning. This is why the industry standard for long-term storage is 12 to 15 degrees Celsius: cool enough to slow the reactions, warm enough to avoid tartrate precipitation and the cost of deep refrigeration.

Physically, warmth expands the wine faster than the glass, raising internal pressure until liquid is forced past the closure. Repeated cycling pumps wine out and air in, so the ullage grows and oxygen ingress accelerates — which is why fluctuation is more damaging than a steady moderate temperature. Weeping capsules and stained labels are the visible symptom, and they render stock unsaleable long before a consumer tastes anything.

## Humidity

Relative humidity of roughly 60 to 75 per cent is the working target, and the reasoning is more about packaging than about wine. Below that, exposed cork surfaces dry, labels curl and cartons lose rigidity. Above it, mould grows on labels, corrugated board loses compression strength and pallets slump — a genuine warehouse safety issue as well as a stock write-off. The often-repeated claim that low humidity dries a cork through and admits air is weaker than it sounds: the wine-facing end of a cork in a bottle stored on its side stays saturated regardless of room humidity. The honest position is that humidity is primarily a packaging integrity variable.

## Light

Light strike is the most under-managed storage risk because the damage is invisible until the bottle is opened. Riboflavin absorbs light in the near-UV and blue range and, in the presence of methionine, drives the formation of methanethiol and dimethyl disulphide — cooked cabbage and drains. Champagne and other low-phenolic whites in clear or pale green glass are the most vulnerable, which is why the fault has long been known in Champagne as goût de lumière. The evidence is unusually good here: the effect is reproducible within hours of supermarket fluorescent exposure. Controls are cheap and effective — dark warehousing, keeping cartons closed, UV-absorbing glass and dark bottles, and limiting display time under retail lighting.

## Taint from the storage environment

A warehouse can contaminate wine it never touches. Chlorophenols and bromophenols from treated timber, pallets, packaging board and fire-retardant coatings are methylated by moulds to chloroanisoles and 2,4,6-tribromoanisole, which migrate through closures and cartons into the wine at parts-per-trillion levels. There are documented industry incidents of whole warehouses being condemned this way. The controls are strict: no chlorine-based cleaning, no treated timber, no solvents, fuels or agrochemicals stored alongside wine, and periodic air and packaging testing. This risk is entirely avoidable and entirely disastrous when ignored.

## Closure behaviour and orientation

Storage duration must be matched to the closure. Natural cork has substantial bottle-to-bottle variation in oxygen transmission, which is the mechanism behind random oxidation and the reason two bottles of the same wine can differ after five years. Screwcaps with tin-saran liners transmit very little oxygen and can leave a reductive wine reduced; Saranex liners transmit more. A warehouse holding stock for a decade is therefore making a different decision from one turning it in six months. Orientation follows from this: horizontal storage is necessary for cork and irrelevant for screwcap and most synthetics, so the received wisdom needs qualifying rather than repeating.

## Stock management

Finally, the commercial issues. Strict rotation on a first-in-first-out basis, because a wine's value falls as its remaining shelf life shortens; accurate batch traceability and retained samples, so that a fault found in the market can be isolated to a pallet; stack heights within the cartons' compression rating; and, in bonded storage, duty-suspension compliance and security.

## Conclusion

The key issues, ranked, are stable cool temperature first, because it governs the rate of everything else; then protection from light and from environmental taint, both cheap to control and catastrophic to ignore; then humidity and closure-matched storage duration; and throughout, disciplined rotation, since the commonest reason a stored wine disappoints is simply that it was kept too long.
