## Definitions and the numbers

**Free SO2** is the fraction in solution as molecular SO2, bisulphite and sulphite, and is the only fraction that does any work. **Bound SO2** is the fraction combined with carbonyls — acetaldehyde above all, plus pyruvic acid, 2-ketoglutarate and, in botrytised wines, glucose derivatives — and is essentially inert. **Total SO2** is the sum, and it is the total that the law regulates while it is the free that protects the wine. Within the free fraction only the molecular form is antimicrobial, and its share is governed by pH: roughly 6% of free SO2 is molecular at pH 3.0, about 2% at pH 3.5 and under 1% at pH 4.0. Conventional practice targets 0.5 to 0.6 mg/L molecular for a dry wine and around 0.8 mg/L where sugar is present.

In numbers, EU total ceilings are 150 mg/L for dry red and 200 mg/L for dry white and rose, higher where residual sugar exceeds 5 g/L and rising to 400 mg/L for the sweetest categories; EU organic wine sits below that, at 100 mg/L and 150 mg/L respectively for dry red and dry white. Conventional free SO2 at bottling is set by pH rather than by colour: roughly 20-30 mg/L in a white at pH 3.2-3.4, 30-50 mg/L at pH 3.4-3.5, and higher again above pH 3.5. ...with 'no added sulphite' wines at the extreme, which still carry fermentation-generated sulphites - under 10 mg/L, the ceiling the US organic rule sets for naturally occurring sulphites.

Every milligram per litre of oxygen picked up costs roughly 4 mg/L of free SO2, so oxygen management at the filler is inseparable from the sulphite decision.

**Anti-microbial.** Molecular SO2 suppresses residual yeast, *Brettanomyces* and lactic acid bacteria, preventing refermentation, bottle malolactic and volatile phenol formation. This is the only role for which there is no substitute short of sterile filtration.

**Anti-oxidasic.** It inactivates polyphenol oxidase and, at higher doses, botrytis laccase, which is why fruit condition governs the addition long before bottling does.

**Binding with acetaldehyde.** Least important but real: bound acetaldehyde is not smelled, so a wine with adequate SO2 does not show the bruised-apple, sherried note that free acetaldehyde gives.

Downstream, the wine is in the trade's hands for months or years. Sulphites are the only protection travelling with it through container shipping, a distributor's uncooled warehouse, a supermarket shelf under lights and a consumer's kitchen cupboard. They are, in effect, the producer's proxy present in every one of those places.

## Implications of reduced free sulphites

**Shorter and less certain shelf life.** With 15 mg/L free rather than 35 mg/L, the oxygen buffer is exhausted in months rather than years. The practical implication is a shelf-life claim that must be shortened, and a wine that must be sold and drunk faster — a stock-turn and listing question, not a winemaking one.

**Narrower tolerance of the supply chain.** Reduced SO2 shifts the burden onto cold-chain logistics, reefer containers, temperature-controlled warehousing and closures specified for low oxygen transmission. Every uncontrolled link — a hot dockside, an unrefrigerated store — now produces a fault that would previously have been absorbed.

**Higher microbial risk in bottle.** Sterile filtration effectively becomes obligatory, and any wine holding residual sugar or malic acid is exposed. The consequence is bottle variation and, at worst, refermentation and recall.

**Greater bottle variation and reputational risk.** The most damaging implication is not spoilage but inconsistency: two bottles of the same batch showing differently in a retailer's or a critic's hands. For a listed brand, variation is worse than a single fault because it is unfalsifiable and cumulative.

**Commercial and regulatory implications.** The sulphite declaration is required once total SO2 exceeds 10 mg/L, so reduction almost never removes the label statement, and a producer marketing on low sulphur is buying reputational risk without the labelling benefit. Against that, low- and no-sulphite wines address a genuine consumer belief about health and headaches, sell at a premium in the natural-wine on-trade, and open organic and biodynamic listings. Retailer specifications and freight costs rise; wastage and returns rise; insurance against a recall becomes a real line item.

## Conclusion

Reduced free sulphites shift risk from the winemaker's judgement to the supply chain and the calendar. My position is that reduction is defensible where the wine is low pH, clean, sterile-filtered, sold within a defined shelf life and shipped in a controlled cold chain — and indefensible for a wine that will sit on a warm shelf a year from bottling. The decision is a commercial one about the route to the consumer, and it should be taken with the numbers above in front of you.
