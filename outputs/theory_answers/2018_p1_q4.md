## The test I am applying

A cover crop is a sward — sown or permitted to volunteer, permanent or seasonal, in every row or alternate rows — grown between vine rows or under the vine. It does four things: it takes up water and nitrogen, it binds and structures soil, it changes the surface's physical and thermal behaviour, and it provides habitat.

The question is not what cover crops do but whether they are worthwhile, and worthwhile is a comparative judgement: worth the seed, the tractor passes, the water and nitrogen forgone, against the alternatives of cultivated bare soil or a herbicide strip. My position is that **a cover crop is worthwhile wherever a grower can name the specific problem it is solving and has matched the species and management to that problem — and is actively harmful where water is the limiting factor and the sward is permanent and unmanaged.** The practice is not good in itself; it is good against a purpose. I test five purposes.

## Erosion and trafficability

On slope this is the strongest case in viticulture. In the Douro, where soils are thin, schistose and exposed to violent autumn rain on steep terraced ground, a cover crop between rows holds the soil that a grower otherwise pays to carry back up the hill. The same logic applies to the Mosel and to Priorat. Verdict: unambiguously worthwhile, and worthwhile even where water is short, because losing soil is a permanent loss and losing a little crop is an annual one.

The related benefit is trafficability. A sward carries a tractor across wet ground in a rainy harvest and prevents the compaction that a bare, worked mid-row suffers under repeated passes — a decisive argument in Bordeaux, Champagne and Oregon, and irrelevant in a dry-harvest region.

## Vigour and nitrogen

Here climate and water availability split the answer completely. On fertile, deep, well-watered soils — Marlborough's alluvial fans, much of Bordeaux's cooler clay, New World sites on old orchard ground — excessive vigour causes shading, poor fruit set, herbaceous character and botrytis. A grass sward that competes for water and nitrogen is the cheapest devigorating tool available, cheaper and more durable than shoot thinning or trimming. Sown legumes reverse the polarity, fixing nitrogen for a block that is under-vigorous or where juice YAN runs too low for a clean ferment.

On dry sites the same competition is a straight loss. In an unirrigated Barossa old-vine block, La Mancha or inland Spain, a permanent sward in the growing season takes water the vine needs at flowering and veraison. The answer is not "no cover crop" but a winter-growing sward mown or ploughed in before budburst, capturing the soil benefits in the wet season and removing the competition in the dry one. Verdict: worthwhile with different species and different management, not the same practice applied everywhere.

## Soil biology and structure

Root channels, organic matter, mycorrhizal association and improved water infiltration are real and cumulative, and in compacted or crusted soils they can increase the water reaching the vine rather than reduce it — the Douro case again, where the sward helps rain penetrate rather than run off. The honest qualification is that these benefits are slow, hard to measure in one season, and easy to claim without evidence.

## Frost

The counter-case that most answers miss. A tall sward insulates the soil and prevents it radiating stored heat on a still, clear spring night, so cover crops measurably increase radiative frost risk. In Chablis or the Loire the correct practice is to mow tight, or to cultivate the mid-row bare, ahead of the frost window. Verdict: on a frost-prone site an unmanaged sward is worse than bare soil, which is exactly why the judgement has to be site-specific.

## Biodiversity, predators and certification

Flowering swards support predatory mites and parasitoid wasps and reduce insecticide need, and for a certified organic grower a cover crop is close to compulsory, since herbicide is unavailable and permanent mechanical cultivation is expensive and erosive. Where certification carries a price premium, this alone can settle the economics.

## The economics

This is where the judgement is finally made. A cover crop costs seed, sowing, and repeated mowing or rolling passes — diesel, machinery hours and labour recurring several times a season — and it costs yield wherever it competes. Set against that, it may replace herbicide, reduce fertiliser, reduce erosion repair, and permit certification. At premium price points, where a small quality gain or a certification is recoverable in the bottle price, the calculation is easy. At commodity price points in a dry region, a permanent sward that costs tonnage and water is difficult to justify, and a herbicide strip under the vine with a mown mid-row is often the rational compromise.

## Conclusion

Cover crops are worthwhile — but as a targeted intervention, not as a default. On slopes, on fertile over-vigorous sites, in wet-harvest regions and under organic certification they earn their cost several times over. In dry, low-vigour, frost-prone or commodity-priced situations, a permanent unmanaged sward costs water, crop and money. The grower who can state what the sward is for, and has chosen the species, the row pattern and the mowing regime accordingly, is nearly always right to have one.
