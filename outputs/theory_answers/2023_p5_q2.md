## Two definitions

Two definitions have to come first, because the question is unanswerable without them.

"Wine" is a legal category before it is a sensory one, and its legal boundary is drawn in alcohol. The OIV and EU definitions both set a floor of 8.5% actual alcohol by volume for a product to be called wine, reduced to 7% in certain cool designated zones, with the alcohol obtained exclusively by fermentation of fresh grapes or must. Below that floor the product is legally something else: in 2021 the EU created separate legal categories for dealcoholised wine, at or below 0.5% abv, and partially dealcoholised wine between 0.5% and the category minimum. So on the strictest reading the question answers itself — alcohol is definitionally essential, because a liquid without it is not wine in law. That is a real point and I make it, but it is a lawyer's answer, and treating it as the whole answer would be an evasion.

"High-quality" is the harder term, and it is not a single standard. A Piedmontese grower means structural completeness and the capacity to improve for twenty years. A large branded producer means consistency, technical faultlessness and delivery of an expected style at volume. A sommelier means a wine that does a job at a table. A health-conscious consumer in Stockholm or California may now include moderation in their own definition of a good wine. Quality is producer-, category-, market- and consumer-specific, and the honest way to handle that is to judge each wine against the expectations of its own category rather than against one fixed grid — which is why I have deliberately not organised this answer around balance, length, intensity and complexity. That structure would smuggle in a single standard the question is really asking me to interrogate.

My position, stated now and defended below: alcohol is essential to high-quality wine as a *product*, because it is both the legal condition of the category and the physical carrier of most of what we mean by quality. It is not essential in any fixed *quantity*, and the belief that more of it signals more quality is one of the most damaging confusions in the trade.

## What ethanol actually does in the glass

Ethanol is not a passive by-product; it is a solvent, a texture, a preservative, a psychoactive agent and a tax base.

As a solvent it dissolves and carries the volatile compounds that constitute aroma, and its evaporation from the surface of the wine in the glass is what delivers them to the nose. Remove it and aroma release collapses — which is the fundamental, and so far only partly solved, problem of dealcoholisation. Spinning-cone and vacuum-distillation processes strip volatiles along with the ethanol; the better producers capture and re-add an aroma fraction, but the carrier is gone, so intensity and persistence fall away.

As a physical component it contributes viscosity and weight, and it registers on the palate as a faint sweetness, which is why a dry wine at 14.5% reads as rounder than the same wine at 12%. It moderates the perception of acidity and of tannin. At excess it is perceived as heat and bitterness and it flattens aromatic detail, which is why over-ripe, over-alcoholic wines so often taste simultaneously powerful and dull.

As a preservative its role is routinely overstated: ethanol is antimicrobial only at concentrations well above those found in table wine, and table wine's real microbial defences are low pH and sulphur dioxide. It is genuinely preservative in fortified wine, where spirit is added precisely to arrest fermentation and stabilise the wine.

## The answer changes by style

The strongest evidence against a universal answer is the range of styles in which high quality is achieved at very different strengths.

Mosel Riesling Kabinett is routinely bottled at around 7.5 to 8% abv, sits at the sharp end of the German quality hierarchy, and derives its tension from the interplay of residual sugar and acidity, not from alcohol. Moscato d'Asti sits at roughly 5.5%, is a DOCG, and would be worse at 12%. Among sweet wines, eiswein and Trockenbeerenauslese can finish below 8% because the yeasts stall in extreme sugar; here alcohol is almost a by-product of a process aimed at concentration.

Provence rosé shows the opposite discipline: quality depends on picking early enough to preserve acidity and delicacy, and rising alcohol is a defect, not a virtue. Champagne is the most instructive case of all, because its base wines are thin, acidic and around 10 to 11%, and its quality is manufactured afterwards by assemblage and autolysis; alcohol is a structural minimum, not a quality lever.

Then the counterweight. Port at around 19 to 20% and fino Sherry fortified to around 15% owe their identity to added spirit: without fortification, Port would be a dry table wine and fino would have no flor. Amarone's stature is inseparable from the concentration that carries it past 15%. Barolo, Napa Cabernet and Châteauneuf-du-Pape need alcohol at their natural levels to carry their tannin and extract in balance. Strip 3% from any of these and you have not made a better wine, you have made a broken one.

## The intoxicant, and the argument it drives

Remarkably little is said in the trade about the fact that ethanol is a drug, yet it explains much of wine's cultural position and most of the pressure now on it. Intoxication alters the drinking experience itself: it dulls acuity, so a wine's fourth glass is not assessed as its first was, and it underwrites wine's social function. It is also why wine is taxed as it is — the UK moved in August 2023 to a duty system charged in proportion to alcoholic strength, which converts an aesthetic argument about ripeness into a direct commercial one, and rewards producers who bottle lighter.

The no/low category tests my position rather than settling it. It is the fastest-growing segment in several developed markets and it now includes technically competent products, and I take seriously the possibility that dealcoholised wine becomes genuinely good. But it does not yet reach high quality on its own category's terms — it lacks aromatic persistence and mid-palate weight, and it commonly compensates with sugar. More importantly, if it succeeds it will succeed as a distinct category with its own quality criteria, in the way that dry sparkling and dry Sherry are separate categories, not as a replacement for wine.

## Conclusion

Alcohol is essential to high-quality wine, in two senses and no more. It is legally essential, because a product beneath the OIV threshold is not wine. And it is functionally essential, because ethanol is the solvent that carries aroma, the substance that gives body and the agent that makes wine socially and physiologically what it is; no current technology removes it without removing much of what quality consists of.

What is not essential is any particular level. Between roughly 5.5% and 20% abv, wines of the highest quality exist at almost every point, and in each case the right level is the one appropriate to the style. The industry's real error is not that it uses alcohol, but that it has repeatedly read alcohol as a proxy for ripeness, concentration and ambition. In a warming climate and a market moving toward moderation, that is the belief that has to go — not the alcohol.
