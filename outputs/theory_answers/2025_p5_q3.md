## The essence I am trying to bottle

Humanity's wine culture is not the sum of its famous bottles, and a blend that tried to sample greatness would only represent the last two centuries of European commerce. I define the essence as four things, and every decision below answers to one of them.

**Continuity**: wine is one of the oldest technologies still practised in something close to its original form, and the vine that carries it is still propagated by cutting, so the plants in the ground are physically descended from ancient ones. **Travel and adaptation**: wine culture has never stayed where it started; it moved with trade, empire, migration and faith, and every time it arrived somewhere it was changed by the place and changed the place back. **The shared table**: wine's social function — hospitality, ritual, negotiation, mourning — is more universal than any style. **The graft**: since phylloxera, almost every vine on earth is two plants joined, an Old World scion on an American root. Nothing better expresses a culture that survives by combining.

That definition has consequences. The wine must be modest in alcohol and built for food, because the table is the point. It must be led by places that are usually spoken about last. And it must not be a luxury object, because a culture belonging to everyone cannot be packaged for the few.

## The blend

A red-fruited, savoury, moderate-alcohol blend of six components, around 12.5% alcohol:

**Saperavi, Kakheti, Georgia — 30%.** The anchor, for continuity. Georgia's qvevri tradition is the most direct living link to the earliest winemaking, and Georgian qvevri winemaking is inscribed on UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage. Saperavi is also a teinturier, so it earns its place technically as well as symbolically, giving colour and acid spine to everything else.

**Areni, Vayots Dzor, Armenia — 10%.** Pale, high-toned, red-fruited, from the region of the Areni-1 cave in Armenia, the oldest known winery site. It contributes lift and perfume where Saperavi contributes weight.

**Xinomavro, Naoussa, Greece — 10%.** For travel: the vine moving into the classical Mediterranean, and with it the idea of wine as a subject of conversation rather than only of consumption. Structurally it adds tannin and a savoury, tomato-leaf register that keeps the blend gastronomic.

**Pais, Maule, Chile — 15%.** Dry-farmed bush vines, brought by missionaries, farmed for generations by smallholders and dismissed as worthless until recently. Chile's old Pais vines are among the oldest continuously producing vines in the Americas. This is travel and adaptation with its uncomfortable colonial half included, and it puts a small grower rather than an estate at the centre of the wine.

**Cinsaut, Swartland, South Africa — 20%.** Old bush vines under South Africa's Old Vine Project, which certifies heritage vineyards of 35 years and older. A French variety that became a Cape variety, and the parent of a variety created there. It gives fragrance, light tannin and drinkability, and it brings an African component that is not a token.

**Marselan, Ningxia, China — 15%.** The newest chapter. Marselan, a French cross, has become the variety China has most convincingly made its own — proof that wine culture is still being founded, not only inherited.

North America is present in the blend without a component: the rootstock. Every one of these vines except the ungrafted Chilean and Greek material stands on American roots, and I would say so on the label rather than adding a seventh wine.

What I have excluded is as deliberate. There is no Bordeaux, Burgundy, Rioja or Tuscan component. A blend that includes everything represents nothing, and those regions already speak for themselves; the tension between authenticity and commercial popularity is resolved here in favour of authenticity, and I accept the cost.

## Winemaking

Components are vinified separately in their own regions, by their own growers, and shipped in bulk for assemblage — the blending itself being the point, since assemblage is the one winemaking technique that is unambiguously about combination.

The Saperavi is fermented and macerated in qvevri, as a living practice rather than as a museum exhibit; the qvevri portion is what makes the wine taste of the definition. Everything else ferments with ambient yeast in concrete and old, large-format oak. No new oak: new oak is a nineteenth-century European signature and would smother five of the six voices. Whole bunches in the Cinsaut and Areni for perfume and freshness. Alcohol held near 12.5% by picking for flavour rather than sugar, because the table demands a second glass. Sulphur used minimally but genuinely — a wine claiming to represent everyone cannot afford to be faulty, and fault tolerance is a niche taste, not a universal one.

This is where authenticity and innovation collide, and I resolve it openly: qvevri, ambient ferments and old vines are heritage; transcontinental bulk shipment and assemblage are unambiguously modern. The wine is honest about being both.

## Packaging

Packaging carries as much of the argument as the liquid, so it is not one decision but five.

**Format.** Three, deliberately. A 750ml bottle for the ceremonial and gifted occasion; a 250ml single-serve can, which is how a great many people under thirty drink and which suits moderation; and a 3-litre bag-in-box for the everyday shared table, which is where most of the world's wine is actually drunk. A single premium bottle would contradict the definition on the first page.

**Materials and footprint.** Lightweight glass well under the industry average, because glass weight is the largest single lever in most wines' carbon footprint; the can and bag-in-box are lighter still. Components shipped in bulk and filled in-market, which cuts freight and, usefully, spreads the work.

**Closure.** Certified natural cork on the bottle, screwcap on nothing here because there is no bottle line that needs it, and the bag-in-box tap otherwise. Cork is chosen because the cork oak landscapes are themselves a cultural landscape sustained by wine demand; the trade-off is a residual taint risk, and I accept it knowingly rather than by default.

**Label and message.** No château, no Bacchus, no coat of arms — those symbols encode a hierarchy the wine is arguing against. The motif is a graft union. The name appears in Latin, Georgian, Armenian, Chinese and Arabic script with none dominant. Full ingredient, nutrition and origin data sit behind a digital label under EU rules allowing ingredient and nutrition information to be carried on a digital label, in many languages. Growers are named; the brand owner is not. Type is set large and high-contrast with braille embossing on the bottle, because accessibility is inclusivity made concrete rather than claimed.

## Conclusion

The blend is Saperavi-led, six-country, moderate in alcohol and gastronomic; the winemaking is qvevri-anchored, oak-neutral and assembled; the packaging is three-format, lightweight and multilingual. Each choice is answerable to the same four-part definition — continuity, travel, the table, the graft — and where the choices conflict with commercial logic, as the exclusion of the European classics and the bag-in-box both do, I have chosen the definition. A wine that represents humanity's wine culture has to be one that most of humanity could actually open.
