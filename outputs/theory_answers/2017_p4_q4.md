## Definition and period

**Social media** are digital platforms on which the content is created, shared and ranked by users rather than published by the brand: networks such as Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, WeChat and Weibo, video platforms, and the wine-specific review and rating communities — Vivino, CellarTracker, Delectable — which behave socially even where they are not usually described that way. The distinction that matters is between social media and digital marketing generally: a brand website, an email newsletter or a banner advertisement is digital but not social, because the audience cannot answer back. Social media is defined by the audience's ability to publish.

The period is meaningful. Facebook and Twitter both opened to the general public in 2006, Instagram followed in 2010, WeChat in 2011, and Vivino, launched in 2010, put a bottle-label scanner and a crowd-sourced score in the pocket of the consumer standing in front of the shelf. So the decade in question is precisely the decade in which social media went from a novelty to infrastructure. I argue four structural changes, then test whether classic tools still prevail.

## 1. Authority moved from gatekeepers to peers

Before 2007 a wine brand's reputation was mediated by a small number of critics and buyers; a Wine Advocate score or a supermarket listing was the route to visibility. Social media replaced a few loud voices with millions of quiet ones. The consequence for marketing is that a brand can no longer buy credibility from a single endorsement, and must instead accumulate it. Gary Vaynerchuk's Wine Library TV, which ran from 2006, is the emblem of the transition: an unaccredited retailer building a national audience with a camera, at a cost the trade press could not match.

## 2. The cost of reaching a consumer collapsed, and the advantage moved to the small

A small producer can now address a global audience without a distributor, an advertising budget or a press office. This is genuinely redistributive: the marketing advantage that scale used to confer has partly evaporated, which is one reason the last decade has been so good for small, story-rich, low-intervention producers. The offsetting change is that attention, not reach, became the scarce resource, and by the mid-2010s the platforms had converted organic reach into paid reach — so the free lunch closed, and large brands with media budgets recovered much of their advantage.

## 3. The producer lost control of the narrative

Marketing became a conversation the brand does not chair. Reviews sit permanently alongside the product, visible at the moment of purchase; a consumer scanning a label sees an aggregate score the producer did not commission and cannot remove. The practical consequences are visible in how wine businesses are now staffed and run: community management, real-time response, crisis protocols, and — more importantly — packaging and label design conceived to be photographed. The rise of pale Provencal rose through the 2010s is the clearest case of a category whose visual identity was unusually well suited to a photographic platform.

## 4. Social media became a channel, not only a medium

The most under-appreciated change is the merger of media and transaction. In China, WeChat is simultaneously a social network, a payment system and a storefront, so a brand's social presence *is* its route to market; Western platforms have moved the same way with shoppable posts. Social media also became the main engine of direct-to-consumer recruitment for wineries with a club, which is the highest-margin sale available to any producer.

## Do the classic tools still prevail?

Largely, yes — and an answer that claims otherwise is not describing the market. For the volume brands that account for most wine sold, purchase is still decided by distribution, shelf position, price promotion, packaging and brand familiarity, and the off-trade research consistently ranks price and prior experience above anything encountered online. Wine Intelligence's Vinitrac survey work across markets has repeatedly found personal recommendation and previous experience to be stronger purchase drivers than social media, with social media's influence concentrated among younger, more involved consumers. On-trade recommendation by a sommelier or waiter remains the single most decisive influence at the premium end.

The honest reading is that social media has changed marketing most at the two ends of the market — luxury, where it manages desire and scarcity, and the artisanal tail, where it substitutes for distribution — and least in the volume middle, where the supermarket shelf still decides.

## Conclusion

Over the past decade social media has changed wine brand marketing by moving authority from critics to peers, collapsing the cost of reach, taking narrative control away from the producer, and finally becoming a sales channel rather than only a message. But it has supplemented rather than displaced the classic tools: for most wine sold, distribution, price and packaging still prevail, and social media has become a hygiene factor that a brand is penalised for lacking rather than a differentiator it can win on. My position is that its greatest commercial effect has not been advertising at all — it has been enabling direct-to-consumer sale, and that is where the marketing budget should follow it.
