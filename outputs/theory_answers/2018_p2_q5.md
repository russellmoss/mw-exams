## Definitions and scope

By "ageing potential" I mean the period over which a wine will hold or improve in bottle before its fruit falls away and its structure hollows out — a capacity for beneficial development, not merely survival. By "finished wine" I mean the wine as it leaves the winery, packaged and sealed, which puts everything up to and including the closure inside the scope of the question and everything after it outside.

The vineyard sets the ceiling: phenolic ripeness, berry size, acidity and freedom from rot determine what the cellar has to work with, and no winemaking decision recovers what was not picked. But the ceiling is rarely the binding constraint, and the question is about winemaking, so I confine the answer to vinification and maturation. Four mechanisms do the work — the antioxidant reservoir, pH, oxygen exposure, and colloidal structure — and every decision below acts through one of them.

## Building the antioxidant and structural reservoir

Ageing is, chemically, slow oxidation. A wine lasts in proportion to what it has to spend on that oxidation before the fruit is consumed.

In reds that reservoir is phenolic. The decisions are maceration length and temperature, cap management, whether to press early or hold on skins after dryness, and which press fractions to keep. Extended post-fermentation maceration matters because it allows tannin to polymerise and to bind anthocyanin into polymeric pigment, which is both colour-stable and slower to oxidise than free anthocyanin. A wine extracted hard and pressed at dryness has tannin but no polymerisation; it will be tough young and hollow at ten years.

In whites the reservoir is lees and glutathione rather than tannin. Ageing on gross lees supplies mannoproteins and a reducing environment; the decision to leave a wine on lees for a year rather than rack it clean in spring buys years in bottle. Sulphur dioxide is the reservoir of last resort, which is why free SO2 at bottling is an ageing decision.

## pH, and the malolactic decision

pH governs everything downstream. Below about pH 3.4 a given free SO2 delivers far more molecular SO2 - at pH 3.2 some 20 mg/L free SO2 gives the 0.8 mg/L molecular target, where at pH 3.8 it takes nearer 80 mg/L. Low pH also shifts anthocyanin towards the coloured flavylium form and slows oxidative reaction rates. So acidification, and the choice of when to acidify, is one of the highest-leverage ageing decisions available.

Malolactic fermentation is the sharpest trade-off in the paper. Completing it removes malic acid as a microbial substrate and makes the wine biologically stable — essential for a red destined for a decade in bottle. Blocking it, as in an age-worthy Riesling, keeps a higher acidity and lower pH, which preserves aroma and freshness for longer, but leaves a wine that must be sterile-bottled and never rewarmed. Both routes can produce long-lived wine; what fails is choosing neither cleanly.

## Oxygen: how much, and how fast

The distinction that matters is between oxygen given slowly during maturation, which polymerises tannin and stabilises colour, and oxygen given at bottling, which is simply consumed and lost. A new 225-litre barrel admits of the order of 20-30 mg/L of oxygen a year, spread over the whole élevage; that is why barrel-matured reds soften and gain longevity while the same wine held anaerobically stays angular. Micro-oxygenation in tank is the industrial substitute for the same mechanism.

Against that, total package oxygen at bottling, which for a wine intended to age should be held under about 1 mg/L, is pure debit: it is spent on the first months in bottle and takes free SO2 with it. Sparging, inerting the filler bowl, jetting the headspace and minimising the drop into the bottle are therefore ageing decisions, not merely handling ones. So is the closure, since oxygen ingress rate sets the whole trajectory: a tin-lined screwcap admits very little, an engineered technical cork a specified amount, and a synthetic closure a great deal more.

## The entry-level case, which runs the other way

For a wine designed to be drunk within eighteen months, "ageing potential" means shelf life, and the decisions invert. Protective, reductive handling from press to bottle; no malolactic, to keep acidity and primary fruit; no phenolic extraction, because phenolics brown; ascorbic acid at bottling with adequate SO2 behind it; CMC rather than cold stabilisation; a very low-ingress screwcap. The aim is to hold the wine still, not to develop it, and a bag-in-box or PET package with a high oxygen transmission rate is a deliberate decision to shorten life in exchange for cost.

## Sparkling and fortified wine

In traditional-method sparkling, ageing potential is bought at tirage. The base wine's acidity, the length of time on lees before disgorgement, the dosage level and the closure under which lees ageing occurs all count; long autolysis supplies mannoproteins and reductive capacity, dissolved CO2 and low pH suppress spoilage, and a crown cap seals more tightly than a natural cork during that period.

In fortified wine the decisive choices are the timing and strength of the spirit addition and the maturation regime. Fortifying mid-ferment leaves both high alcohol and high residual sugar, two preservatives at once, which is why vintage Port ages for decades. Oxidative maturation goes further: a Madeira heated and held in cask, or an oloroso passed through a solera, has already undergone the oxidation that would spoil a table wine, and so is effectively indestructible in bottle.

## Conclusion

The decisions that matter most are those setting pH, the antioxidant reservoir, and the rate of oxygen supply — and among them the malolactic decision, the élevage regime and the total package oxygen at bottling carry the greatest weight, because each acts on the wine's entire life rather than on one stage of it.
