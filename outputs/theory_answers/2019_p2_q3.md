## What counts as rot-degraded fruit, and the scope I take

**Rot-degraded fruit** means grapes colonised by spoilage organisms that damage composition: grey rot, which is *Botrytis cinerea* on unripe or wet fruit without the concentrating conditions of noble rot; and the sour rot and bunch rot complexes — acetic acid bacteria, *Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*, *Rhizopus*, *Cladosporium*, often following powdery mildew damage or insect injury. This is emphatically not noble rot, which is a deliberate style and is out of scope.

The damage is specific and dictates every procedure below. *Botrytis* secretes **laccase**, a polyphenol oxidase that is far more aggressive than grape tyrosinase, oxidises a wide range of phenolics including anthocyanins, and — critically — is largely resistant to sulphur dioxide. It also secretes glucans that block filters, consumes sugar and yeast-assimilable nitrogen and thiamine, degrades varietal thiols and terpenes, and produces gluconic acid and glycerol. Sour rot brings acetic acid, ethyl acetate and high volatile acidity, plus *Acetobacter*. *Penicillium* and *Aspergillus* bring earthy-mouldy taints — geosmin and related compounds — and *Aspergillus* is the ochratoxin A risk in warm regions.

The question says "in the winery", so vineyard decisions — spraying, canopy work, selective picking — are outside my answer. I take the process from the receival door to the end of maturation.

## Reception and sorting

Assess before accepting. Sample loads for rot incidence, gluconic acid and laccase activity, and set a rejection threshold; heavily infected loads are worth declassifying or refusing rather than contaminating a cellar. Chill the fruit to slow both laccase and *Acetobacter*, and process immediately rather than holding.

Sorting is the highest-value intervention and cannot be replaced later: a vibrating table, then a hand-sorting belt, then optical sorting on destemmed berries where budget allows, and density sorting in brine or by flotation to float off shrivelled and mouldy berries. In whites, whole-bunch pressing without destemming leaves much of the rot in the press cake.

## The must, and why sulphur alone is not enough

Sulphur dioxide inhibits bacteria but does not reliably kill laccase, so a winemaker who reaches only for SO2 has not addressed the main problem. The available measures are:

- **Heat**: laccase is a protein and denatures. Heating must to about 45-50C for a short hold denatures laccase, and higher-temperature red processes do the same more emphatically.
- **Ascorbic acid with SO2** to protect against oxidation in the interim, used together because ascorbic acid alone produces peroxides.
- **Fining**: bentonite removes some laccase and unstable protein; PVPP and casein reduce browning and browning precursors; activated carbon at low rates removes mouldy taint and colour, at the cost of aroma; specific carbons are used for ochratoxin A.
- **Hyperoxidation** of white must: deliberately saturating with oxygen so oxidisable phenolics polymerise and settle out before fermentation, leaving a wine that will not brown later.
- **Clarification**: hard settling, flotation or centrifugation of white musts, since rot residues carry both taint precursors and microbial load.
- **Enzymes**: pectolytic preparations with glucanase activity to break the *Botrytis* glucans that otherwise make the wine unfilterable.

## Reds specifically

Reds cannot be pressed clear of their skins, so they need a different answer, and **thermovinification is the principal one**. Thermovinification heats destemmed red fruit to roughly 60-75C, holds it, then cools and either presses for a white-style ferment or ferments the heated must: it denatures laccase completely, extracts anthocyanin fast, and is standard practice for salvaging rot-affected reds in Bordeaux, Beaujolais and elsewhere. Flash détente takes the same principle further with vacuum flash cooling, which also strips green and mouldy volatiles with the flashed-off vapour. The trade-off is a fruit-forward, less structured, less age-worthy wine — an acceptable price on fruit that would otherwise be undrinkable.

Otherwise, keep macerations short, work at lower temperatures, avoid aggressive extraction of the laccase-rich, taint-bearing skins, and press early rather than running a long post-ferment maceration.

## Fermentation

Rot-affected must ferments badly, so the winemaker must compensate. Inoculate with a vigorous, SO2-tolerant, low-VA-producing strain rather than relying on ambient flora, which will include the spoilage organisms already on the fruit. Supplement nitrogen and thiamine, which the mould has consumed, to avoid a stuck ferment; a stuck ferment on rotten fruit is how a difficult vintage becomes a lost one. Control temperature, keep the ferment covered and CO2-protected, and monitor VA daily. Getting to dryness quickly, then sulphuring, closes the window in which acetic bacteria can work.

## Post-fermentation and maturation

Rack off gross lees promptly, since they carry the residues. Decide on malolactic fermentation on the numbers: a co-inoculated or early MLF shortens the unprotected period, but where VA is already high, blocking MLF and sulphuring immediately is safer. Adjust acidity where gluconic acid and potassium have distorted the balance.

Corrective processing: fining trials for taint and bitterness; reverse osmosis with an anion resin or distillation column to reduce volatile acidity; membrane and adsorptive treatments for mouldy taints; and, where legal, treatments for ochratoxin. Filter earlier and more thoroughly than usual, using glucanase pre-treatment because *Botrytis* glucans blind filters.

In maturation, protect rather than develop: these wines have already been oxidised and are low in the intact phenolics that carry barrel ageing, so use neutral vessels or none, keep free SO2 high relative to pH, top scrupulously and minimise transfers. Plan for early bottling and early drinking. Finally, blend and declassify honestly — segregating affected lots and either blending them into a second wine or selling them off is a legitimate winery procedure and often the correct one.

## Conclusion

The controlling logic is that laccase, microbial load and lost nutrition are three separate problems needing three separate answers: heat and fining for the enzyme, sorting and hygiene and prompt fermentation for the organisms, supplementation for the ferment. Handle them at the earliest stage possible, because nothing done in maturation recovers what was lost at the press.
