## Scope: a trend, not a run of bad vintages

Five consecutive years of decline is a different object from five poor harvests. Frost, hail, a heat spike at flowering or a wet set explain a small vintage; they do not explain a trend, and if the grower's records show a step down after one event with no recovery, that is a chronic consequence of an acute event, not a recurring one. So I look only for causes that are cumulative, progressive or self-reinforcing.

The diagnostic frame that makes this tractable is the yield equation: **vines per hectare, multiplied by bunches per vine (a function of bud fruitfulness and node number retained), multiplied by berries per bunch (fruit set), multiplied by berry weight.** Any real decline shows up in one or two of those terms, and each term points at a different family of causes. The first thing I would ask the viticulturist for is not an agronomy report but five years of that arithmetic, block by block. The second is the same figures for neighbouring growers and the regional average, because a decline shared across the district is a climate or seasonal trend and a decline confined to one estate is a management or biological one — and the two lead to entirely different responses.

## Falling vine numbers — usually the largest and least noticed term

Yield per hectare falls quietly when vines die and are not replaced, or are replaced with young vines that carry little. The dominant cause worldwide is the grapevine trunk disease complex — esca, Eutypa and Botryosphaeria dieback — which kills vines slowly and cumulatively, and whose incidence has risen since effective chemical control was withdrawn. Nursery-borne Petri disease loads young plantings from the start. Alongside it sit phylloxera on own-rooted vines, crown gall after winter freeze events in continental regions, and vertebrate or machinery damage. Layered on top, replant blocks in an old vineyard suffer replant disease from nematodes and depleted soil, so the gaps stay unproductive.

## Falling fruitfulness — the multi-year feedback loops

Bud fruitfulness is set in the season before harvest, which is why its causes compound. Viruses are the classic case: leafroll spread by mealybug moves vine to vine, so incidence rises annually and yield falls annually with it; fanleaf, vectored by dagger nematode, does the same in patches. Progressive shading of the renewal zone as vigour or canopy density creeps up suppresses primordia initiation. Repeated water or heat stress during the previous summer, when the primordia are differentiating, depresses fruitfulness the following spring, which is the real mechanism by which a warming, drying trend shows as declining yield. Pruning drift matters too and is embarrassingly common: spur positions walking away from the cordon year on year, or a slow reduction in retained node number as the pruner reacts to weak vines, mechanically reduces the crop.

## Set and berry weight — nutrition, soil and water

Poor set and small berries point at nutrition, soil condition and water. Nitrogen depletion under a permanent grass sward that was installed for devigoration and never adjusted; boron and zinc deficiency directly reducing set; potassium and magnesium decline. Soil health: falling organic matter, compaction from repeated passes, acidification, and under irrigation the slow accumulation of salinity and sodicity where the leaching fraction is inadequate — a genuine multi-year decline mechanism in irrigated Australia, inland California and parts of Argentina. Water supply: reduced allocation, a falling aquifer, or the mundane and very common failure of blocked or unevenly performing drip emitters. Cover crop competition, rootstock too devigorating for the site, and simply old vines, which yield less as trunk vasculature declines, complete the list.

## Addressing it

Diagnosis first, and in a sequence set by cost. Map missing and weak vines by counting or by imagery; that immediately separates a stand problem from a per-vine problem. Then petiole and soil analysis, an irrigation distribution-uniformity test, virus testing of the weak patches, and a check of pruning records and retained node counts against the block's history. Only then intervene.

Remedies follow the cause: remedial trunk surgery and sanitation pruning — pruning later, making small wounds, sealing or protecting them — plus systematic replanting and field grafting to restore stand density; mealybug control, roguing and replanting with certified virus-tested material where leafroll is spreading; organic matter, deep ripping and controlled traffic for compaction; recalibrated fertiliser split to demand, lime for pH, a leaching irrigation to move salt; repaired and re-uniform irrigation; adjusted node numbers and canopy work to restore light on the renewal zone. Finally, confirm the decline is not intentional — a change in pruning policy, an appellation yield cut, or a deliberate move to a higher-priced specification.

## Conclusion

A five-year decline is almost never one cause. Typically it is an uncounted loss of living vines plus a progressive biological agent plus a slow drift in soil or pruning, each individually small. The insight the situation demands is that the remedies are largely mutually exclusive uses of the same capital, so identifying which term of the yield equation is falling matters more than knowing the remedies themselves.
