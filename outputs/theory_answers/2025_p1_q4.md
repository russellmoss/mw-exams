## Terms and scope

Soil **management** is what the grower does to the soil after the site is chosen: tillage or no-till, cover cropping and sward composition, mulching, compost and organic matter additions, fertilisation, liming and amendment, drainage and subsoiling, under-vine weed control, traffic and compaction management, and the irrigation regime insofar as it works through the soil. It is not soil *type*. Geology and depth are given; the question is how much the management of them moves the fruit.

Grape quality I take as the measurable parameters a winemaker would specify: sugar and potential alcohol; titratable acidity and pH; tannin and anthocyanin concentration and their ripeness; flavour and aroma concentration, including methoxypyrazine level and thiol precursor supply; yeast assimilable nitrogen; fruit health; evenness of ripening; and yield at the intended specification. My argument is that soil management reaches these parameters through exactly three mechanisms — vine water status, nitrogen supply and vigour, and mineral status, principally potassium — and that its extent is therefore large where soil is the limiting factor and small where climate or season is.

## Mechanism one: water status, and the largest effect

Soil management's biggest quality lever is how much water the vine can get and when. A competitive sward, shallow tillage that severs surface roots, mulching, and the water-holding capacity built by years of organic matter addition all shift plant available water, and plant water status governs berry size. Smaller berries raise the skin-to-pulp ratio, which raises tannin and anthocyanin concentration and flavour concentration per unit volume — the mechanism behind most claims made for "quality" viticulture. A moderate deficit from fruit set to veraison delivers that. Beyond it the effect reverses: severe stress closes stomata, sugar accumulation stalls short of ripeness while acids are still respired away, aroma synthesis is impaired and the fruit arrives high in pH and low in flavour. Soil management can therefore both make and break the same parameters, and the difference is degree, not direction.

## Mechanism two: nitrogen and vigour

Nitrogen supply from the soil sets canopy density, which sets fruit-zone light and temperature. Excess nitrogen — from over-fertilisation, from incorporating a legume cover at the wrong moment, or simply from a fertile alluvial soil left clean-cultivated — gives shading, which retains methoxypyrazines, suppresses anthocyanin, delays sugar accumulation, raises malate and creates the humidity in which botrytis develops. Every one of those is a named quality parameter degraded. Restricting nitrogen through a permanent competitive sward is the standard correction on vigorous sites. But the opposite error is equally real: a starved sward-competed vineyard delivers musts low in yeast assimilable nitrogen, which cause sluggish and stuck ferments and reductive sulphides, and low vine nitrogen status is associated with reduced thiol precursor supply in aromatic whites. Soil nitrogen management therefore has a genuine optimum rather than a direction of travel.

## Mechanism three: potassium and mineral status

Potassium supply is the most underrated soil-management effect on quality. Potassium fertilisation on already potassium-rich soils, or the potassium released by heavy tillage of clay, raises berry potassium, which raises must pH — costing colour stability, microbial stability and freshness, and forcing acidification. Soil pH management matters at the extremes: strongly acid soils restrict rooting depth and so reduce drought resilience; boron and zinc deficiencies impair fruit set, which cuts yield, though the resulting millerandage can concentrate flavour. Compaction from repeated tractor passes belongs here too, because a shallow root system is the direct cause of unevenness in a dry season.

## How far does it actually go?

There is no recipe. Soil management is a strong modifier *within* the envelope set by climate, variety and season, not a substitute for any of them. It cannot make a hot region cool, and in a vintage with harvest rain it counts for very little; in a dry year, the water-holding built over a decade counts for a great deal. It is also slow — organic matter and soil structure change over years, so it is a strategic lever, whereas canopy and irrigation decisions are tactical ones available this week. My assessment is that soil management is the second-strongest set of levers a grower holds on an established vineyard, behind canopy and water management and ahead of everything else, and its effect is largest at the extremes: on high-vigour fertile sites, where devigorating the soil transforms the fruit, and on shallow droughty sites, where conserving water prevents a failure of ripening. On a well-matched middling site it is a refinement.

## Three scenarios, and the large-volume case

On a fertile, deep, wet-climate site — Marlborough's Wairau plains, the Veneto — a permanent competitive sward and zero nitrogen are transformative, cutting vigour and yield and lifting phenolic and aromatic ripeness. On a shallow, dry, warm site — Barossa dryland old vines, inland Spain — the same sward would be destructive, and management inverts to tillage or mulch to conserve water. In an irrigated arid region — Mendoza, the Riverland, the San Joaquin — soil management is mainly about salinity, irrigation efficiency and compaction, and its quality contribution is defect avoidance.

That third case is the large-volume grower's, and it deserves stating plainly. At scale, the objective is a consistent tonnage delivered to a compositional specification at the lowest cost per tonne. Cover crops that cost yield through water competition are a direct financial loss; sward choice is constrained by trafficability across hundreds of hectares; compaction is a bigger practical risk than organic matter is an opportunity. Soil management still affects quality there, but through uniformity and the avoidance of high pH and rot rather than through complexity — and a practice that cannot be justified per tonne will not be adopted, however good the fruit it makes.
