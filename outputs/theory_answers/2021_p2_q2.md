## Region, and what the two targets mean

I take the Côte de Nuits as my premium region: land values, the cru hierarchy and a market that pays three-figure sums for single-vineyard bottlings define premium far better than any statement about quality alone. It is also a region that genuinely makes both wines — a village Gevrey-Chambertin from young vines against a premier cru off the same commune — so the comparison is not smuggling in a difference of fruit quality dressed up as winemaking. Where I need a New World counterpoint I use Central Otago, premium on the same test.

**Early-drinking** means built to show best between roughly one and four years from vintage: primary red fruit, supple tannin, no requirement for reductive reserve. **Age-worthy** means built to improve for a decade or more: enough phenolic structure and acid, and enough oxygen-buffering capacity, that the wine gains complexity rather than merely surviving. Pinot Noir makes this hard, because its anthocyanins are largely non-acylated and therefore intrinsically unstable, and its tannin is modest. Age-worthiness in Pinot cannot be extracted by force; it has to be assembled.

## Picking

Both wines want whole, unbroken fruit, but the decisions diverge. For the early-drinking wine machine harvesting is defensible: it is fast, cheap and lets the winery pick at optimum ripeness across a wide window, and the berry damage it causes releases juice and colour that suits a wine wanted fruity and forward. For the age-worthy wine that damage is disqualifying — oxidation and premature extraction start in the bin — so hand-picking into small crates, cool, with sorting at the winery, is the only route, and it is also the only route that permits whole-bunch work later. The contrast is not quality of fruit but optionality: machine harvest closes doors that hand harvest keeps open.

## Must adjustments

Both may need chaptalisation in a cool Burgundian vintage and acidification in a warm Otago one. The difference is intent. In the early-drinking wine adjustment is used to hit a commercial target — a little sucrose for mid-palate weight, a little tartaric for freshness — and enological tannin at crush is genuinely useful, since a skin-derived proanthocyanidic tannin binds anthocyanin into polymeric pigment and holds the colour of a thinly extracted wine. Tannin is far more widely used across styles than candidates usually credit. In the age-worthy wine adjustments are minimised, not for ideology but because the wine must age on its own phenolic architecture; an added gallotannin gives immediate grip but does not build the long chains that soften over ten years.

## Destemming, whole berry and cold soak

These are three different things and get conflated. Cold soak works because anthocyanins are water-soluble and extract at 5-10C before alcohol appears to dissolve seed tannin: it gives colour and primary aroma with little grip, which is exactly the early-drinking profile, so a three-day soak there is a colour tool. In the age-worthy wine a cold soak is used more sparingly, because the aim is not maximum early colour but colour that has tannin to bind to. Whole-bunch inclusion is a different mechanism again: intact berries ferment intracellularly, generating carbonic aromatics, while the stems contribute their own tannin, absorb some alcohol and heat, and open the cap for gentler extraction. It also has a cost — the stems raise potassium and so nudge pH upwards, and unlignified stems give green pyrazine-like notes. So whole bunch is a structural and aromatic investment for the age-worthy wine, and largely 100% destemming for the early-drinking one, where cleanliness of fruit is the whole point.

## Fermentation and cap management

The early-drinking wine ferments warm-ish and fast on a cultured strain chosen for fruit expression, with pump-overs: efficient, oxygenating, and extracting quickly to a modest total. The age-worthy wine ferments longer and often on ambient yeast, with punch-downs by hand or foot — slower, gentler, more selective for skin than seed phenolics — and often a cold start to stretch the ferment. Pump-over versus punch-down is the clearest contrast in the answer: one maximises extraction per unit time, the other maximises control over which phenolics come out.

## Post-fermentation maceration and pressing

The early-drinking wine is pressed at or just before dryness, and the press fraction goes straight back in for colour and volume. The age-worthy wine sits on skins for a further one to three weeks: colour intensity actually falls as pigment adsorbs onto skins and lees, but polymeric pigment and softer tannin build — a deliberate trade of present appearance for future stability. It is then pressed gently, in fractions, and each fraction assessed before blending.

## Élevage

Eight to ten months in older barrels or larger foudre for the early-drinking wine, an early first racking, fining and filtration to bottle bright and stable. Sixteen to eighteen months, with perhaps 30-50% new oak, extended lees contact, minimal racking and no fining or filtration for the age-worthy wine. Oak here is not flavour but slow oxygen ingress plus ellagitannin as a polymerisation co-factor.

## Conclusion

Every divergence follows one principle: the early-drinking wine is made by extracting quickly and stabilising what you got, the age-worthy wine by extracting slowly and letting oxygen assemble it. Both are interventions; they simply pay off on different timescales.
