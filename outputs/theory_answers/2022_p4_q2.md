## What a wine brand is

A brand is a promise held in the consumer's mind that reduces their risk in choosing. In wine that promise can be owned by a company (Yellow Tail, Kim Crawford), by a place acting as a collective brand (Sancerre, Barolo, Prosecco), or by a retailer (own-label tiers). The distinction matters for this question, because only the first has a manager with the authority to change it. I answer part one across all three types and part two for the company brand, since that is where brand management is actually exercised.

## Measures of strength

There is no single measure, and the first sign of a competent answer is knowing that the right measures depend on what the brand is for. I use three families and then a test.

**Market measures.** Penetration (what proportion of category buyers bought the brand at least once), market share by value and volume, weighted distribution, rate of sale per point of distribution, and average selling price. Penetration is the one I would put first: growth in consumer goods comes overwhelmingly from recruiting light buyers, not from deepening loyalty among heavy ones.

**Consumer measures.** Spontaneous and prompted awareness, consideration, conversion from consideration to purchase, and repeat rate. Awareness without conversion diagnoses a brand that is famous and unwanted — a real and expensive condition.

**Financial measures.** Price premium against the category average, gross margin per case, and price elasticity: how much volume the brand loses when it takes a price rise. That last is the sharpest single test of strength, because it measures whether the consumer is buying the brand or the price. For fine wine the equivalents are secondary-market value and liquidity on Liv-ex, allocation demand and the length of the waiting list.

**The test that ties them together.** The measures must match the brand's purpose and its life-cycle stage. Yellow Tail — launched in the US market in 2001 and now selling of the order of ten million cases a year worldwide — is properly judged on share, penetration, distribution points and average selling price. A first-growth château or a Napa cult wine judged on distribution points would look like a failing brand; its correct measures are secondary-market price, allocation and critical standing. Applying mass-market KPIs to a luxury brand is the commonest analytical error in this area, and it drives genuinely value-destroying decisions.

## What a brand manager actually controls

Part two is the harder part, and the honest answer is that a brand manager influences long-term performance through four levers, of which marketing communication is the least important.

**Price architecture and promotional discipline.** This is the first lever because it does the most long-term damage when mishandled. A large majority of branded wine volume in the UK grocery multiples sells on promotion, and the mechanism is a ratchet: each deeper deal resets the consumer's reference price, so the next promotion must be deeper to move the same volume, and the brand's everyday price becomes fictional. The manager's long-term job is to hold the shelf price and control the depth and frequency of deal even when it costs a quarter's volume — which is precisely the decision an annual bonus discourages.

**Range architecture.** Extensions either recruit new buyers or cannibalise existing ones, and the difference is whether they reach an occasion the core range does not. Yellow Tail's extensions into rosé, sparkling and reserve tiers used the equity to widen occasions while holding one visual identity. 19 Crimes went further, using celebrity association and augmented-reality labels to recruit consumers who were not buying wine at all — an expensive strategy that works for recruitment and is dangerous for longevity, because a brand built on a device must keep finding new devices.

**Supply security.** At any serious scale this is where wine brands actually die. A million-case brand needs contracted fruit at a known cost across multiple vintages and, ideally, more than one region or hemisphere to absorb a short crop. Casella's model of long-term grower contracts is the reason Yellow Tail could grow without the vintage variation that would have broken the consistency promise. Brand managers who cannot influence sourcing are managing a label, not a brand.

**Consistency and renewal.** The liquid must not drift, and the packaging must. Consistency protects the promise; periodic pack and communication refreshes protect relevance. Investment continuity matters more than investment level: advertising and promotion held at a stable percentage of net sales through downturns compounds, while brands cut to protect a bad year rarely recover the awareness cheaply.

## Conclusion

Strength is not one number. It is the set of measures that corresponds to the brand's purpose — penetration, share and price elasticity for a mainstream brand, allocation, secondary-market value and critical standing for a luxury one. Long-term performance is influenced far less by campaigns than by three unglamorous disciplines: refusing the promotional ratchet, extending the range only where it recruits, and securing fruit on contracts longer than the manager's own tenure.
