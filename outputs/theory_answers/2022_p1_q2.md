## Grape quality, and the three ways pests damage it

By grape quality I mean the condition of the fruit at the weighbridge: sugar, acid and pH balance, phenolic and aromatic ripeness, evenness of ripening across the block, and the physical and microbial integrity of the berry. That is a different thing from wine quality, and pests act on it by three distinct routes: they damage the berry directly and open it to spoilage organisms; they vector disease that alters ripening physiology; or they reduce the vine's capacity to ripen a crop at all. I organise the answer by pathway, because the control logic follows the pathway rather than the species.

## Pathway one: loss of vine capacity through root and canopy feeding

**Phylloxera** and **nematodes** attack the root system, so their effect on grape quality is indirect but severe: a debilitated vine has less leaf area per unit of crop, and stalls at low sugar with unripe seed tannin and uneven veraison. Phylloxera thrives on clay soils and is checked by sand, which is why Chile, and pockets of Colares and South Australia, remain ungrafted while Victoria's Yarra and King Valleys sit inside declared phylloxera zones. Root-knot and dagger nematodes are the reverse — a sandy-soil problem in the Australian Riverland and California's Central Valley — and *Xiphinema index* additionally vectors fanleaf virus, so it damages quality twice.

Control here is genetic and pre-plant, and it is decided once. Grafting to *V. berlandieri* and *rupestris* hybrids solves phylloxera; nematode tolerance requires specific choices such as Ramsey, Freedom or Harmony. Beyond that, control is quarantine and hygiene: footwear and machinery disinfestation, restricted vine movement, and area-wide zoning. Chemical nematicides are largely withdrawn, so biofumigant cover crops such as mustard and long fallows are what remain.

## Pathway two: sap-feeders as disease vectors

**Mealybugs and soft scale** spread grapevine leafroll virus, and the quality effect is precisely measurable in the fruit: delayed and incomplete veraison, suppressed anthocyanin, retained malic acid and lower sugar, with the added problem that infected vines look normal. South Africa's Western Cape has made this its defining vineyard-health programme. **Leafhoppers** vector phytoplasmas — *Scaphoideus titanus* carrying flavescence doree through Burgundy, Beaujolais and northern Italy — while sharpshooters carry *Xylella* in California and Texas, killing the vine outright.

Control is a package, not a spray. Roguing and replanting with certified virus-tested material removes the reservoir; monitoring with pheromone and yellow sticky traps sets treatment thresholds; biological control using parasitoid wasps and predatory beetles suppresses mealybug where ant populations are also managed, since ants farm mealybug and must be baited out. Insecticide is targeted at the vulnerable crawler stage, and in the flavescence doree zones it is legally compulsory — a control decision taken away from the grower entirely.

## Pathway three: direct berry damage and taint

This is where the quality loss is most immediate. **European grapevine moth** larvae bore into berries; the wound itself is minor, but it is the entry point for *Botrytis*, *Aspergillus* and sour rot, and for vinegar flies that carry acetic bacteria into the bunch. The result is rotten fruit, volatile acidity and ochratoxin risk in warm regions — a Mediterranean and southern Italian problem, and the reason *Lobesia botrana* is a declared quarantine pest in Chile. **Wasps, birds and mammals** remove or split fruit: starlings and silvereyes in New Zealand, kangaroos and foxes in Australia, wild boar and deer in Europe. **Mites** bronze and defoliate leaves in dusty, hot conditions, cutting ripening capacity. And some pests taint the fruit without damaging it — the multicoloured Asian lady beetle, whose alkaloids taint juice at very low incidence, is a real harvest problem in Ontario and the US Midwest.

Controls differ sharply by pathway. Mating disruption with pheromone dispensers is now the standard *Lobesia* tool in European vineyards, works area-wide rather than vine-by-vine, and is compatible with organic certification; *Bacillus thuringiensis* and spinosad cover the larval stage. Canopy management that opens bunches reduces both moth shelter and the rot that follows. Birds need netting, which is expensive but the only reliable answer on premium blocks; scaring devices habituate. Vertebrates need fencing. Mites are controlled by suppressing dust on tracks and conserving predatory mites — which means avoiding the broad-spectrum insecticides used against something else.

## What I would prioritise

Almost every effective control is either decided before planting or is an area-wide programme, and almost every one interacts with the others: an insecticide applied for leafhopper removes the predators holding mites in check, and a cover crop sown for soil health shelters snails and rodents. The single greatest quality risk in most regions is the berry-damaging insect that opens the door to rot, because it converts a small compositional loss into an unsellable parcel within days of a warm, wet spell. The greatest long-term risk is the vectored virus, because it degrades ripening invisibly for a decade and the fruit never reaches the sugar, colour and tannin ripeness the site is capable of. Monitoring and thresholds rather than calendar spraying, and certified planting material rather than remedial treatment, are what let a grower spend on the right one.
