## Scope

The asset already exists: a planted, trellised, probably irrigated block farmed for tonnage. My task is conversion, not establishment, and the two are different problems. Establishment lets me choose everything; conversion means deciding, block by block, what to keep, what to retrofit and what to pull — under the constraint that the vines are already in the ground and the company needs some cash flow from them meanwhile. A bulk vineyard of this type is the Midi, La Mancha, Lodi and the inland Central Valley, the Riverland and Riverina, Mendoza's eastern departments, the Bekaa flats: high-vigour, wide-spaced, machine-farmed, generously watered, often cordon-trained and mechanically or minimally pruned. Everything below assumes that starting point.

## Year one: audit before action

I would farm the vineyard one full season and change nothing structural, because I cannot convert what I have not measured. The audit records: variety, clone and rootstock by block; virus status, because leafroll and fanleaf cap quality permanently and settle the replant question on their own; vine age, trunk health and missing-vine count; soil pits for depth, texture, salinity, pH and organic matter; the water source, allocation, delivery system and any legal restriction; existing vigour mapped against fruit composition at harvest. Then I would micro-vinify the best two or three blocks separately. Overachieving growers in the Midi built their reputations on identifying the two hillside blocks in an estate of flat, irrigated Carignan and treating them differently — that is the model, and it is the honest answer to "is this site actually capable of high quality?"

The audit produces a three-way split: blocks convertible in place, blocks to graft over, and blocks to replant. Converting everything at once is neither affordable nor wise.

## Reducing vigour: water, floor and nutrition

A bulk vineyard's defining fault is excess vigour, and water is the fastest lever. I would move from flood or full-cover sprinkler to drip, install soil moisture probes, and shift to a deficit schedule — a mild, controlled stress from fruit set through veraison, relieved before shutdown. Where irrigation is by allocation this also saves water the company can trade or bank. I would stop or sharply reduce nitrogen fertigation, and replace clean cultivation with a permanent cover crop between rows to compete for water and nitrogen, build organic matter and carry machinery in a wet spring. On the vine row I would mulch rather than cultivate. These changes are cheap, reversible and act within one or two seasons.

## Canopy and pruning

Most bulk blocks are on a single cordon wire with a sprawling, shaded canopy. Retrofitting foliage wires to give a properly positioned VSP, or dividing the canopy on a Scott Henry or Smart-Dyson where vigour is genuinely high, converts shade into exposed, well-aerated fruiting zone — the single largest quality gain available without moving a vine. Raising the fruiting wire lifts fruit out of the frost layer and away from radiated soil heat.

Pruning is a multi-year conversion, not a switch. Machine-pruned or spur-pruned high-count cordons carry far too many buds. I would step bud numbers down over two or three winters — cutting straight to a low count invites vigorous water-shoot regrowth and does nothing for balance — and where the variety fruits poorly on basal buds, retrain to cane pruning by laying down replacement canes and progressively removing the old cordon arms. Shoot thinning in spring and a light bunch thin at veraison finish the job. I would also fill missing vines, since gaps concentrate vigour into neighbours.

## Density and row alignment

These are the expensive ones and I would not assume them. Wide-spaced rows at three metres or more mean low density and high vigour per vine, but rows can be interplanted — adding a vine between existing vines within the row raises density without touching the trellis or the tractor width. Full row realignment, or narrowing row spacing, requires a replant and is only justified where the existing orientation is genuinely damaging: rows running so that the fruiting zone takes full afternoon sun on the western face in a hot region, or rows aligned across a prevailing wind that shreds shoots and stalls set. In windy blocks, orientation and density decisions should be made for wind protection first.

## Grafting and replanting, in that order

Where the variety is wrong for the site but the trunk, rootstock and root system are sound, field grafting is the answer: a new variety in production within two seasons, at a fraction of replant cost and with a mature root system already established. Replanting is reserved for virused blocks, an unsuitable rootstock — for salinity, lime or drought — dead and missing vines beyond economic repair, or where density and alignment must change. I would phase it over several years so the company retains fruit and income.

## Conclusion

Conversion is a sequence: measure, then de-vigorate through water and floor management, then restructure the canopy and step down the pruning load, then graft, and only then replant what genuinely cannot be saved. Replanting the whole property is the expensive way to avoid the harder judgement about which blocks were ever capable of high quality.
