## Definition and scope

By **intermediary** I mean any party standing between the producer and the consumer and taking title, commission or margin for doing so: agents, brokers (courtiers), importers, wholesalers, distributors, sole traders and, at the end of the chain, retailers and the on-trade. My test for justification is functional: an intermediary is justified where it performs a function the producer cannot perform as cheaply or as well itself. I assess that function by function, test it against six markets, and then look at what is eroding it.

## What intermediaries are actually paid to do

Six functions, and they are separable.

**Regulatory access.** In several markets the intermediary is compulsory. Under the US three-tier system, in place since repeal in 1933, a supplier may not sell to a retailer; a licensed distributor must sit in between, and in franchise-law states terminating one is close to impossible.

**Logistics and working capital.** Consolidating pallets, bonded storage, duty deferment, temperature control and last-mile delivery to thousands of small on-trade accounts. Just as important, the intermediary buys the stock and carries the receivable — for a small producer, cash on shipment rather than cash on sale is the difference between solvency and not.

**Credit and risk.** The intermediary absorbs the risk of a restaurant failing, and often insures it.

**Assortment.** No retailer wants 400 supplier relationships; the intermediary aggregates a portfolio and gives the buyer one negotiation, one invoice and one delivery.

**Selling and brand-building.** A field sales force calling on independents and sommeliers is a fixed cost only viable across a portfolio.

**Price discovery and market-making.** The clearest case is la place de Bordeaux, where the courtier takes around 2% and the negociant roughly 10 to 15% for finding the clearing price of a wine sold before it exists and distributing it worldwide without the chateau employing a single salesperson.

## Does the justification hold, market by market?

In the **USA** it holds legally but is under strain economically: consolidation has produced very large distributors, Southern Glazer's Wine & Spirits, formed by merger in 2016, foremost among them, and a small brand inside a portfolio of thousands can buy access without buying attention.

In the **UK** the justification has already partly collapsed. The multiple grocers buy direct from producers and contract logistics separately, unbundling the intermediary's functions and paying for only the one they need; the agency houses survive by serving the on-trade and independents, where the selling function is genuinely expensive.

In the **Nordic monopolies** — Systembolaget in Sweden, Vinmonopolet in Norway and Alko in Finland — the state is simultaneously the wholesaler and the retailer, and listings are won by blind tender. The intermediary here is unavoidable but transparent, and a producer's marketing spend counts for very little.

In **China** the importer-distributor layer is fragmented and provincial, and its justification is real but narrow: language, licensing, label registration, customs and access to a highly regional trade. Its weakness is that it often provides no brand-building at all.

In **Australia**, two retail groups buy on such a scale, and own so much private label, that the wholesale layer is thin and the retailer has become the intermediary.

## How it is changing, and why

The direction of travel is **unbundling**. The functions above were historically sold as one bundle at one margin; digital infrastructure has priced them separately. Third-party logistics, bonded warehousing, credit insurance and payment processing can all now be bought individually, so a producer keeps the margin attached to the functions it does not need.

**Direct-to-consumer** is the most visible expression. In the USA, winery shipping opened up substantially after the Supreme Court's 2005 Granholm decision, and cellar door plus club now delivers full retail price to Napa and Sonoma producers rather than an ex-cellar price. In luxury, LVMH launched Clos 19 in 2017 as its own consumer-facing platform, and Chateau Margaux opening its own wine bar is the same instinct: owning the consumer relationship, and the data.

**Marketplaces and drop-ship** cut the chain differently — Tmall and JD in China, Amazon in Europe — with the producer holding the stock and the platform providing only demand. Meanwhile **new intermediaries** have appeared where the old ones added information: Vivino and Wine-Searcher have taken over recommendation and price discovery, and Naked Wines finances production directly from consumer subscriptions.

## Conclusion

Intermediaries can be justified only where they perform a function, and the general movement is towards them becoming less relevant in economic terms as those functions are unbundled and bought individually. What survives is not the intermediary as a category but the specific jobs that resist digitisation: compulsory regulatory access in the USA, physical distribution to a fragmented on-trade everywhere, credit for producers who cannot carry it, and the price-making machinery of la place. Where the intermediary's contribution was information or promotion, the producer and the platform have taken it back, and no amount of incumbency will return it.
