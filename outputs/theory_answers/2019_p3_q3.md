## Definitions and scope

**Quality assurance** is the preventive half: the written specifications, procedures, supplier approvals, training and audits that build conformity into the process and give confidence it will be met. **Quality control** is the verifying half: the sampling, inspection and testing that prove a given delivery or a given production run conforms, and the decision to accept, hold or reject it. QA designs the system; QC measures the output of it.

**Dry goods** means everything the winery buys that is not wine. Two families. Packaging: bottles, casks, kegs, bag-in-box bladders and taps, closures of every type, capsules, labels, cartons, dividers, tape, shrink wrap and pallets. And winemaking inputs: yeast and nutrients, enzymes, tartaric acid, SO2, fining agents, filter sheets and membranes, gases and oak in all its forms.

One exclusion. Negotiating price, minimum order quantities and lead times is procurement's work and I leave it there. Supply enters this answer only where it touches quality — most obviously when a shortage tempts someone to substitute an unapproved item.

The animating principle throughout is that wine is a food product and consumer safety is paramount. A physical, chemical or allergen failure in dry goods reaches the consumer directly, and glass is the one hazard that can injure.

## The written system

Everything below is documented, version-controlled and owned by a named person, so that winemaking, packaging, warehouse, technical and commercial departments all work from the same procedure rather than from custom.

**A specification per item.** For each dry good: an approved technical drawing with dimensions and tolerances, material composition, a food-contact declaration of compliance with migration data, an agreed defect classification with acceptable quality limits, and the brand requirements — artwork version, signed colour master, legal text for each destination market, glass colour and weight. Dry goods must match brand specifications as strictly as they match technical ones; a label that is legally correct but off-colour is still a rejection.

**An approved supplier list**, maintained against evidence: current certification (BRCGS Packaging Materials certification for packaging suppliers is the usual demand), a completed technical questionnaire, and an annual supplier audit either performed by us or accepted from a recognised scheme. No item is ordered against an unapproved supplier, and any emergency substitution requires technical sign-off, not a warehouse decision.

## Receipt

Every delivery arrives with a certificate of conformity or analysis referencing the specification and the batch. Goods are booked into quarantine, given a unique internal lot code, and released only after inspection against a documented sampling plan — an ISO 2859 style attribute plan, with sample size and accept/reject numbers set by the criticality of the item. Bottles are checked for dimensions, annealing, and visible faults; closures for dimensions, moisture, coatings and, for cork, a soak test against TCA on a sample basis; labels for artwork version, legal text, print quality and adhesive; cartons for board grade and print. Non-conforming deliveries go to a hold area under a non-conformance report, with a corrective and preventive action logged against the supplier and reviewed at the annual audit.

## Storage

Storage is where good goods become bad ones. Dry goods are stored off the floor on clean pallets, in sealed original packaging, in a dry, temperature- and humidity-controlled area, under a documented pest control programme with bait stations mapped and inspected. Segregation is critical: closures and cork must never be stored near cleaning chemicals, fuel or anything chlorinated, because chlorophenols are the precursor of chloroanisole taint. Stock rotates first-in-first-out with expiry dates observed for yeast, enzymes and fining agents. Obsolete artwork is destroyed against a signed record — retained superseded labels are one of the commonest routes to a mislabelling recall.

## Use on the line

A written line clearance precedes every run: previous dry goods removed and reconciled, new lot codes verified against the works order by two people, and a first-off pack signed against the specification. In-process QC then samples at set frequencies through the run — fill height, closure application and torque on screwcaps checked at a set frequency, cork insertion depth, capsule and label placement, carton and pallet configuration, and date and lot coding legibility. Retained samples are kept from every batch. At the end of the run, dry goods issued are reconciled against bottles produced and waste recorded; an unexplained variance on labels or closures is investigated before release.

## HACCP, flow diagrams and audit readiness

The system sits on a HACCP plan built from a flow diagram running goods-in through storage, line, warehouse and despatch, with hazards analysed at each step: physical (glass, foreign body, cork fragments), chemical (migration from packaging, cleaning residue, taint), and allergen and label integrity, since a missing sulphite or egg-fining declaration is a food-safety failure carried by a dry good. Critical control points typically include bottle rinsing and inversion, the glass-breakage procedure, and label verification. Above the plan sit internal audits on a schedule, a management review, a training matrix, and records adequate for an annual external audit — British Retail Consortium certification being the usual retailer requirement — including a traceability exercise that can reconstruct the full chain within four hours, from a bottle on shelf back to the pallet of glass and forward to every customer who received it.
