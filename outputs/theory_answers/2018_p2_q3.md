## Lysozyme

**What it is.** An enzyme extracted from hen egg white that hydrolyses the peptidoglycan of Gram-positive bacterial cell walls, lysing the cell.

**What it does.** It is antibacterial only, and specific: it kills lactic acid bacteria — *Oenococcus*, *Lactobacillus*, *Pediococcus* — and has no effect on yeast, including *Brettanomyces*, or on acetic acid bacteria, which are Gram-negative. Its two working uses are to delay or suppress malolactic fermentation, for instance in an aromatic white where malic acidity is wanted, and to control lactic bacteria in a high-pH must or a stuck ferment where SO2 is ineffective because so little of it is in the molecular form.

**Dose and limits.** Permitted in the EU and by the OIV to a maximum of 500 mg/L in must and wine combined. Working rates are 100-500 mg/L against a developing bacterial population, lower for prophylaxis.

**Effects and limitations.** It remains active as pH rises, which is its advantage over SO2. But it is a protein: it can precipitate with tannin, stripping colour and clarity in reds, and it can leave the wine protein-unstable, requiring bentonite afterwards. It is an allergen requiring declaration on the label, is neither vegan nor acceptable under some organic schemes, and is expensive.

## CMC — carboxymethyl cellulose

**What it is.** A cellulose derivative, a soluble anionic polysaccharide, used as a tartrate stabiliser. E466.

**What it does.** It does not remove potassium or tartaric acid. It is a crystallisation inhibitor: the polymer adsorbs onto the surface of nucleating potassium bitartrate crystals and blocks their growth, so a supersaturated wine stays clear. The commercial argument for it is energy — it substitutes for cold stabilisation at around -4°C for several days, which is one of the largest electricity costs in a winery.

**Dose and limits.** E466, permitted in the EU to a maximum of 100 mg/L. Typical dose is up to 100 mg/L, added in-line at least two to five days before bottling and always after fining but before final filtration, since it raises filterability index.

**Effects and limitations.** Effective and sensorially neutral in whites, rosés and sparkling wines. It is not suitable for reds: it destabilises colloidal colour and can throw a pigment deposit. The wine must already be protein-stable and filtered, or CMC will blind the filters. It does not prevent calcium tartrate instability.

## Copper sulphate

**What it is.** CuSO4, added as a solution to remove reductive sulphur compounds.

**What it does.** Cu2+ reacts with hydrogen sulphide and with free thiols such as ethanethiol to form insoluble copper sulphides, which are then removed by settling or filtration. It treats rotten-egg and struck-flint reduction; it does not remove disulphides, which must first be reduced back to thiols, and it does not fix mercaptan-derived heaviness permanently.

**Dose and limits.** The OIV and EU cap treatment at 1 g/hL of copper sulphate, with residual copper in the finished wine not to exceed 1 mg/L. Effective doses are small, of the order of 0.1-0.6 mg/L of copper, and should always be established by bench trial on graded additions.

**Effects and limitations.** It is a blunt tool. Copper also binds desirable volatile thiols, so overdosing a Sauvignon Blanc strips passionfruit and box aromas along with the fault. Residual copper above roughly 0.5 mg/L is a pro-oxidant in bottle and can throw a copper casse haze, so the wine must be filtered off the precipitate and the residue verified analytically before bottling.

## YAN — yeast assimilable nitrogen

**What it is.** The fraction of must nitrogen that *Saccharomyces* can actually use: ammonium ions plus free alpha-amino nitrogen, excluding proline, which yeast cannot assimilate anaerobically. Reported as mg N/L.

**What it does.** It determines yeast biomass and fermentation rate. A must fermenting to 12-13% abv needs of the order of 150 mg N/L, and the requirement scales with sugar — roughly 25-30 mg N/L for every 1% of potential alcohol, so a high-Brix must needs proportionally more.

**Dose and limits.** Deficiency is corrected with diammonium phosphate or with a complex organic nutrient, added in split doses at inoculation and around one third of sugar depletion, when uptake is most efficient. DAP addition is capped at 100 g/hL in the EU, and thiamine at 0.6 mg/L.

**Effects and limitations.** Below about 150 mg N/L, ferments run sluggish or stick and yeast produce hydrogen sulphide as a stress response. Above roughly 250-300 mg N/L, nitrogen is left in the finished wine as a microbial substrate, increasing spoilage risk, and urea from excess DAP is a precursor to ethyl carbamate. Organic nutrients also supply sterols and vitamins that DAP does not, which matters in a clarified white juice with low solids.
