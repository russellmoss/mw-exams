## What stability means here, and the chemistry that decides it

The instability at issue is the precipitation of potassium bitartrate, and secondarily calcium tartrate, as visible crystals in the bottle. Cold stabilisation is the family of treatments that prevents that; it is not a synonym for chill filtering, which is only the step that removes crystals already thrown. Tartaric acid, whose two dissociation constants sit near pH 3.0 and pH 4.4, exists in wine largely as the bitartrate anion, which pairs with potassium taken up from the vine. Solubility falls as temperature falls and as alcohol rises, so a wine that was saturated in the tank is supersaturated once fermentation is complete and again once it meets a cold lorry. Crystallisation is a nucleation process, though, so a supersaturated wine can sit clear for months and then precipitate suddenly — which is why judgement by eye is worthless and why the question of testing is the important half.

## Why red is a different decision from white or rosé

Reds are less often treated, and not because they are chemically safer. At pH 3.5-3.8, where reds normally sit, the proportion of tartaric acid present as bitartrate is near its maximum, so the driving force for precipitation is if anything higher than in a low-pH white. What protects red wine is colloidal: anthocyanins, condensed tannins, mannoproteins and polysaccharides adsorb onto growing crystal faces and inhibit nucleation and growth, so reds behave as natural inhibitor systems. Practice reinforces chemistry — a red held in barrel or cask through a cool cellar winter has effectively been cold stabilised over months at no cost, whereas an early-bottled white or rosé never gets that time.

Then there is consequence. A crystal deposit in a red is one deposit among several the consumer already expects, and the wine is not usually served chilled or shown in clear glass. Crystals in a pale Provence-style rosé in flint glass, poured cold, look like broken glass and generate returns. That is why supermarket specifications routinely demand stability certificates for whites and rosés: the requirement exists because of cold-warehouse transit, chiller display and the cost of handling complaints, not as an arbitrary rule.

## Deciding: risk against what the treatment costs the wine

The decision is a measured risk weighed against a real quality cost. On risk: the wine's potassium, calcium, pH, alcohol and colloid load, and above all its saturation temperature; and its route to market — a wine shipped in a heated container to a warm market and drunk within the year is a different case from one crossing the North Atlantic in January into a Scandinavian monopoly warehouse.

On cost, the methods are not equivalent. Conventional refrigeration at around minus 4 degrees C for one to two weeks is effective but strips aroma and, with the filtration that follows, colloids and mid-palate texture; it also carries the largest energy bill in the winery, which is now a carbon as well as a cash consideration. Contact treatment with seeded potassium bitartrate at 0 degrees C achieves the same end in hours and is gentler because the wine is cold for far less time. Electrodialysis removes ions selectively with less sensory damage but needs capital. Cation exchange, which replaces potassium with hydrogen and lowers pH, alters the wine's acid balance and the efficacy of its sulphur dioxide, so it is a compositional change, not a clean-up. Among additives, metatartaric acid is the cheapest option but hydrolyses at ambient temperature and loses effect within about twelve to eighteen months, so it is disqualified for anything intended to age; carboxymethylcellulose is effective and gentle in whites and sparkling wines but is not recommended for reds because it can destabilise colour; potassium polyaspartate, which is usable in reds, has partly closed that gap. Organic and other certifications constrain the additive routes, which can force a producer back onto refrigeration.

## Testing that the wine is stable

Three tests, in increasing usefulness. The freeze test — a sample held at around minus 4 degrees C for several days, or frozen and thawed, then inspected — is cheap, slow and a poor discriminator, since it tests conditions harsher than the wine will meet.

The saturation temperature test measures, by conductivity, the temperature at which the wine is saturated with potassium bitartrate. It is diagnostic rather than pass/fail: it tells me how far from stability the wine is, and therefore which treatment is proportionate.

The mini-contact conductivity test is the working standard. That is done by the contact process, chilled to 0 degrees C (5 degrees C for reds) and seeded with roughly 1 g/L of finely ground potassium bitartrate, or by electrodialysis at volume, or by carboxymethylcellulose, which is cheap and effective in whites but must be added after protein fining because it interacts with bentonite. It is quick enough to gate a bottling run.

Calcium needs separate handling: calcium tartrate is far less temperature-dependent, so cold treatment does not remove it and no rapid test predicts it well. Measure calcium — above roughly 80 mg/L, typically after bentonite or calcium-containing filter media, the risk is real — and allow time before bottling.

## Conclusion

Measure first, then treat as little as the route to market allows: for most reds no tartrate treatment at all, for whites and rosés the gentlest method that the saturation temperature and mini-contact test say will hold, verified again on bottled samples.
