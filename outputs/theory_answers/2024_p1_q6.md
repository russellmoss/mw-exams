## What "better" has to mean

Both states are faults, so better cannot mean good. I define better as the fault I would rather be presented with on taking over a vineyard, judged on four things: how far it moves the vine away from the balance the intended wine needs; the yield and fruit quality lost while it persists; whether the wine style and price point can absorb that loss; and above all how cheaply, quickly and reliably it can be corrected. Reversibility is the decisive criterion, because a deficiency corrected within a season costs one season, while an excess that takes a decade to draw down costs a decade of wine. My position is that the statement is right for the nutrients that most shape wine quality, and wrong as a general law.

## Starting in the soil

The question is about soil, and soil nutrient status is two different things: the reserve present, and the fraction the vine can take up. Availability is governed by pH, cation exchange capacity, organic matter, microbial activity and water. A calcareous soil may hold ample iron and still give chlorotic, weak vines because at high pH the iron is unavailable; the answer there is lime-tolerant rootstock at planting or foliar chelate in season, not iron fertiliser. Conversely a light sandy soil with low exchange capacity holds little and leaches fast, so nutrition must be delivered in small, timed doses. Excess of one cation also suppresses another — heavy potassium depresses magnesium uptake — so "too many" is rarely a simple surplus.

## Nitrogen

Nitrogen is where the statement is strongest. Excess gives vigour: long shoots, dense shading canopies, poor fruit exposure, delayed and uneven ripening, thin colour, botrytis pressure in any humid region, dilute fruit and nitrate leaching. Deficiency gives a weak canopy, low yields and low must nitrogen, which in the winery means sluggish or stuck fermentations and reductive sulphides.

The two are not equally tractable. Nitrogen can be added cheaply and precisely — compost, a legume cover crop, fertigation, a foliar application near veraison that lifts must nitrogen within weeks — and if the grower still gets it wrong, a nutrient addition in the fermenter closes the gap for a few euros a tonne. Removing nitrogen is slow: a permanent grass sward, no-till competition, higher retained crop and canopy division will bring a vigorous site back into balance over several seasons, not one. Verdict: too little is clearly better.

## Potassium

Excess potassium raises must pH, which costs colour stability, microbial security and freshness, and forces tartaric acidification with all its cost and its flattening effect. It builds up on exchange sites, the vine takes up more than it needs, and there is no practical way to strip it out — the grower can only stop adding, use competitive cover crops and, at replanting, choose a rootstock that takes up less. Deficiency causes marginal leaf scorch, poor sugar transport and uneven ripening, and is corrected in one or two seasons with soil or foliar potassium. Verdict: too little is better again, and by a wider margin than nitrogen, because the excess is effectively permanent.

## Micronutrients, where the statement fails

Boron, zinc and magnesium invert the argument's premise even if not always its conclusion. Their deficiencies bite immediately and expensively: boron and zinc deficiency disrupt flowering and fruit set, producing millerandage and direct crop loss; magnesium deficiency is implicated in bunch stem necrosis, which shrivels fruit before harvest. These are one-season, cheap foliar corrections, so on ease-of-correction grounds deficiency remains preferable — but the yield lost in the year of diagnosis can be severe, whereas mild excess of these elements often does nothing at all.

Copper is the outright counter-example. Decades of copper-based fungicide have accumulated in the topsoil of many long-established vineyards, particularly organic ones, where it is toxic to soil biology and root growth and cannot practically be removed. Here too much is unarguably worse.

## Where too many is the better problem

Situation changes the verdict. For high-volume wine in the Riverland, La Mancha or the Central Valley, the business model needs tonnage at low cost; a well-supplied fertile soil delivers it, and the dilution that follows is acceptable in a category whose quality claim is clean, consistent varietal fruit. Young vines establishing, and old devigorated vineyards being restored, also want generosity rather than restraint. And a vine with adequate reserves is more resilient through drought and heat stress. Against that, a cool humid region making structured reds cannot afford excess nitrogen at all: the rot risk alone can cost the vintage.

## Correction: the asymmetry that decides it

The practical toolkit is diagnostic first — soil analysis before planting, petiole or blade analysis at flowering and veraison, read alongside vigour and canopy observation. In-season deficiency is then corrected by foliar spray or fertigation within weeks. Structural correction runs the other way and is slow: cover crops and sward competition to remove vigour, mulch and compost to build exchange capacity, lime or acidifying amendment to shift availability rather than quantity, and rootstock choice, which is available only at replanting. Adding is a decision; subtracting is a project.

## Conclusion

On balance I agree with the statement, for a specific reason rather than the intuitive one. It is not that lean soils make better wine — that is a half-truth that fails wherever yield pays the bills. It is that deficiency is diagnosable, cheap, fast and precisely correctable, while excess of the nutrients that most damage wine quality, nitrogen and above all potassium, is slow or impossible to reverse and pushes the vine away from balance in the direction that costs colour, freshness and health. The statement fails for micronutrients, for copper, and for high-volume production where fertility is the asset. The defensible formulation is that it is better to have too few nutrients than too many wherever the excess cannot be reversed within a growing season.
