## Scope

A closure has five technical jobs: to seal the bottle, to come out again without a struggle, to admit enough oxygen for the wine to mature as intended, to admit no more than that, and to add nothing of its own. Every technical factor is a consequence of one of those five, qualified by wine style, intended shelf life and intended ageing. Marketing and consumer image influence closure choice in practice but are not technical factors and I leave them aside. Availability of bottling equipment is technical, and I take it at the end.

## Sealing performance

The seal is a mechanical fit. A standard bottle has an internal bore of about 18.5 mm, and a natural cork of 24 mm diameter is compressed into it, the seal maintained by the elastic recovery of the cork's cell structure. That recovery is what fails: a cork that dries out, or one cut from poor bark with cracks or lenticels running its length, leaks. Cork therefore imposes storage requirements — horizontal or otherwise humid, and away from temperature cycling, since a bottle warmed and cooled pumps liquid past the closure.

A screwcap seals differently, compressing a liner against the flat glass lip, so it is indifferent to orientation and humidity but sensitive to glass finish and capping torque. It also requires an intact skirt: a knocked cap in transit breaks the seal invisibly.

The pressure the seal must hold sets the option list absolutely. Extraction force is a specification, not an accident: cork is specified in the region of 12-45 daN depending on closure length and standard, and a cork above that range is a consumer problem and a restaurant one. Too little and the seal is inadequate. A screwcap requires no tool at all, which matters for on-trade speed, for airline and outdoor consumption, and for an ageing consumer population; it also reseals, which cork does not. Agglomerate and synthetic closures sit between, with synthetics historically the hardest to extract and the worst to reinsert.

## Oxygen transmission rate: the decisive variable

OTR determines the wine's whole trajectory in bottle and is the factor most often chosen badly. A tin-Saran lined screwcap admits very little; natural cork admits roughly 0.5 to more than 4 mg a year, with the spread between individual corks from the same plank the defining problem; a technical micro-agglomerate cork is manufactured to a declared OTR class; and a co-extruded synthetic sits at the top of the range.

Too little ingress is a fault in its own right. A wine bottled with residual copper, low free SO2 or a reductive lees history, sealed under a very low-OTR screwcap, will develop sulphidic reduction in bottle. The remedies are technical and quantifiable: manage copper before bottling, set a reference dose of free SO2 at bottling of the order of 25-35 mg/L for a white, and specify a higher-OTR liner where the wine's chemistry demands it.

Too much ingress oxidises. A synthetic closure on a delicate aromatic white will show flat, oxidised fruit within a year, which is why synthetics belong on wines with a turnover measured in months. Natural cork's problem is different and more insidious: its OTR varies substantially from cork to cork within a single batch, so a case of the same wine develops at different rates. Technical corks exist precisely to remove that variance, and the ability to specify OTR at purchase is the strongest technical argument for them.

## Taint and scalping

The closure must contribute nothing. 2,4,6-trichloroanisole is the principal risk with cork: TCA is detectable at roughly 1.5-4 ng/L in wine and lower in a delicate white, and it suppresses fruit well below the level at which it is recognised as a fault. Modern practice screens for it — steam or supercritical CO2 extraction of the granulate, and gas chromatography on individual corks in the premium grades — and incidence has fallen from the several per cent widely reported in the 1990s to well under 1%. A producer specifying natural cork should be buying to a stated releasable-TCA limit and running soak tests on delivery.

Synthetics carry the opposite problem: aroma scalping, the absorption of the wine's own volatile compounds into the polymer, which strips aromatic intensity over months. Screwcap liners are effectively inert but must be verified as food-contact compliant.

## Matching the closure to style, shelf life and intended ageing

The four cases follow. An aromatic white for consumption within two years: a low-OTR screwcap, to hold thiols and esters, with SO2 and copper managed to avoid reduction. A commercial red at high volume and fast turnover: an agglomerate or technical cork, or a screwcap, chosen on unit cost and consistency. A structured red intended for fifteen or twenty years: a long, high-grade natural cork or a specified-OTR technical cork, giving the slow ingress that continues tannin polymerisation. Traditional-method sparkling: crown cap for the lees-ageing period, cork and wirehood at disgorgement.

## Availability of specialist bottling equipment

Finally, the closure is only usable if the line can apply it. Cork and screwcap need different glass — a cork finish against a BVS 30 x 60 threaded finish — so the choice is made when the bottles are ordered, not at filling. Cork needs a compression corker with jaws and an inert-gas jetting stage; screwcap needs a capping head with controlled torque and rollers; sparkling needs a wire-hooder. A small producer without a screwcapping head must use a mobile line or a contract bottler, and that availability, on the required date and volume, is often what actually decides the question.
