## Scope: what bottling quality control is, and why it is the last chance

Quality control is the testing and inspection that verifies a batch conforms to specification; quality assurance is the system that makes conformity repeatable. At bottling the two converge, because bottling is the last point at which anything can be changed. Once the closure is in, a defect is no longer a correction but a recall, unsaleable stock, a disappointed consumer and lasting brand damage. That is the logic behind every check below: each is justified by the cost of the failure it prevents. Wine is a foodstuff, so the frame is a HACCP plan sitting inside a quality assurance system — ISO 9001 or ISO 22000 for most commercial producers — and inside EU wine and food law. I cover the wine, the dry goods, the packaging operation, compliance with specification, and staff safety.

## Checks on the wine

**Microbiological status.** Plating or membrane counts for yeast, *Brettanomyces* and lactic acid bacteria, with residual sugar and malic acid measured alongside. Necessary because fermentable sugar plus a viable population means refermentation in bottle — gas, haze, pushed corks — and residual malic acid means a possible bottle malolactic. The result sets the filtration specification: a 0.45 micron membrane retains yeast and most spoilage bacteria, with a 0.65 micron prefilter to protect it, and integrity is confirmed by a bubble-point test before and after the run. Without that test a torn cartridge is invisible until the market finds it.

**Free and total sulphur dioxide, read against pH.** Necessary because protection depends on molecular SO2, not on the free figure alone: a target of roughly 0.5 to 0.6 mg/L molecular for a dry red and 0.8 mg/L where sugar is present requires about 25 to 30 mg/L free at pH 3.5, and considerably more at pH 3.8. Total SO2 is checked at the same time against the legal ceiling — 150 mg/L for dry red and 200 mg/L for dry white and rose in the EU, rising to 400 mg/L for the sweetest categories — because the addition that protects the wine must not make it unsaleable.

**Dissolved and total package oxygen.** Measured in tank, at the filler and on filled bottles off the line. Necessary because line pick-up is the largest single determinant of early shelf life; a reading above about 1 mg/L total package oxygen in an aromatic white stops the line and sends the operator to the filler-bowl inerting and the vacuum-jetting step.

**Physical stability.** Protein by heat test, tartrates by conductivity or cold test, turbidity, and iron and copper for casse risk. Necessary because a haze or a crystal in a bright wine reads to the consumer as a fault even where it is harmless — and because every remedy has a lead time. This is why the analysis is booked days before the fill, not on the morning of it.

**Sensory.** A trained panel tastes the tank before filling and a bottle off the line. Necessary because no instrument detects a fault nobody thought to assay for.

## Checks on dry goods

Bottles are inspected dimensionally, for internal cleanliness and for glass defects. Closures are checked for oxygen transmission rate, extraction or torque force and, for cork, releasable TCA on a soak test — necessary because cork taint is perceptible at only a few nanograms per litre, so one bad lot compromises an entire run. Labels, capsules and cartons are verified against approved artwork, barcode and the destination market's mandatory text. Necessary because a wrong barcode blocks a retailer's delivery as effectively as a faulty wine does.

## Checks on the packaging operation

Fill height, closure torque or insertion depth, headspace volume and headspace oxygen, seal integrity, label placement, batch coding and traceability marks, sampled at a defined frequency and recorded. Necessary because these are continuous processes that drift: fill height governs legal net content and headspace oxygen, and low torque leaks. Alcohol must sit within the EU label tolerance - 0.5% abv for most still wines, but 0.8% abv for sparkling, liqueur and overripe-grape wines - or the label is wrong. Volatile acidity is checked against the legal ceiling, of the order of 1.2 g/L as acetic acid, and against the buyer's tighter internal limit. Sulphites must be declared once total SO2 exceeds 10 mg/L; egg, milk and fish fining residues are verified so the allergen declaration is accurate; ochratoxin A is screened against the EU limit of 2 micrograms per kilogram. Retail customers impose their own analytical specification, and a delivery outside it is rejected at the depot at the supplier's cost.

## HACCP, and the safety of the people running the line

HACCP identifies the critical control points — the final filter, the filler, the closure — each with a limit, a monitoring method, a corrective action and a record; everything else sits in prerequisite programmes such as CIP verification and glass-breakage policy. Finally, the operators. Bottling is the winery's most hazardous operation: glass breakage causes cuts and eye injuries, line noise requires hearing protection, palletising causes lifting injuries, CIP chemicals and SO2 are burn and respiratory hazards, and tank entry kills through carbon dioxide asphyxiation. A manager who signs off the wine but not the line has passed the cheaper check and failed the more important one.
