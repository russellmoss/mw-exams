## What "importance" means here

Ethanol is the principal product of fermentation and, after water, the largest single component of most wines by volume. I read "importance" as a counterfactual: what would change if alcohol were absent, or materially different? That question cannot be answered on one axis, so I take four — what alcohol does in the glass, what it does to wine as a physical product over time, what it does to wine's legal and fiscal identity, and what it does to wine's standing in society. My argument is that alcohol is not one component among many but the thing that simultaneously creates the category, preserves it, defines it in law and now threatens it.

## In the glass: structure, balance and style

Ethanol is not a neutral solvent. It tastes faintly sweet at moderate concentration, contributes viscosity and weight, and produces a warming or, in excess, burning tactile sensation. But its importance is relational: almost every effect it has depends on what else is present.

Because it reads as sweetness, alcohol balances acidity and tannin. A Mosel Riesling at low strength needs residual sugar to make its acidity palatable; a Barossa Shiraz carries firm tannin partly on alcoholic sweetness that a Loire Cabernet Franc at two degrees less simply does not have. Alcohol is also the medium in which aroma compounds are dissolved and from which they volatilise, so strength alters aromatic expression — raising it lifts some compounds to the nose and suppresses others, which is one reason de-alcoholised wine so often smells hollow rather than merely lighter. During fermentation the rising alcohol curve governs extraction, anthocyanins coming out early and tannins later, so the winemaker's maceration decisions are in practice decisions about alcohol. And alcohol shapes how a wine behaves with food, where it interacts with heat, salt and fat.

The operative concept is balance, not level. Moscato d'Asti at around 5.5%, Sancerre at 12.5%, Napa Cabernet at 14.5% and Pedro Ximénez at 17% can each be in balance on their own terms. That is why I regard the consumer habit of reading the label figure as a proxy for quality or heaviness as a genuine problem: it treats a relational property as an absolute one, and it drives producers towards declared numbers rather than balanced wines.

## Stability, preservation and longevity

Here alcohol is not merely important but constitutive. Ethanol is an antimicrobial. Above roughly 15% abv most spoilage yeasts and bacteria cannot function, which is the entire logic of fortification: Port arrested at roughly 19 to 20% abv, sherry fortified to between about 15 and 18%, Madeira built to survive the tropics. Those styles exist because alcohol solved the preservation problem before refrigeration, sterile filtration and inert gas existed, and they were consequently the wines that could be shipped, aged and traded internationally. Historically, alcohol is the technology that made wine a global commodity rather than a local drink.

At the other end, a wine below about 11% is fragile and leans far harder on sulphur dioxide, acidity, low pH and immaculate hygiene. Sweet wines make the same point from the opposite direction: a botrytised Sauternes or Trockenbeerenauslese is stable at moderate strength only because very high residual sugar suppresses microbial activity, whereas an off-dry wine with neither high sugar nor high alcohol is among the most vulnerable products in any cellar and depends on sterile bottling to survive. Alcohol also participates in the slow esterification and condensation reactions of maturation and holds the phenolic and volatile matrix in solution, so it contributes to how a wine ages as well as whether it survives.

The no- and low-alcohol category is the proof of this by subtraction. Remove the ethanol and the producer must replace what it was doing — higher sulphur, sterile bottling, added sugar or CO2 to restore body — and still ends up with a shorter shelf life. We are discovering what alcohol does by taking it out.

## Legal identity, taxation and the control of consumption

Alcohol is what makes wine legally wine. EU law defines the product by a minimum actual alcoholic strength, generally 8.5% abv, with lower thresholds in certain zones and for certain designations, and appellation rules set minimum and sometimes maximum degrees that in turn dictate picking dates and whether enrichment is permitted. The declared figure is compulsory and policed within a tolerance of 0.5% abv in the EU, which is why analytical strength is a compliance matter and not just a stylistic one.

Fiscally, alcohol is the tax base. Duty is assessed on strength or on strength bands in most markets: the United States has historically taxed still wine in bands with a threshold at 14% abv, and although that excise boundary was raised to 16% abv in 2020, 14% abv remains the labelling threshold for 'table wine' - a boundary that has demonstrably influenced where producers choose to finish. Sparkling and fortified wines sit in separate, generally higher, categories for the same reason. Alcoholic strength therefore sits directly in the cost of goods and in the retail price ladder.

Most importantly, alcohol is why governments regulate wine at all. Licensing regimes, minimum purchase ages, drink-driving limits, advertising restrictions — France's Loi Evin restricts alcohol advertising in a major producing country — state retail monopolies in the Nordic countries and much of Canada, minimum unit pricing in Scotland, and mandatory health warnings all attach to the ethanol, not to the grape. In policy terms wine is an alcoholic beverage first and an agricultural product second, and any producer who forgets this misreads the industry's real operating environment.

## Health and society: part of the answer, not the whole of it

I place this fourth deliberately, because it is where an unbalanced answer would begin and end. Ethanol carries wine's health risk: the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies alcohol as a Group 1 carcinogen, and the epidemiology once used to support a protective effect of moderate consumption has weakened considerably. The commercial consequences are real — warning labels, the growth of moderation, a younger cohort drinking less. But this is alcohol's importance to public health, which is a different question from its importance in wine, and treating them as the same is the commonest way to write a narrow answer to a broad question.

## The direction of travel

Warming has pushed sugar at harvest, and therefore alcohol, upward across three decades. Producers now spend real money on the problem: earlier picking at the risk of unripe tannin, later-ripening varieties and clones, canopy and rootstock changes, and in the winery reverse osmosis or spinning-cone correction where it is legal. That so much viticultural and technological effort is directed at one component is itself the strongest evidence of that component's importance.

## Conclusion

Alcohol is important in wine because it does five jobs at once: it preserves, it structures, it defines the product in law, it sets the tax, and it determines how society treats the whole category. If I must rank them, I would say alcohol's historical importance was preservative — it is why wine could travel — and its contemporary importance is regulatory. The component that built the category is now the principal reason wine's future is being decided in health ministries rather than in vineyards, and that reversal is what makes this a contemporary question rather than a technical one.
