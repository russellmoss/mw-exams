## Defining the term

pH is the negative logarithm to base ten of the hydrogen ion activity of a solution — here, of the soil solution held around the soil particles and available to the roots. Because the scale is logarithmic, a soil at pH 5 is ten times more acidic than one at pH 6; small numerical shifts are large chemical ones. Soil pH is a property of the soil solution, not of the rock: it is set by the balance between basic cations held on the exchange sites (calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium) and hydrogen and aluminium ions, and it is measured conventionally on a soil-water or calcium-chloride suspension, which is why laboratory results must be compared on the same basis. Quality grapes are grown across a general soil pH range of about 5.5 to 8.0, and pH within that range is determined by parent geology, climate, organic matter, the leaching of water and minerals, and time.

## Part One: why soil pH matters

**Acidic soils** arise where high rainfall leaches basic cations out of the profile and replaces them with hydrogen and aluminium, and where the parent material — granite, schist, sandstone, some volcanic material — was low in bases to begin with. Beaujolais granite, Douro schist and the acid soils of parts of Oregon and the Loire sit at this end. As pH falls, aluminium and manganese become increasingly soluble below about pH 5.5 and are directly toxic to root tips, restricting the root system before any nutritional problem appears. Phosphorus is progressively fixed by iron and aluminium oxides and becomes unavailable; molybdenum and calcium fall away; nitrifying bacteria work best in roughly the pH 6 to 8 range, so nitrogen cycling and organic-matter breakdown slow; earthworm activity and rhizobial nitrogen fixation in legume cover crops both decline.

**Alkaline soils** arise on calcareous parent material where free carbonate buffers the profile — the chalk of Champagne, the Kimmeridgian marls of Chablis, the albariza of Jerez, terra rossa over limestone in Coonawarra. Above roughly pH 7.5 to 8, iron, zinc, manganese and boron precipitate into unavailable forms, producing lime-induced chlorosis: interveinal yellowing, reduced photosynthesis, weak shoot growth, delayed ripening and poor set. The severity depends less on pH itself than on active lime and on waterlogging, which raises bicarbonate in the soil solution.

Between those extremes, pH governs the whole nutritional and biological function of the soil: cation exchange, microbial populations, organic-matter turnover, and structural stability, since calcium flocculates clay while sodium in sodic alkaline soils disperses it and destroys drainage. The consequences for the grower are vigour, canopy density and root depth — that is, the three things that determine whether fruit ripens evenly and whether disease pressure can be controlled.

## Part Two: how growers can influence it

**Raising pH on acid soils** is done with lime. Ground calcium carbonate is the standard material, dolomitic lime where magnesium is also short, and the rate required depends on the soil's buffering capacity rather than on the pH reading alone — a clay or high-organic-matter soil needs several times the lime of a sand to move the same distance. Because lime is only slightly soluble and migrates down the profile very slowly, the single most effective intervention is incorporation to depth before planting, which is precisely why a full soil analysis and soil pits belong in the site-assessment stage rather than after establishment. Gypsum is often confused with lime here: it supplies calcium and improves the structure of sodic soils but does not raise pH.

**Lowering pH on alkaline soils** is harder and, on genuinely calcareous soils, largely impractical, because the carbonate reserve buffers almost any amendment. Where free lime is absent, elemental sulphur oxidised by soil bacteria to sulphuric acid is the standard tool, slow and dependent on soil temperature and moisture; ammonium-based fertilisers acidify over time as they nitrify; and acidification of irrigation water is used in Australia and California where bicarbonate-rich water is itself raising pH. Where the carbonate cannot be beaten, growers manage the symptom instead: chelated iron applied to the soil or foliage, careful drainage to reduce bicarbonate, mycorrhizal management, and tolerant rootstocks — which mitigate chlorosis but, as should be said plainly, do not change soil pH at all.

**Organic matter and cover cropping** move pH slowly but do the more valuable work of raising cation exchange capacity and buffering, so the soil resists swings, holds water better and leaches less. Compost, mulch and permanent swards are the standard means.

**Diagnosis must precede any of this.** Grid soil sampling by depth, cation exchange capacity and base saturation, and in-season petiole or leaf-blade analysis at flowering and veraison identify whether a symptom is a pH problem, a nutrient problem or a water problem, and allow correction inside the season rather than a year late.

## Linking pH management to the wine

The target is not a number but the grape quality the style requires. On acid soils, lifting pH toward six restores phosphorus and molybdenum availability and root depth, which stabilises yield and lets red varieties reach full phenolic ripeness rather than shutting down in a dry spell. On chalk and limestone, growers generally accept mild alkalinity and the moderate vigour it imposes, correcting only frank chlorosis, because the small berries and open canopies that result suit high-acid white and sparkling base wine. The judgement is therefore to correct pH where it is limiting root function, and to work with it where it is limiting vigour usefully.
