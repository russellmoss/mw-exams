## Defining the term

"Influencer" has been narrowed by usage to mean someone with a following on social media. The question deliberately resists that: it says "who or what", and it says influencers in the plural. I therefore treat an influencer as any person, institution or force that changes what a consumer buys, and that includes things with no voice at all — a shelf price, a monopoly's range decision, an appellation name, a piece of health legislation. Some of the most powerful influences on wine purchasing are structural rather than personal.

## How I rank them

I rank by three tests: **reach** (how many purchase decisions the influence touches), **trust** (whether the consumer acts on it), and **proximity** (how close it sits to the moment of purchase). An influence that scores on all three beats one that scores heavily on a single test. This is why a critic with global authority can matter less to total wine sales than a supermarket buyer nobody has heard of.

## The gatekeepers: the most important, and the least visible

The retail buyer is my first-ranked influencer. In markets dominated by a few grocers, a range review decides what several million consumers can physically buy; no amount of consumer demand survives delisting. The state monopolies make this explicit — Systembolaget in Sweden, Alko in Finland, the LCBO and the SAQ in Canada each publish tenders, and winning one is worth more than any campaign. Sommeliers perform the same function in the on-trade at lower volume but with higher influence per bottle, because they choose on the customer's behalf.

## Price and promotion

The second-ranked influence is a "what": the shelf price and the deal attached to it. In every mainstream market, promotional mechanics move more volume than any other single lever, and they sit at maximum proximity to purchase. Regulation belongs in this category too, because it acts on price directly — Scotland's minimum unit pricing, in force since 2018 at 50p per unit, changed the cheap end of the Scottish market by statute rather than persuasion. Duty regimes, health warning labelling and campaigns such as Dry January influence purchasing at a scale no individual commentator approaches.

## Region and brand as shorthand

The third is the appellation or category name doing the work of a recommendation. A consumer who reaches for Prosecco, Sancerre or Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc is being influenced by a collective brand built over decades. This influence is strong on reach and trust, weak on nothing, and it is the reason regional bodies spend generic promotional money at all.

## Critics and the specialist press

Fourth, and important to place accurately. Robert Parker's Wine Advocate, Jancis Robinson MW, Antonio Galloni's Vinous, James Suckling and Tim Atkin MW still move prices decisively at the fine end — Bordeaux en primeur pricing is still set in dialogue with published scores, and a high score on a shelf-talker demonstrably lifts rate of sale in US retail. But their reach into everyday purchasing is small and shrinking, and authority has fragmented from one dominant palate to many.

## Digital, social and peer

Fifth, and rising fastest. The important distinction is that digital influence mostly functions as validation at the point of purchase rather than as authority. Vivino, with of the order of sixty million registered users, is consulted in the aisle with the bottle already in hand. Livestream selling on Douyin and Tmall has become a genuine sales channel in China rather than a marketing one. Celebrity ownership — Kylie Minogue's wine range, Brad Pitt's involvement at Miraval, Snoop Dogg's association with 19 Crimes, Cameron Diaz's Avaline — recruits consumers who were not shopping the wine category at all, which is a different and valuable job.

Underneath all of it sits the influence consumer research keeps finding to be the strongest of any single source: friends, family and the host who poured it. Word of mouth wins on trust and proximity even though it has no reach of its own.

## The ranking changes by market and by drinker

This is where a general answer fails. In Sweden the monopoly's range decision is close to absolute. In China the gift context, the giver's status and now the livestream host outweigh critics entirely. In the US, the distributor's book and the retail chain's set determine availability before any consumer influence operates. In the UK the supermarket promotion is decisive at the mainstream and the specialist merchant's recommendation at the top. By age, older drinkers still index to critics and to region; drinkers in their twenties index to peers, to apps and to whatever is visible on social platforms, and to price.

## Conclusion

The most important influencers of wine purchasing today are not the ones the word now implies. Ranked by reach, trust and proximity together, they are the trade gatekeepers who decide availability, the price and promotion attached to the bottle, and the region or brand name acting as shorthand. Critics retain decisive influence over a small and valuable slice; social and celebrity influence is the fastest-growing force and mostly does the work of recruitment and validation rather than authority; and the single most trusted influencer remains the person who last poured the consumer a glass.
