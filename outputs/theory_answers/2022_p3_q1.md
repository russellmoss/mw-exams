## Scope: the change, not bulk shipping in general

Bottling at source is the shortest and most controlled route a wine can take. It moves from tank to line inside one cellar, under the producer's own quality control, and everything after that point is a sealed glass package. Bulk shipping breaks that. It inserts several thousand kilometres, several weeks and three or four extra transfers between finished wine and bottle, and it hands the final operation to a third party. I therefore treat each technical issue as a delta against the source-bottled baseline: what new exposure is created, what preparation must be added before loading, and what must be measured to prove the wine survived. Market perception and the commercial merits of bulk are outside the question; the container appears only where its engineering bears on the wine.

## The new exposures

**Distance and time.** A Southern Hemisphere shipment to northern Europe is of the order of four to six weeks at sea, plus port handling, road legs and holding at destination — two to three months from tank to bottle rather than a single day. Everything below follows from that gap.

**Temperature.** A container is not temperature-controlled unless specified. A deck stow through the tropics can take the interior well above 40°C, and the same box may then sit on a winter quay. The wine sees both a heat load, which accelerates oxidative and Maillard reactions, drives SO2 loss and provokes protein haze, and a chill, which throws tartrates. Insulated liners, reefer specification, routing and seasonal timing are the levers.

**Oxygen and handling.** Source bottling involves perhaps two movements. Bulk involves winery tank, road tanker, flexitank or ISO tank, destination tank, often a second tank, then the line. Every pump, hose and fitting is an oxygen pickup and a microbiological entry point, and it is cumulative dissolved oxygen rather than any single transfer that ages the wine.

**Contamination.** The vessel is not the producer's. A flexitank is single-use and food-grade but depends on supplier certification and an undamaged container floor; an ISO tank is reusable and needs a cleaning certificate and prior-cargo verification.

## Preparation before loading

Because the wine will be handled again, months later, by someone else, it must leave already stable to the conditions it will meet.

**Style-appropriate cellar stabilisation.** Tartrate stability must be achieved before loading, by cold stabilisation, electrodialysis or an inhibitor as the style allows, because the voyage will test it. Whites and rosés need heat-test-verified protein stability from bentonite, since the heat load will find any residual instability; reds need colloidal and colour stability. A full analysis of the finished wine — pH, TA, alcohol, residual sugar, malic acid, turbidity — sets that specification.

**Microbial stability.** Malolactic fermentation must be complete or reliably inhibited: a conversion starting in a warm flexitank produces CO2, volatile acidity and a bloated bag. Any fermentable sugar with viable yeast is a re-fermentation risk. The controls are membrane or crossflow filtration to a low viable count before loading, a molecular SO2 target of roughly 0.5 to 0.8 mg/L calculated from pH, and lysozyme or DMDC where appropriate. Plating or PCR before loading is the evidence.

**Chemical stability.** Free SO2 must be set meaningfully above the level intended at bottling so that it survives the voyage; dissolved oxygen must be driven low at fill, below roughly 0.5 mg/L; copper and iron should be checked, since casse risk rises with the extra metal contact.

## Shipment method, ullage and temperature

The two realistic vessels behave differently in ways that matter to the wine. A flexitank is a single-use bladder filled to its design volume, of the order of 24,000 litres, effectively brim-full so headspace is minimal — but it cannot be inerted or topped once sealed, cannot be sampled in transit, and has no thermal control without an insulating liner. An ISO tank can be nitrogen-blanketed and pressure-held, insulated or heated, and sampled through a manway; but a part-filled tank sloshes, which agitates the wine and enlarges the exposed surface. Ullage management is therefore a design decision taken before loading: either eliminate headspace or fill it with inert gas and verify the pressure on arrival.

## QC, QA and the samples

This is where the change is most demanding, and it is the part most easily under-specified. A representative sample must be drawn and retained at loading, with a full certificate of analysis and a sensory assessment; the destination bottler approves against that reference. A second sample must be drawn at discharge, before the wine leaves the container. Comparing the pair — free and total SO2, dissolved oxygen, volatile acidity, turbidity, microbiology, and above all a sensory check — is the only way to know whether transit damaged the wine, to choose the corrective treatment (SO2 adjustment, resting, re-filtration, fining) and to place liability if it did. The producer must also specify and audit the receiving operation: filtration regime, dissolved oxygen at fill, headspace and closure. Bulk programmes fail at the destination line as often as at sea.

## Conclusion

The technical question is whether the wine can be made stable enough, before it leaves, to tolerate months of heat, movement and extra handling — and whether the producer can prove by paired samples what actually arrived. Robust dry wines, tartrate- and protein-stable, with no fermentable sugar, will take it. Delicate aromatic whites, low-SO2 wines and anything with residual sugar carry a risk that no container specification removes.
