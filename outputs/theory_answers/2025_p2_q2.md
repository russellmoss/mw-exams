## What ageing potential means, and what destroys it

Ageing potential is the capacity to evolve positively in bottle over decades rather than merely to survive. A wine fails to age for four reasons: oxidation, microbial spoilage, colloidal or colour instability, and simple lack of matter to evolve. Winemaking cannot manufacture longevity absent from the fruit, but it decides how much of that potential survives. I evaluate the levers by the mechanism each one addresses, weighting them by how decisive they are, and I take the styles together rather than confining myself to red and white, because ageability is engineered quite differently in sparkling, sweet and fortified wine.

## Acidity and pH — the most under-rated lever

pH is the master variable and does three separate jobs. First, it governs sulphur dioxide efficacy: only the molecular fraction of free SO2 is antimicrobial and antioxidant, and that fraction rises steeply as pH falls, so a low-pH wine achieves protection at a free SO2 level a high-pH wine cannot reach at any legal addition. Second, it governs microbial stability directly — lactic acid bacteria and acetic acid bacteria are progressively inhibited below about pH 3.4 and increasingly comfortable above 3.5. Third, it governs colour: anthocyanins exist in a pH-dependent equilibrium in which the red flavylium cation predominates at low pH and gives way to colourless carbinol forms as pH rises, so a low-pH red both looks better and retains more pigment to polymerise. The practical decisions follow: harvest timing, tartaric addition made early enough to be integrated, and a considered choice to block malolactic conversion where retaining malic acid matters. A pH at or below roughly 3.4 is the working target for a wine intended to age. German Riesling, Loire Chenin Blanc and Hunter Semillon are the benchmark demonstrations; Hunter Semillon is picked at around 10-11% potential alcohol precisely to combine low pH with low alcohol, and ages for decades with no oak and no phenolic structure at all.

## Phenolic management in reds

Tannin and pigment supply the substrate for the slow polymerisation that carries a red wine forward. The mechanism is condensation of anthocyanins with tannins, largely mediated by acetaldehyde produced through controlled oxidation, into stable pigmented polymers; these resist the pH-driven colour loss that free anthocyanins suffer and soften the palate as they grow and eventually precipitate. Cold soak, extended maceration and cap management determine how much and what kind of phenolic material is extracted; seed tannin extracted late in ferment is harsher but more polymerisable, while over-extraction gives a wine that dries out rather than integrating. Micro-oxygenation and barrel maturation supply the acetaldehyde pathway in a controlled way.

## Oxygen strategy, vessel, lees and hygiene

Oxygen is neither friend nor enemy in the abstract; the question is total dose and timing. Whites intended to age take one of two coherent routes. Protective handling — inert gas, low temperature, minimal press pressure, early SO2 — preserves thiols and esters but leaves oxidisable phenolics in the wine, so the finished wine must be shielded for life. Controlled hyper-oxidation does the opposite: it deliberately oxidises and precipitates the phenolic substrate pre-fermentation so that the finished wine has little left to oxidise, which is the more robust long-term strategy. Barrel size and age set the oxygen ingress rate and add hydrolysable ellagitannins that act as an oxygen buffer, consuming oxygen preferentially and sparing the wine's own phenolics. Lees ageing releases mannoproteins that bind and stabilise colour, improve tartrate and protein stability and add colloidal weight, and lees also consume oxygen and glutathione-protect the wine — though they simultaneously consume free SO2, so the SO2 regime must be managed rather than set once. Hygiene underpins all of it: a Brettanomyces or lactic bacteria population that survives into bottle will express itself over years, and sound fruit, clean cooperage, topped vessels and a molecular SO2 target maintained against pH are the only reliable defences. Supplementary tools matter too — ascorbic acid as a fast oxygen scavenger used only alongside adequate SO2, chelating agents to bind the copper and iron that catalyse oxidation, and closure choice, since oxygen transmission rate over twenty years is a larger cumulative dose than anything applied in the cellar.

## Beyond red and white — sparkling, sweet and fortified

Sparkling wine ages on a different mechanism: dissolved CO2 and very low pH suppress microbial and oxidative reaction rates, autolysis supplies mannoproteins and Maillard precursors, and the wine continues to develop on lees for as long as it is left undisgorged. Champagne's second fermentation adds up to about 1.5% alcohol by regulation (roughly 27 g/L sugar via the liqueur de tirage), with typical practice adding somewhat less. Sweet wines age on the combination of high sugar, which lowers water activity and slows reaction, with high acidity and, in botrytised styles, high levels of SO2-binding compounds that force higher total additions — the reason Sauternes and Trockenbeerenauslese carry SO2 regimes no dry wine would tolerate. Fortified wines are stabilised by alcohol above the tolerance of any spoilage organism, then split by oxygen strategy: Vintage Port is bottled reductively and evolves in bottle, while tawny Port and Madeira are matured oxidatively to exhaustion and are effectively indestructible once bottled.

## Conclusion

The hierarchy is clear. pH and microbial stability are the necessary conditions — nothing else compensates for their absence. Phenolic and colloidal structure supplies the material to evolve. Oxygen management, vessel choice and closure determine the rate. Blending and oak refine. A candidate technique earns its place only if it can be tied to one of those mechanisms.
