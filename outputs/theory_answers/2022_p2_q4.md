## Defining style, and defining consistency

**Style** is the sensory and structural identity a buyer recognises and returns for: colour, aroma spectrum, sweetness, the balance of acid, alcohol and tannin, oak signature and texture. **Consistency** is not identity — two vintages of anything are never identical — but the wine falling reliably inside an agreed tolerance band around that identity, year after year, so that a repeat purchase delivers what the last one did.

Which wines need it, and why, varies sharply. High-volume branded wine and own-label supply live or die on it: a supermarket buyer lists against a specification and delists against a deviation, and the consumer buys the label rather than the vintage. Non-vintage sparkling and fortified wines are sold explicitly on a house style. Fine wine sits at the other end: a Grand Cru buyer expects a *house* signature but pays for vintage difference, so pursuing tight consistency there would destroy the proposition.

The failure I want to avoid is the assumption that repeating last year's operations reproduces last year's wine. It does not, because the input changes every year. Consistency is achieved by *responding* to variation, not by ignoring it.

## What works against consistency

Vintage variation is the largest force: heat and drought raising sugar, alcohol and pH while dropping acid; rain at harvest diluting and spreading botrytis; frost and hail cutting volume. Then yield variation, which changes concentration; vineyard age and replanting, which changes the fruit profile; grower contracts and bulk-market availability, which change the sourcing; barrel-lot and cooper variation; and personnel change. Underneath all of it, a warming climate is moving the baseline, so a style fixed twenty years ago may no longer be achievable from the same fruit at all.

## Levers, and why each works

**Sourcing breadth and blend scale.** The single most powerful tool, and it works by statistics: the more independent parcels in a blend, the more a poor sub-region or a hailed block is diluted toward the mean. A brand blending across South Eastern Australia, or a Champagne house across dozens of villages, has structural protection that a single-vineyard producer cannot buy. It is also why large producers hold multi-region contracts rather than one.

**Picking to a specification.** Harvesting to target analysis and sensory ripeness rather than to a calendar date, and picking a block in several passes to blend to a target, corrects for vintage advance and retreat at source.

**Must and wine adjustment.** pH is the master variable, because it drives colour, SO2 efficacy, microbial stability and the perception of freshness together; holding pH inside a narrow window with tartaric addition in warm years, or deacidification in cool ones, holds much else steady. Alongside it: chaptalisation or must concentration versus alcohol reduction by reverse osmosis or spinning cone; RCGM, süssreserve or sweet reserve to hit a fixed residual sugar; and tannin or oenological tannin additions to correct a thin, dilute year.

**Microbiological control.** An indigenous ferment presents a different microbial population every vintage, so its aromatic output varies; inoculating a chosen yeast strain, running to a fixed temperature curve, and either inoculating MLF or blocking it outright removes the largest single source of aroma variation. This is the clearest case where the *reason* matters: it is not that inoculation is better, but that it is repeatable.

**Maturation.** Specify the oak by cooper, forest, seasoning, toast and format, and rotate barrels on a fixed replacement schedule so the new-oak fraction is constant year to year — otherwise oak character drifts as a barrel park ages. Where a precise dose is wanted, staves and chips with micro-oxygenation are more repeatable than barrels, because the dose is weighed rather than inferred. Inert vessels are the choice where no oak signature belongs.

**Blending across years.** This is where commercial reality is most often missed. Holding back a proportion of the previous vintage, or a large-volume neutral lot, and blending it into the new season's wine both freshens a heavy year and fills out a thin one; most jurisdictions permit a minority proportion of another year in a vintage-labelled wine, commonly around 15%, and the option is used far more widely than it is discussed. Blending across varieties does the same job: a Cabernet-dominant blend can carry more Merlot in a hard year without leaving the style.

**Bench work and specification.** None of it functions without a written spec, retained benchmark samples from previous releases, blending trials assessed by a tasting panel against those benchmarks, and homogenised large blending tanks before bottling.

## Sparkling and fortified, briefly

Non-vintage sparkling wine is the purest expression of the principle — assemblage across villages and vintages, a reserve wine bank or perpetual reserve, and dosage as the final adjustment. The Sherry solera achieves the same by fractional blending, each *criadera* averaging many years. Both are worth naming, but neither should displace the still-wine discussion, since still wine is where most of the world's consistency problem sits.

## Conclusion

Consistency is bought principally with scale and blending, and only secondarily with process control; the winery levers correct at the margin what the vineyard and the blend have already largely determined. My position is that it is a commercial obligation at volume and a diminishing virtue as price rises — and that under a shifting climate, the honest answer is periodically to re-specify the style rather than to chase an unreachable original.
