## Definitions and scope

Three words decide this question. **Availability** is how much water a grower can physically, legally and affordably obtain in a given season — a stock question. **Sustainability** is whether the rate of extraction can continue indefinitely without drawing an aquifer below recharge, taking a river below ecological flow, or salinising the soil — a rate question, and a different one. A vineyard can be water-available and wholly unsustainable, which is the condition of a good deal of the world's irrigated vineyard. **Drought-prone** means structurally arid: perennial rainfall well below evapotranspirative demand, not a run of dry vintages. I therefore exclude Bordeaux or Marlborough in a hot summer and confine the answer to places like inland Chile's Limarí, Mendoza, the Swartland and Klein Karoo, the Riverland and McLaren Vale, Paso Robles and the San Joaquin, La Mancha and Jumilla, Santorini and the Negev — sites under roughly 200mm of growing-season rainfall against an evapotranspirative demand several times that.

The answer has two halves because the question does: what is decided at establishment, and what remains available once the vines are in the ground.

## Establishing: where sustainability is actually decided

**Size the vineyard to the water budget, not the land.** The first act is a hydrological audit — aquifer recharge rate, licensed allocation, river flow and its trend, water quality and salinity. Mendoza's supply is Andean snowmelt with a declining trend; planting an area that only works in a full-snowpack year is the original error, and no in-season technique corrects it.

**Rootstock and scion.** Drought-tolerant, deep-rooting stocks — 110R, 140Ru, 1103P — rather than 101-14 or Riparia Gloire; Ramsey and Ruggeri where salinity accompanies aridity, as in the Riverland. Scions chosen for low water use and late ripening: Grenache, Mourvèdre, Carignan, Assyrtiko, Xinomavro, Touriga Nacional. This is the cheapest and most permanent lever available and it exists only at planting.

**Density, training and layout.** Low densities let each vine command more soil volume: dry-farmed bush vines at 1,600 vines per hectare across La Mancha and Jumilla are a water strategy, not a tradition. Santorini's basket-trained kouloura is the extreme case, harvesting dew and sheltering fruit where there is effectively no summer rain. Deep ripping before planting opens the profile so roots follow stored winter moisture down instead of sitting in a drip bulb.

**Irrigation design.** Drip or subsurface drip rather than the flood irrigation still widespread in Mendoza; storage dams to capture winter flow; and the decision to install monitoring — capacitance probes, dendrometers, a pressure chamber — at planting rather than retrofitting.

## Managing an established vineyard

**Scheduling, not volume.** The gains here come from knowing the vine's status. Regulated deficit irrigation holds a mild, measured stress from fruit set to veraison and relieves it before the vine shuts down; partial rootzone drying, developed in Australia, alternates wetted sides to trigger the stress signal at lower total volume. Both reduce application substantially without a quality cost; both require the sensors above.

**Reform the canopy and the training regime.** Transpiration is proportional to functional leaf area, so a large VSP wall on a dry site is a self-inflicted wound. Trimming to a lower canopy, reducing shoot number, moving to a sprawl or bush habit and shading fruit with the canopy rather than cooling it with water all cut demand.

**Change the fertilising regime.** Nitrogen drives vegetative growth and therefore water use; on a drought-prone site it should be low, split and delivered by fertigation timed to actual soil moisture rather than broadcast in spring. Compost and under-vine mulch raise organic matter, water-holding capacity and infiltration while lowering soil temperature — the single best in-vineyard improvement to the water balance. Cover crops must be terminated or restricted to alternate rows once they compete.

**Remove water-based frost protection.** Overhead sprinkler frost protection consumes more water in a few nights than the vineyard uses in a month. In a drought-prone region it should be replaced by wind machines, heaters, delayed pruning and higher trunks.

**Reduce losses and reuse.** Fix leaks and pressure-compensate the lines; recycle treated winery wastewater to the rows, as many South African and Australian wineries now do; desalinated supply underpins agriculture in parts of the Middle East and the Aegean islands.

## The legal and shared dimension

Water sustainability is rarely a decision one grower can make alone, and the better response works with the regulation. California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act obliges basins to reach balanced extraction, which caps planting in Paso Robles regardless of a grower's own efficiency. Chile's water code allocates tradable rights that can be over-allocated relative to the river. Australia's Murray-Darling allocations are traded annually, so in a dry year irrigating a low-value block costs more than the crop is worth. Much of the EU restricts or prohibits irrigation in appellations such as Champagne and Bordeaux. A viticulturist must plant for the legal regime that will exist, not the one that exists now.

## Conclusion

Sustainability is settled at establishment — by matching planted area to a long-term water budget and by choosing rootstock, variety, density and training that need little water. In an established vineyard the grower can only improve efficiency: schedule with measurement, mulch, cut leaf area, strip out sprinkler frost protection and moderate nitrogen. Those measures are worth a great deal, but they cannot rescue a vineyard that was too large for its aquifer on the day it was planted.
