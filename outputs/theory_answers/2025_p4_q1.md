## What primacy means here

Champagne long ago ceased to be the largest sparkling category by volume: Prosecco overtook it on that measure more than a decade ago. Primacy therefore has to be defined in value — the highest average price per bottle of any sparkling wine sold at scale, the largest share of category value, and the benchmark against which every other traditional-method region positions itself. I read the question as asking how that value position has been defended, and I answer it under four heads: legal architecture, collective economic governance, price mechanics, and competitive response. The point worth making at the outset is that these are one system rather than four separate advantages, and the system's logic is scarcity converted into price.

## Legal architecture

The appellation is the foundation, and it is unusual in two respects. First, the delimited zone is tight and effectively closed, so supply cannot respond to demand by planting — which is the ordinary mechanism by which a successful wine region destroys its own price. Second, the name has been defended internationally with more money and more persistence than any other. The CIVC litigates use of the term worldwide, and the use of "méthode champenoise" for wine made outside the region was prohibited within the EU in the 1990s. That was a commercial act, not a legal nicety: it forced every competitor to build a new category name from scratch — Crémant, Cava, Franciacorta, Cap Classique, Trentodoc — and none of them carries the equity Champagne had already accumulated.

## Collective governance: the CIVC

The CIVC is what turns a legal boundary into managed scarcity. As an interprofession it sets, each harvest, the marketable yield in kilos per hectare and the volume that may be placed in individual reserve. That is a functioning supply-management tool of a kind almost no other appellation possesses: in a large vintage the surplus is locked away rather than shipped, and in a short one it is released. The effect is to stabilise the quantity reaching the market and the consistency of the blend, and therefore the price. It also holds together thousands of growers and a few hundred houses whose interests are directly opposed on grape price, converting what would be an annual conflict into a negotiated framework.

## Price mechanics

The numbers matter, because Champagne's cost base is what makes its price defensible rather than merely aspirational. Grapes trade at of the order of €6 per kilogramme, and the pressing rules imply roughly 1.2 kilogrammes of fruit per 75cl bottle, so fruit alone costs somewhere around €7 a bottle before base wine, tirage, a legal minimum of fifteen months on lees for a non-vintage wine, disgorgement, packaging, marketing and distribution. Prosecco's fruit cost per bottle is a small fraction of that. Champagne's price is therefore not pure brand rent; the floor under it is genuinely high, which is why discounting can be tactical without becoming structural.

That is where price architecture becomes a strategic choice. The grandes marques hold a high list price and use deep, time-limited promotion — the UK Christmas off-trade being the clearest case — to move volume without conceding the everyday shelf price. Critically, this trains a segment of consumers to buy only on deal and hands the retailer the price narrative. The houses accept that risk because the alternative, a permanently lower list price, would reset the category.

## Branding and distribution

Branding without distribution is decoration. Champagne's advantage is that a handful of houses with genuine marketing budgets — the largest sitting inside luxury groups whose spending norms come from fashion and spirits, not wine — buy presence in every channel at once: prestige on-trade placement and sponsorship, national off-trade listings, travel retail, and an export base that takes the majority of shipments by value. Grower Champagne then supplies the credibility layer, giving sommeliers and specialists something to discover without the category losing its luxury framing. Very few wine regions manage both registers simultaneously.

## Competition, critically examined

It is too easy to say Champagne "saw off" Prosecco. Prosecco did not take Champagne's occasions; it took a price point Champagne could not profitably serve, and in doing so grew total sparkling consumption and recruited drinkers into the category. Champagne's response — defend value, concede volume — was correct, but it was also the only option its cost base allowed. The evidence is mixed rather than triumphant: shipments have not returned to their 2007 peak of roughly 340 million bottles, while turnover has risen. Margin was protected at the cost of volume.

The more serious long-term threat is not Prosecco but premium traditional-method wine — English sparkling, Tasmania, Cap Classique, Franciacorta — attacking entry-level Champagne on quality from below, while climate change simultaneously erodes Champagne's marginal-ripeness advantage and improves theirs.

## Conclusion

Champagne has maintained primacy by converting a legally fixed and collectively rationed supply into a defensible price, then spending the resulting margin on brand and distribution at a scale no competitor can match. It is a system, and its weak point is demand, not supply: managed scarcity protects price elegantly in a growing market and simply shrinks the business in a contracting one.
