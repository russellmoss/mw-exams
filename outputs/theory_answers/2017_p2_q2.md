## The technical variables that make the styles

Sauvignon Blanc arrives at the winery with three things the winemaker can act on: a pool of odourless cysteine- and glutathione-bound thiol precursors that yeast must cleave to release 3-mercaptohexanol, its acetate and 4-methyl-4-mercaptopentan-2-one; methoxypyrazines already formed in the grape and reduced only by sunlight and time, not by winemaking; and high acid with a low tolerance of phenolic extraction. Every stylistic difference around the world comes from choices about oxygen, yeast, oak and blending applied to that raw material. I compare on those four axes, and then separately on volume, because the high-volume route is technically distinct rather than simply less good.

## Oxygen: the reductive and oxidative poles

The reductive route is the modern default in Marlborough, Chile's Casablanca and Leyda, South Africa's Elgin and Durbanville, and Touraine. Fruit is machine-harvested at night, chilled, sulphited at the crusher, pressed under inert gas at low pressure with ascorbic acid, and the juice settled cold. The purpose is to preserve glutathione and the thiol precursors, which oxidise readily; thiols themselves are lost to even small oxygen pickups later, so the wine stays under nitrogen or carbon dioxide to bottling, is closed under screwcap and often retains around 1 g/L of dissolved carbon dioxide to lift the aromatics.

The oxidative route is deliberately opposite and is practised in white Bordeaux and Pessac-Léognan, in parts of Sancerre, and in Margaret River. Juice hyperoxidation, or simply relaxed handling, allows polyphenol oxidase to oxidise the oxidisable phenolics so they polymerise and drop out before fermentation. The wine that results is less overtly aromatic and more citrus and mineral in profile, but it is far more oxygen-stable and ages for a decade rather than three years. Which pole a producer chooses is a longevity decision as much as an aroma one.

## Yeast: expression is inoculated in or fermented out

Yeast is not neutral here. Strains differ substantially in beta-lyase activity, and an expressive commercial strain releases several times more thiol from the same precursor pool than a neutral one, which is the single largest technical lever on Marlborough's passionfruit character. Fermentation conditions amplify it: cool ferments of 12-15C with generous assimilable nitrogen maximise both thiols and acetate esters, whereas barrel-fermented lots run warmer, at 18-22C, deliberately trading aromatic intensity for texture and integration.

The counter-practice is ambient fermentation on indigenous flora, used in Sancerre, in parts of Bordeaux and by a growing minority in New Zealand and South Africa. This gives slower, cooler, mixed-population ferments with more non-*Saccharomyces* activity, lower ester expression, higher glycerol and a broader, less varietal wine. Neither is old-world or new-world practice; both are in use in both.

## Oak, lees and vessel

Oak in Sauvignon Blanc is used for texture and oxygen transfer, not for aroma — new oak overwhelms the variety. The Bordeaux and Margaret River model ferments in barriques of mostly seasoned wood, ages on full lees with bâtonnage for six to twelve months and often allows partial malolactic fermentation, converting acidity into texture. Fumé Blanc in California, the term Robert Mondavi coined in the 1960s, established the same idea in the New World; the useful contrast today is with the newer Californian and Chilean lots fermented in concrete eggs, foudres or amphorae, which give oxygen exchange and lees contact without oak flavour. Loire practice generally uses no small oak at all, relying on extended lees ageing in tank. In Styria and Friuli the vessel of choice is large old wood, and in the orange-wine fringe skin contact is applied for weeks, which extracts exactly the phenolics that the reductive school works to avoid.

## Blending

Blending is a technical instrument, not an afterthought. In Bordeaux and Margaret River, Semillon adds weight, wax and ageing capacity while diluting pyrazine. In Marlborough, blending across Wairau and Awatere lots is standard, the Awatere component supplying higher acid and more herbaceous, saline character. In Rueda the appellation permits Sauvignon Blanc alongside and blended with Verdejo. Press-fraction blending is the finest-grained version: free run kept separate, the phenolic later fractions either declassified or reintroduced in measured proportion for structure.

## The high-volume model

Most Sauvignon Blanc in the world is made at scale, and the technical route is genuinely different. Machine harvest, refrigerated bulk transport, must clarification by flotation or centrifuge rather than long settling, pectolytic enzyme addition, fermentation in temperature-controlled tanks of hundreds of thousands of litres with a reliable, fast, expressive strain plus staged nitrogen, then back-blending of unfermented juice or rectified concentrate to a few grams per litre of residual sugar to fill the mid-palate and soften the acidity. Stabilisation is by bentonite for protein and by carboxymethylcellulose or potassium polyaspartate rather than energy-hungry cold stabilisation. The wine is sterile filtered, bottled under screwcap, and often shipped in flexitank and bottled in market, which adds an oxygen and SO2 management problem the estate producer never faces. Chile's Central Valley, Pays d'Oc, South African bulk and much of Marlborough work this way.

## Conclusion

Comparing across the world, the decisive variable is not latitude or tradition but oxygen management from picking to bottle, with yeast strain the second lever and oak a distant third. A producer with the knowledge and nerve to move along the reductive-oxidative axis can make wines from the same fruit that share almost nothing.
