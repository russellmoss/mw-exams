## What "inexpensive" means here, and why the three words bind together

I take inexpensive to mean a wine retailing at roughly £6-8 in a Northern European multiple, or its equivalent — bottled in tens of thousands of cases, with a pre-bottling budget of a few cents a bottle and no time for barrels, long lees ageing or natural clarification. A £20 bottle is not inexpensive in most markets. Think co-operative Languedoc, La Mancha or Chilean Central Valley, not a classed growth.

The three qualifiers describe **one wine**, not three. Organic sets what I may add and do; vegan sets what I may use as a processing aid; the price sets how fast and how cheaply I must work. Where they conflict the organic rulebook wins, because it is legally enforceable and the vegan claim is not.

One point of vocabulary, since it is routinely confused. Sulphur is the element, used as a vineyard fungicide. What I add in the winery is sulphur dioxide. What the regulation caps and the label declares is sulphite. They are not interchangeable.

## What organic status removes from the toolkit

Under the EU organic winemaking rules a set of processes I would otherwise reach for at this price is closed: electrodialysis and cation exchange for tartrate stability, dealcoholisation, and physical removal of SO2. On the additive side PVPP, CMC, sorbic acid and DMDC are all unavailable. The consequence is concrete: for tartrate stability I am left with cold contact stabilisation, seeded with cream of tartar and filtered cold, or with metatartaric acid, which is permitted but hydrolyses and buys perhaps a year in a warm warehouse. For a wine shipped to a hot market I would cold-stabilise and verify by conductivity rather than trust an inhibitor.

Sulphite ceilings are lower. EU organic caps total SO2 roughly 50 mg/L below the conventional limits for dry wines — about 100 mg/L for a dry red and 150 mg/L for a dry white or rose — and 30 mg/L below for wines above 2 g/L residual sugar. The USA diverges sharply: NOP "organic wine" permits no added sulphites at all, total below 10 mg/L, so my wine must be sold there as "made with organic grapes", which permits up to 100 mg/L total. That is a specification decision taken before bottling, not after.

## What vegan status removes

Vegan is not an ingredient claim. It excludes any animal-derived processing aid even where nothing is detectable in the finished wine: gelatine, isinglass, casein, egg albumen, lysozyme (egg-derived) and crustacean chitosan. I am left with bentonite, pea and potato protein, plant tannin, activated carbon and yeast-derived aids. The claim therefore rests on traceability rather than analysis — supplier declarations for every fining agent, filter sheet, enzyme and yeast product, and line segregation if non-vegan wines share the equipment. One useful consequence: with no egg or milk fining there is nothing to declare against the EU allergen threshold of 0.25 mg/L, and the label carries only the sulphite declaration above 10 mg/L.

## The pre-bottling treatments I would prioritise

1. **Bentonite to heat stability, done once and early.** Protein haze is the classic cheap-white failure and bentonite is vegan, organic-permitted and cheap. Trial to the minimum effective dose; over-fining costs volume and aroma.
2. **Cold stabilisation to tartrate stability**, as above, integrated with the chilling the line already needs.
3. **Fining for clarity with pea or potato protein**, bench-trialled, in place of gelatine. Expect a slightly lower efficiency and plan the dose accordingly.
4. **Cross-flow filtration then a final membrane.** With the chemical preservatives closed to me, filtration is the microbial control, not a polish.
5. **SO2 and pH management as a pair.** Molecular SO2 is what protects, and 25 mg/L free delivers roughly 0.6 mg/L molecular at pH 3.4 but only about 0.4 mg/L at pH 3.6. Under a reduced total ceiling I would acidify to hold pH low rather than chase free SO2 upward.
6. **Dissolved oxygen and CO2 adjustment** on the way to the filler, with inert gas cover throughout.

## The QC analyses that matter

Free and total SO2 by aspiration, with molecular calculated from the measured pH; pH and titratable acidity; volatile acidity; alcohol; glucose plus fructose, not "residual sugar" by difference; malic acid, to confirm whether any fermentable substrate remains; dissolved oxygen and CO2; turbidity and filterability index before the membrane; heat and cold stability confirmation; copper and iron; and microbiological plating for viable yeast and lactic acid bacteria, with a sterility check on first and last bottles. Documentary QC is equally load-bearing: organic certificates for every input, vegan declarations, and lot traceability sufficient to survive a certifier's audit.

## Where the three constraints compound

The dangerous case is an inexpensive wine finished with a few grams of residual sugar to soften it. Sorbic acid and DMDC are barred by the organic rules, the SO2 ceiling is lower than usual, and reduced protection means the whole defence rests on a sterile 0.45 micron membrane, an aseptic filler and obsessive hygiene. That is where I would spend the money: on filtration integrity testing and line sanitation, not on additives I am not allowed to use.
