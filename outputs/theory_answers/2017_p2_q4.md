## What is distinct about each place

The three are not stand-ins for their countries, and the winemaking only makes sense against what makes each specific.

**Cornas** is the outlier of the northern Rhône. It permits no white grapes at all, where Côte-Rôtie allows up to 20 per cent Viognier and Hermitage and Crozes up to 15 per cent Marsanne and Roussanne — so co-fermentation, the classic northern Rhône softening and aromatic tool, is unavailable here as a matter of law. Its steep granite amphitheatre faces south and is sheltered from the north wind, making it the warmest and earliest-ripening of the northern appellations. The fruit arrives with high anthocyanin, ripe but assertive tannin and firm acidity, and there is no white grape and no appellation white to lean on.

**McLaren Vale** is not Barossa. It is Mediterranean and maritime, cooled by afternoon gulf breezes, with a mosaic of soils — the deep sands of Blewitt Springs giving perfumed, fine-boned Shiraz, the ironstone and clay of Seaview and Willunga giving darker, firmer wine. Compared with Barossa's warmer, more continental sites, the fruit is medium-bodied rather than opulent, more savoury and red-fruited, with better natural acid retention. Barossa's traditional winemaking answer was full ripeness and American oak hogsheads; McLaren Vale's contemporary one is very different.

**Hawke's Bay** is the marginal site of the three. Syrah ripens at its limit on the free-draining Gimblett Gravels, a defined area of roughly 800 hectares, and the neighbouring Bridge Pa Triangle. The risk is not excess ripeness but pyrazine, thin colour and low tannin, and the season can end wet.

## Cornas: extraction is not the problem, structure is

The traditional model still defines the appellation: whole-bunch fermentation with stems providing tannin, structure and lift; open wooden or concrete vats; long cuvaison of three to four weeks with foot treading and pigeage; and, in the most traditional cellars, a submerged cap. Ambient yeast, warm ferments and post-fermentation maceration all push extraction hard, because the wine is built to be reductive and tannic and to need a decade. Maturation is in old foudres and demi-muids rather than new barriques, because the tannin needs air and time, not oak flavour, and many wines are bottled unfined and unfiltered. The modernist strand within the appellation destems fully, controls temperature and uses a proportion of new oak, producing an earlier-drinking wine — but with no Viognier permitted, even the modernists cannot soften the wine that way. Chaptalisation is permitted; acidification is not, so picking date is the acid decision.

## McLaren Vale: managing ripeness and alcohol

Here the winemaker starts from the opposite problem. Sugar accumulates ahead of phenolic ripeness, so the defining decisions are picking earlier than the region once did, and controlling alcohol. Contemporary practice includes partial whole-bunch inclusion where stems are genuinely lignified, cold soak for a few days to build colour, fermentation in small open fermenters with hand plunging or gentle pump-overs, and cooler ferment peaks than in Barossa to preserve red fruit and perfume. Acidification with tartaric acid at the crusher is routine and legal, since natural pH runs high. Maturation has moved decisively from American oak hogsheads toward seasoned French puncheons and larger foudres, with concrete and amphora used for a proportion of the blend to keep the fruit unmarked by wood. Alcohol adjustment by reverse osmosis or spinning cone is legal in Australia and used discreetly. Blending with Grenache and Mataro is a McLaren Vale reflex that Cornas cannot use and Hawke's Bay does not need.

## Hawke's Bay: building a wine that has little to give

The techniques invert again. The winemaker must extract carefully from a must with modest anthocyanin and tannin, so cold soak of several days is used to draw water-soluble colour before alcohol arrives, and extraction thereafter is gentle — pump-over or délestage, or a genuine infusion approach, since hard plunging pulls green tannin from unripe seeds. Ferment temperatures are held moderate to retain floral and peppery rotundone character. Whole-bunch is used, but only in ripe years and only where stems are brown and lignified; in a cool vintage it would add exactly the green note the region is fighting. New Zealand has no appellation constraint, so co-fermentation with a few per cent of Viognier is available and is used. Malolactic fermentation is often completed in barrel, and maturation is in seasoned French barriques with a low new-oak proportion and a shorter élevage, because the wine's frame will not absorb more. Where Cornas fears dilution, Hawke's Bay fears rain at harvest, so selective picking and sorting matter more here than anywhere.

## The techniques set side by side

Stems: structural and traditional in Cornas, selective and vintage-dependent in the other two. Extraction: maximal in Cornas, moderate and perfume-preserving in McLaren Vale, minimal and colour-seeking in Hawke's Bay. Acid: fixed by picking date in Cornas, added in the two New World regions, where acidification with tartaric acid is routine and legal in Australia and New Zealand, while in Cornas chaptalisation is permitted and acidification is not. Oak: large and old in Cornas, moving that way in McLaren Vale, small and seasoned in Hawke's Bay. Blending: forbidden in Cornas, a Rhône-blend habit in McLaren Vale, a small Viognier option in Hawke's Bay.

## Conclusion

The common thread is that each winery is correcting what its site does not supply. Cornas has structure and needs time; McLaren Vale has ripeness and needs restraint; Hawke's Bay has perfume and needs gentleness. The best wine in each place comes from techniques chosen against that specific deficit — which is why applying Barossa's methods in McLaren Vale, or Côte-Rôtie's in Cornas, produces a lesser wine.
