## A definition that can be tested

The word is used in wine to mean at least four incompatible things: made without additives; made as it was made a century ago; made by a person rather than a company; and genuinely being what the label says. Only the last is a definition that can be tested, and the first three are the reason the debate goes in circles.

I define an authentic wine as one in which **there is correspondence between what the wine claims and what the wine is**. Authenticity is not a property of the liquid. It is a relationship between a claim and its delivery, and it is therefore a question about honesty and verification, not about technique. Three consequences follow immediately, and I will defend all three. A wine made in a stainless-steel tank from machine-harvested fruit with cultured yeast and a fining agent is authentic if that is what it presents itself as. A wine made in amphora with no added sulphur is inauthentic if it is sold as the product of a single old vineyard and is in fact bought-in fruit. And a wine can be excellent and inauthentic, or dull and entirely authentic, because authenticity and quality are separate axes that the trade routinely conflates.

The most common failure on this topic is to read it as a question about natural wine. Natural wine is one claim-type among several, and by my definition it has no privileged position: it is authentic exactly to the extent that its practitioners do what they say, which — in the absence of any legal definition or audit — is highly variable.

## The claim-types, and how each can fail

**Place.** The oldest and best-codified claim. A Chablis claims a delimited area, permitted varieties and yields; a single vineyard claims a parcel. This claim fails when fruit is imported across a boundary, when yields are exceeded and the surplus is laundered, or when a permitted variety rule is broken — as alleged in the 2008 Brunello investigation, which is the definitive modern case of a place claim failing. It matters that a regional blend is not less authentic than a single vineyard: it claims less, and delivers it. Authenticity is not a synonym for specificity.

**Process.** Organic, biodynamic, natural, unfined, unfiltered, wild-fermented, amphora-raised. Here the certified claims are the strong ones, because organic and biodynamic certification is audited, and the uncertified ones are the weak ones, because "natural" means whatever the producer wishes. Josko Gravner's return to Georgian qvevri is authentic under my test not because the vessel is ancient but because the wine is unmistakably the product of the process it claims. The same test convicts a "low-intervention" wine that has been acidified and reverse-osmosed in private.

**Person and vision.** The claim of a named individual making a wine to a conception. This is where the industry's romance concentrates and where the claim most often decays quietly: the estate whose founder's face remains on the label a decade after a corporate purchase, the "grower" bottling with contracted fruit, the consultant-made wine sold as an owner's expression. The failure here is rarely fraud; it is the persistence of a claim after the thing that justified it has gone.

**Provenance and identity.** The most literal form: is this wine physically what the bottle says? Austria's diethylene glycol scandal of 1985 and Rudy Kurniawan's forgeries sit at the two ends of it, one an adulteration of a whole country's product and the other a counterfeiting of the top of the fine-wine market. Both are pure authenticity failures under any definition, and both were detected by chemistry and by outsiders rather than by the trade's own vigilance, which is a serious indictment.

**Brand.** The claim least respected and most often honoured. A large-volume branded varietal wine claims a flavour profile, a price and a consistency, and delivers all three with high reliability. By my definition it is authentic, and the discomfort that causes is a useful diagnostic: what the trade usually means by "inauthentic" is "industrial", which is a preference, not a judgement about truthfulness.

## Authenticity is made along the whole chain, not in the cellar

The claim can be broken after the wine is finished, which is why this cannot be a question only about production. Bulk shipping with bottling at destination is often described as inauthentic; it is not, provided the label says where bottling occurred, and it is an environmentally superior practice that the trade disparages on aesthetic grounds. Blending back at a bottler, storage in poor conditions, parallel imports of heat-damaged stock, and retail relabelling all damage correspondence between claim and delivery. So does the communication layer: a merchant's description of a wine as "made by hand in tiny quantities" that the producer never wrote is an authenticity failure committed at the point of sale.

The mechanisms that actually protect authenticity are unglamorous and largely institutional — appellation inspection, wine-of-origin schemes, organic audit, isotopic and trace-element analysis, tamper-evident capsules and bottle-level traceability. They are also under-funded relative to the value they protect.

## Why it matters, and to whom

Authenticity is not equally valuable to everyone. To the **consumer** it is consumer protection: the guarantee that price paid corresponds to product received, which matters most at the top of the market, where the premium over the liquid's material cost is greatest, and at the bottom, where fraud is cheapest. To the **producer** and to a **region** it is the defence of an asset: an appellation's name is collective property, and a member who breaks the claim expropriates every other member — which is why regions police themselves harder than any regulator requires. To the **investor and collector** it is the entire basis of value. To the **critic and educator** it is the precondition of the exercise: a tasting note about a wine that is not what it says it is describes nothing.

There is also a group for whom it matters very little, and honesty requires saying so: the great majority of the world's wine drinkers, buying at everyday prices, are served by a claim so modest that it is almost never broken.

This is why I resist the position that authenticity is purely subjective. If it were, it could not be enforced, and the appellation system, organic certification and fraud prosecution would all be category errors rather than the working machinery they are. What is subjective is which claims a drinker cares about; what is objective, and testable, is whether the claim that was made has been kept.

## Conclusion

What makes wine authentic is not naturalness, not tradition, not the absence of technology and not the presence of a person's name. It is the truthfulness of the wine's claim about itself — of place, process, person, provenance or brand — and the existence of a means by which that claim can be verified. This definition has the virtue of applying across the whole chain and the whole price spectrum, of separating authenticity from quality and from taste, and of producing verdicts the trade finds uncomfortable: that a well-run commercial brand is authentic, that an uncertified natural wine may not be, and that the most authentic wines are simply those whose makers claim no more than they can prove.
