## Definition and scope

Direct to consumer means a sale in which the producer transacts with the end consumer and no intermediary takes title: cellar door, wine club or mailing list, winery e-commerce and direct shipping, and events. The test I use is title, not technology. An online retailer selling a producer's wine is not DTC, because the retailer bought the wine; a producer selling through its own site is, even if a third party fulfils it. I exclude direct-to-trade, which is a different disintermediation. I note the boundary case of marketplaces such as Tmall, where a producer operates its own storefront but the platform controls the customer data - functionally DTC, strategically less so.

## Where it is increasing

**The United States, by a distance.** This is the largest and fastest-growing DTC market and the one with the clearest data, because interstate shipping is licensed and therefore counted. The 2005 Supreme Court decision in *Granholm v. Heald* began the dismantling of discriminatory shipping bans, and more than forty states now permit some form of winery-to-consumer shipping. Growth is concentrated in Napa, Sonoma, the Willamette Valley and Washington. Ridge Vineyards has run its Advance Tasting Program club for decades and is the template most Californian producers have since copied.

**Australia and New Zealand**, through cellar door and mature wine-club culture, and increasingly through producer e-commerce as small producers find the domestic retail duopoly closed to them.

**Northern Europe**, but by a different mechanism: Germany's is a mail-order market of long standing, with Hawesko in Germany the largest specialist group, and Britain's growth has come through subscription and funding models rather than the cellar door, with Naked Wines the clearest instance.

Producer-country home markets where the property has always sold at the gate -- recoltant-manipulant growers in Champagne sell a meaningful share of their production direct from the property -- and the same holds across Austria, southern Germany and rural France.

**Wine-tourism economies**: South Africa, where Stellenbosch and Franschhoek cellar doors with restaurants have become destinations, and England, where Chapel Down in England has built a business heavily weighted to visitor and online sales.

## Why

**Margin, first and last.** A producer selling through a three-tier chain typically receives a third to a half of the shelf price. Selling direct, it keeps everything except fulfilment and payment costs. In a premiumising market where volume growth is unavailable, margin recapture is the only lever left.

**Regulation has loosened** in the US and platform and payment technology has made a small winery's shop indistinguishable from a large one's.

**Retail has closed.** Consolidated grocery and distribution mean fewer, larger customers with fewer slots. For a producer of a few thousand cases, DTC is not a strategy of choice but the only route to market.

**The customer relationship is itself the asset.** DTC gives the producer names, purchase history and permission to contact - none of which a distributor will hand over. That data is what makes allocation, pre-release and club economics possible.

The US shipping channel runs to several million cases and several billion dollars a year, which is small in volume against total US consumption but disproportionate in value. That gap is the whole business case.

Against it sit real costs. Customer acquisition is the binding constraint: a tasting room is an expensive shop with staff, licences, insurance and a rent that does not vary with sales, and club members attrit steadily, so a club is a leaking bucket that must be refilled every year. Compliance in the US means licences, reporting and tax in every state shipped to. Fulfilment is heavy, fragile and temperature-sensitive, and shipping cost is a large fraction of a bottle's value. Finally, DTC consumes management attention - CRM, e-commerce, hospitality - that a winemaking business does not naturally possess.

## What leads producers away from it

Scale is the first thing. A producer of half a million cases cannot acquire and service that many individual customers, and its economics work through distribution. Channel conflict is the second: a producer undercutting its own distributor's customers will find its brand deprioritised, so DTC pricing must usually be held at or above retail, which removes the consumer's incentive. Third, DTC only works where there is something to be scarce about - a wine freely available at a supermarket has no reason to be bought at the gate. Fourth, remoteness: cellar-door DTC is a function of visitor numbers, which is why it works in Napa and not in La Mancha. And fifth, regulation still blocks it outright in several US states and in markets with monopoly retail.

## Conclusion

DTC is increasing where three conditions coincide: regulation permits it, the wine is scarce or premium enough that the consumer will seek it out, and the producer is too small to matter to a distributor. That is why it is a US, Antipodean and small-producer-Europe phenomenon rather than a global one - and why the largest producers, who would gain most in absolute margin, are the least able to use it.
