## What own-label is, and the winery I am answering for

Own-label means a label owned and registered by the retailer or buyer rather than by the winery. It is most often an off-trade, supermarket-driven project — including the tiered own-label ranges of the kind British supermarkets run beneath their fascia names — but the same structure appears in the on-trade, where a restaurant or pub group commissions its house wine, and in the buyer's own brand supplied to a distributor or importer. The defining feature is not who makes the wine but who owns the brand and therefore the consumer relationship. Retailer own-label accounts for a substantial share of supermarket wine volume in the UK, which is why this is a strategic question rather than a marginal one.

It is worth separating own-label from two things it is confused with. An exclusive brand, made for one retailer but owned by the winery, keeps the asset with the producer and is a different commercial animal. Straight contract production, where the winery is paid a fee to process someone else's fruit, carries no brand risk at all.

I am answering for a winery of roughly 200,000 to a million cases: large enough to hold significant capacity and press a genuine cost advantage, too small to run dedicated own-label lines or to absorb the loss of one major account.

## The strengths

**Capacity utilisation and fixed-cost dilution.** A winery is a fixed-cost business with one intake a year. Own-label fills tanks, keeps a bottling line running and spreads overhead across more litres, which lowers the unit cost of everything the winery makes — including its own brand. For a medium producer this is often the single largest source of margin improvement available.

**Cash flow and forecastability.** An own-label contract gives agreed volumes at agreed dates. That allows confident grape and dry-goods purchasing, reduces working capital held against uncertain demand, and — for a business without the balance sheet to fund a brand launch — turns wine into predictable cash.

**No marketing cost and instant distribution.** The retailer supplies the shelf space, the footfall and the promotional support. A medium winery that could never afford to buy a national listing gets volume at a marketing cost of nil, and gets it immediately.

**A relief valve in oversupply.** In a surplus — the context behind vine-pull and distillation schemes in Bordeaux and the Languedoc — own-label absorbs parcels that would otherwise be sold as distressed bulk or written down. It liquidates volume without discounting the winery's own brand, which is a genuine strategic protection.

**Market intelligence.** Working with a major grocer gives visibility of consumer data, pack trends and category performance that a medium winery cannot buy.

## The weaknesses

**Structural margin compression.** Own-label is bought competitively, re-tendered regularly, and priced against the lowest capable supplier. Margins are thin by design and are squeezed further by retailer costs the winery cannot control: promotional funding, retrospective discounts, packaging and technical audits, ESG and compliance requirements, and specification changes mid-contract.

**No asset is built.** Every case sold builds the retailer's brand equity, not the winery's. After ten years the winery owns nothing it can sell, and the customer relationship it has been serving belongs to someone else.

**Power asymmetry and concentration risk.** The retailer can move the contract to another supplier at short notice; the winery cannot replace a third of its volume at short notice. This is the decisive weakness, and it is asymmetric in exactly the wrong direction.

**Cannibalisation.** The own-label wine frequently sits on the same shelf as the winery's branded wine, from the same region and often the same blend, at a materially lower price. If the consumer works that out, the branded price is undermined.

**Opportunity cost of locked capacity.** Contracted volume cannot be redirected. In a short vintage, or when a better-priced opportunity appears, the own-label commitment is honoured at the old price with fruit that now costs more — as producers have found where drought and disease pressure in Penedes have cut grape availability for Cava, turning a comfortable contract into a loss-making one.

## Why the medium-sized case is the difficult one

For a very large winery the answer is easy: it has the scale, dedicated capacity and buying power to make thin margins profitable, and no single contract is existential. For a small premium estate the answer is also easy — it has neither the volume nor a reason to dilute its brand.

The medium winery sits precisely where the trade-off bites. Its volume is large enough that own-label meaningfully improves its cost base, and large enough that losing the contract would be severe. It has a brand worth protecting but not yet strong enough to fund the business alone. And it lacks the negotiating weight to resist retailer cost pressure. Own-label is therefore neither obviously right nor obviously wrong at this size — it is a calculated exposure.

## Conclusion

Own-label is a capacity and cash-flow instrument, not a strategy, and I would use it as such. For a medium-sized winery it is the right decision when it is ring-fenced: capped at a defined share of capacity — I would work to no more than a third — spread across more than one customer, priced to make a real contribution rather than merely to cover variable cost, and never applied to the winery's flagship tier or its most distinctive parcels.

The strength is that it turns idle capacity into cash without discounting the brand. The weakness is that it is addictive, because the volume is easy and the alternative is expensive, and a winery that lets it grow past a third of production has quietly stopped being a brand owner and become a contract supplier that has not yet been told.
