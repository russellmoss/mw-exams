## What "responsible" means here

Responsibility is an obligation to bear the consequences of one's actions on people, on the environment and on the users of one's product. I assess the wine industry in three domains — environmental stewardship, social and labour conduct, and product responsibility, meaning honesty about a product that causes harm — and against a fourth, cross-cutting test: **accountability**, which is whether performance is measured, independently audited and disclosed, rather than asserted. An industry that behaves well but cannot prove it is only accidentally responsible.

"The wine industry" is the whole chain: nurseries, growers, wineries and co-operatives, packaging suppliers, bulk shippers and bottlers, importers, distributors, retail and on-trade, and the marketing and education layer. Responsibility must be tested at each link, because the industry's habit is to judge itself by its best producers, who account for a trivial share of the volume.

My verdict, argued below: the wine industry is **moderately responsible and improving unevenly**. It is better than comparable agricultural sectors on land stewardship and biodiversity, roughly average on labour, and weakest — and most exposed — on product responsibility and on the gap between premium rhetoric and commodity practice.

## Environmental: genuine strength, badly measured

Wine's environmental record is its best domain, for a structural reason: a perennial crop grown on land whose value depends on its long-term condition creates an incentive to farm well that annual commodity agriculture lacks. Organic and biodynamic certification is proportionally far higher in vines than in most crops, and the reduction in herbicide use, the return of cover cropping and the retention of uncultivated habitat are real. Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand now certifies the great majority of the country's vineyard area, which turned sustainability from a marketing claim into a condition of market access — the single most effective piece of environmental governance the industry has produced. California's regional programmes and the Porto Protocol and International Wineries for Climate Action follow similar logic on carbon.

The weaknesses are in the links nobody photographs. The glass bottle is usually the single largest contributor to a bottled wine's carbon footprint, and the trade has spent thirty years making bottles heavier as a quality signal, which is an act of straightforward irresponsibility. Bulk shipping with bottling at destination is the largest carbon reduction available to the category, and it is resisted for reasons of image rather than quality. Copper accumulation in organically farmed soils is a genuine and under-discussed liability. Water is worse still: irrigation entitlements in Australia's Murray-Darling Basin, aquifer drawdown in central Spain and Chile, and the Western Cape's drought years put wine in direct competition with drinking water and food crops, and the industry has no coherent position on it.

## Social and ethical: the chain's weakest visible link

Grape growing is labour-intensive, seasonal and frequently migrant-staffed, which is the classic setting for exploitation. Human Rights Watch reported on farmworker housing and pesticide exposure in the Western Cape in 2011, and the response — the WIETA ethical seal and Fairtrade certification, now widely held by South African producers — is a fair example of the industry correcting under pressure rather than in advance of it. Champagne has faced repeated scrutiny of seasonal picker accommodation and labour contractors. California's harvest depends on migrant labour whose legal status is precarious. New Zealand's Recognised Seasonal Employer scheme is the counter-example worth citing: a state-and-industry framework that gives Pacific workers documented status, minimum guarantees and return rights, and it demonstrates that the problem is solvable when the industry wants it solved.

Further down the chain the pressure inverts. Retailer and distributor consolidation transfers margin and payment risk onto growers and co-operatives; long payment terms and delisting risk are as much an ethical issue as picker housing, and are almost never discussed as one. Fraud is the other ethical failure: Rudy Kurniawan showed how weakly the fine wine market authenticates its own goods, and the 2008 Brunello investigation in Montalcino showed that appellation compliance is enforced episodically. Both are self-policing failures.

## Product responsibility: where the industry is least defensible

This is the domain where the industry's conduct is closest to the sectors it dislikes being compared with. Wine markets an addictive Group 1 carcinogen while resisting the labelling that every food product accepts as routine — ingredients, nutritional information and unit content have all been fought rather than volunteered. The industry has funded research into its own product's health effects: a large United States trial of moderate drinking was cancelled in 2018 when its funding was traced to the drinks industry, an episode with an exact tobacco precedent. Industry-funded moderation bodies do useful work, but they are also a well-understood mechanism for occupying the space a regulator would otherwise fill.

Two mitigating points are fair. Wine's marketing is genuinely less aggressive than that of spirits and ready-to-drink brands: it is directed largely at existing drinkers, it does not target the youngest legal cohort with the same intensity, and the category has not built products whose format encourages fast intake. And the on-trade and retail links have taken server training, age verification and drink-driving campaigns seriously over three decades, with measurable results. The failure is concentrated at the point where responsibility would cost the industry volume — the label — rather than across the whole domain.

## Where the industry is most exposed

Four challenges would be hard to answer. First, **health and honesty**: that wine trades on a health halo it knows the evidence no longer supports. Second, **the premium-commodity gap**: that certified, low-yield, hand-worked viticulture is concentrated in wines that represent a small fraction of volume, while the bulk of what is drunk is farmed conventionally and shipped in heavy glass. Third, **water**: that irrigating a non-essential luxury crop in water-stressed catchments is difficult to justify to a community rationing supply. Fourth, **labour**: that the romance of the estate obscures a workforce that is seasonal, low-paid and often undocumented.

Context matters here, and it cuts both ways. Against **tobacco**, wine is plainly better: it has no equivalent of a product harmful at any dose with no legitimate use, and it has not run comparable disinformation campaigns. Against **brewing and spirits**, wine is broadly comparable on marketing conduct and behind on packaging weight and on low- and no-alcohol innovation. Against **beef, dairy and palm oil**, wine's land-use footprint and biodiversity record are markedly better. Against **fast fashion**, wine's supply chains are shorter and far more traceable. The fair conclusion is that wine sits in the better half of consumer-goods industries on environment and traceability, and squarely in the middle on labour and product honesty — and that it is very much less responsible than its own self-image.

## Conclusion

The wine industry is moderately responsible: genuinely good at stewarding the land it owns, adequate and improving on labour where regulators and buyers have forced the issue, and weakest where responsibility costs it money or market share — packaging weight, water in stressed catchments, and honesty about alcohol. Its defining problem is not misconduct but asymmetry. Responsibility is concentrated in the producers with the smallest volumes and the loudest voices, while the wine most people actually drink is made and shipped under conditions nobody markets. Until the industry is accountable at the commodity end — measured, audited and disclosed rather than asserted — its claim to be a responsible industry rests on a rounding error of its own production.
