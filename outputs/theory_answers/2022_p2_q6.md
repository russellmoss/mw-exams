## Definition and scope

Premature oxidation is the appearance of oxidative characters — deepening gold, honey, bruised apple, dried fruit, and the loss of primary fruit and tension — at an age far earlier than the wine's provenance and structure predict, typically between four and eight years for wines expected to hold fifteen. It is distinguished from normal oxidative development by its speed, and from cork taint by its pattern: it appears within a case, bottle to bottle, which is itself a diagnostic clue.

My scope is high-quality still Chardonnay: white Burgundy, above all the Côte de Beaune, and its premium New World analogues. I exclude commercial-tier Chardonnay, other varieties, and Champagne — whose long ageing under crown cap on tirage lees is a different oxidative regime and not a premox problem.

I take the question as an analysis of winemaking options, not as an essay on excluding oxygen, because the evidence points to the wine's *antioxidant capacity* mattering at least as much as the oxygen dose.

## The chemistry

Molecular oxygen does not react directly with wine phenolics. It must be activated: iron in the Fe(II)/Fe(III) couple, with copper as co-catalyst, reduces oxygen to hydrogen peroxide, which then oxidises ethanol to acetaldehyde and o-diphenols to quinones. Quinones polymerise to brown pigments and bind thiols; acetaldehyde drives the aldehydic, sherried character. Two consequences follow. First, metal content matters, so bentonite, copper additions and equipment contamination are part of the problem. Second, the wine's reservoir of reducing agents — SO2, glutathione and phenolics themselves — determines how much oxygen it can absorb before damage shows.

## Pressing and phenolic extraction

This is the lever most often neglected, and the one I would address first. Whole-bunch pressing at low pressure, with short cycles and minimal skin contact, limits the extraction of hydroxycinnamic acids such as caftaric acid and of flavanols such as catechin and the procyanidins. That orthodoxy is double-edged. Phenolics are the substrate for browning and bitterness, but they are also the wine's own antioxidant buffer: strip them out with hyper-protective, ultra-gentle handling and the wine has almost nothing but SO2 standing between it and the oxygen it will meet.

The alternative is controlled must oxidation, or hyperoxidation: allow the juice to oxidise before fermentation so that the readily oxidisable phenolics polymerise and precipitate, and remove them at settling. What remains is a wine that is intrinsically more stable, at the cost of some aromatic lift. For a wine intended for fifteen years I would accept moderate juice oxidation over total protection, and press for a modest, not minimal, phenolic load — press fractions being the practical control.

## Turbidity, glutathione and lees

Over-clarified juice ferments poorly, produces more hydrogen sulphide and yields a thinner, less protected wine; a target of the order of 100-250 NTU keeps enough solids to support the ferment. Glutathione, a tripeptide antioxidant carried from the grape, traps caftaric quinones as grape reaction product and preserves varietal thiols. It is preserved by early SO2 at the press, limited must oxygen, and lees contact, since yeast release it during autolysis; glutathione-rich inactivated yeast preparations are permitted in the EU for the same purpose.
Lees are an oxygen consumer and a glutathione source, so full lees contact is protective — but every *bâtonnage* is an oxygen addition, and the retreat from aggressive stirring in the Côte de Beaune is one of the clearest practical responses to the problem. Fewer rackings, silicone bungs, disciplined topping, minimal transfers and lower-oxygen barrels (older wood, larger format) all reduce cumulative ingress.

## pH and SO2, which must be handled together

pH governs ageing directly: high pH accelerates oxidation, weakens microbial protection and gives a flabbier wine. It also governs SO2 efficacy, because only molecular SO2 is active, and the molecular fraction roughly halves for every 0.3 units of pH increase. A Chardonnay at pH 3.5 therefore needs far more free SO2 than one at pH 3.2 for identical protection — which is a direct argument for earlier picking, tartaric addition, and either blocking or only partially completing malolactic fermentation.

On rates and timing: SO2 at the press to inhibit tyrosinase; then, because oxidation consumes roughly 4 mg/L of SO2 for every 1 mg/L of dissolved oxygen, free SO2 must be measured through élevage rather than assumed. In practice that means a check after fermentation and MLF, and free SO2 restored to roughly 25-35 mg/L at each racking, adjusted upward for higher pH. Before bottling, the target is best set as roughly 0.5-0.8 mg/L molecular SO2 rather than as a fixed free figure, and dissolved oxygen at bottling should be minimised by inert gas sparging of bottle and headspace.

## Closure and package

The problem was inseparable from cork. Natural cork's oxygen transmission varies widely between individual corks, which explains the bottle-to-bottle variation within a case. Technical corks with specified and consistent OTR, screwcaps with a chosen liner, and glass stoppers all convert an uncontrolled variable into a specified one. Total package oxygen — headspace plus closure plus dissolved — is the number worth managing.

## Which Chardonnays are most at risk, and my conclusion

The vulnerable profile is a ripe, high-pH, fully malolactic, very gently pressed and low-phenolic wine, stirred vigorously on lees, held at low free SO2, and sealed under variable natural cork. The options that most reduce risk, in order: pick and adjust for lower pH, accept a moderate phenolic and glutathione reservoir at pressing, manage SO2 to molecular targets rather than habit, stir less, and specify the closure. Oxygen exclusion alone was never sufficient, because the wines that failed were often the most protected of all.
