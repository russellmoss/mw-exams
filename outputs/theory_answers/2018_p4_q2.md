## Definition

By private label I mean wine sold under a brand owned by the retailer or the distributor rather than by the producer, made to that owner's specification by a third party. It covers the tiered grocery ranges - Tesco Finest and Sainsbury's Taste the Difference in the UK, Aldi's Exquisite Collection, Costco's Kirkland Signature - and the discounter and club labels such as Trader Joe's Charles Shaw, made by Bronco Wine Company. I distinguish it from two neighbours that are often confused with it: an *exclusive* brand, which is the producer's own label sold through one customer only, and a *licensed* brand, where the retailer rents someone else's name. In private label the customer owns the brand equity, and that single fact drives every advantage and disadvantage below.

## Wineries

**Advantages.** Private label is volume without marketing spend. A winery secures a defined quantity at a known date, which lets it plan harvest intake, fill tank space and amortise plant it has already built; a bottling line running at capacity is worth more than a higher price on half the throughput. It gives access to customers a small producer could never reach on its own listing merit, and it is a route into markets - Sweden through Systembolaget, Ontario through the LCBO - where shelf space is rationed. It also allows a winery to move surplus without discounting its own brand, protecting its price architecture, and it generates working capital and information: retailers share sales data most producers cannot otherwise buy.

**Disadvantages.** The winery is a contract manufacturer. It builds no brand equity, so it cannot capture the consumer relationship it is helping to create, and it is competing to make a product that sits on the shelf next to and cheaper than its own. Margins are set by tender and compress every renegotiation. Dependence is the real danger: a producer with a large share of turnover in one private-label contract has handed a customer the power to close it, and delisting is a single meeting. There is also a reputational cost, since a retailer will normally not name the source, and quality control is dictated by another party's specification.

## Distributors

**Advantages.** In markets where the distributor holds title and territory, a control label is the most profitable line it carries: it owns the margin at both wholesale and brand level, and it cannot be arbitraged, because no competing distributor can quote the same item. In the American three-tier system this matters enormously - a wholesaler's exclusive or control brands are the defence against a supplier moving its agency elsewhere. Private label also fills gaps in a portfolio at price points suppliers will not serve.

**Disadvantages.** The distributor takes on functions it is not built for: sourcing, specification, quality assurance, label compliance and inventory risk. It carries the stock, and unsold private label cannot be returned to anyone. It also creates channel conflict, because the branded suppliers whose agencies it depends on can see it competing with them. In markets that have disintermediated - UK grocery buys largely direct from producers, and monopolies tender direct - the distributor's private-label role has been squeezed out altogether.

## Retailers

**Advantages.** Higher percentage margin, price control, and differentiation. A private label cannot be price-compared, which removes the consumer's ability to check the shelf price against another chain and is the single most valuable property it has. It builds retailer brand equity, secures supply continuity, and allows precise specification to a customer profile. Aldi and Lidl have built their entire wine credibility this way; Woolworths' Pinnacle Drinks and Coles' exclusive liquor brands did the same in Australian retail.

**Disadvantages.** The retailer inherits the risk it removed from the producer: sourcing in a short vintage, quality failure and recall liability, and the reputational consequence of a fault, which attaches to the fascia rather than to an anonymous winery. It needs technical buying capability. Over-extension can hollow out the fixture, because consumers still come to a wine aisle for names they recognise, and brands are what drive footfall and trade-up. And the model is vulnerable to exactly the supply squeeze of the past three years: when bulk prices rise, the private-label specification cannot absorb it and the retailer must reformulate or take the margin hit.

## Conclusion

The advantages and disadvantages are not symmetrical, and that is the point worth making. Private label moves brand equity and pricing power towards the retailer and moves volume risk towards the winery, while the distributor is the party whose role it most often eliminates. It is therefore a good business for a winery only as a deliberate, capped use of spare capacity, and a bad one as a strategy - because the winery is funding the growth of the asset that will eventually replace it.
