## What brand loyalty is, and why wine has so little of it

Brand loyalty has two components and conflating them is the reason most answers to this question go wrong. **Behavioural loyalty** is repeat purchase, which in wine is often nothing more than habit and availability. **Attitudinal loyalty** is commitment: a willingness to seek the brand out, to pay more for it, and to refuse a substitute when it is out of stock. Only the second is worth anything, because only the second gives pricing power.

Wine is structurally a low-loyalty category. Consumers buy from a repertoire, treat variety, region and price as the navigation system, and switch brands constantly and cheerfully; novelty is part of the pleasure, so switching is not dissatisfaction. The Ehrenberg-Bass tradition in marketing science makes the further point that loyalty metrics track penetration — big brands have more buyers who buy slightly more often, and small brands cannot buy their way past that with engagement. Applied here, that means most wine brands' realistic ambition on social media is mental availability, not loyalty.

So my answer is a qualified yes, and the qualification is the whole argument: social media can drive genuine loyalty only where the brand owns the transaction and the customer's identity. Everywhere else it drives awareness, and calling that loyalty is a category error.

## The three conditions loyalty needs

**1. A reason to return that is not price.** Community, identity, access, or information the buyer values. A discount code trains a customer to wait for the next discount.

**2. A channel that reaches the same identified person again.** This is where social media is weaker than it looks. Followers are a rented audience; the platform's algorithm, not the brand, decides who sees a post, and reach on an unpaid post is a fraction of the follower base. The owned assets — email lists, club memberships, a private messaging group — are the loyalty infrastructure. Social media's honest job is to feed them.

**3. A repeat-purchase mechanism the brand controls.** Loyalty that cannot be transacted is affection. If the buyer's next purchase happens in a supermarket aisle where the brand is invisible and on promotion, engagement upstream will not survive the shelf.

## Where it does work

**Direct-to-consumer wineries with clubs.** The North American DTC and wine-club model satisfies all three conditions: the winery knows the customer's name, purchase history and preferences, sells to them repeatedly, and uses social channels to sustain the relationship between shipments. Here loyalty is measurable — club retention and churn, repeat rate, lifetime value — and social media demonstrably contributes to it.

**Community-owned business models.** Naked Wines built its business on exactly this logic: a subscription that funds named growers, with customers cast as investors in the winemakers rather than buyers of a label. The community is the product, the social channels sustain it, and the economics are retention economics. It is a better example than any campaign because it shows loyalty designed into the model rather than bolted on.

**China's closed-loop social commerce.** WeChat is the sharpest case internationally, because discovery, community, customer service and payment sit inside one app. A brand running private groups is engaging with identified individuals it can reach again and sell to directly — the three conditions in a single channel. Western platforms separate them, which is precisely why loyalty is harder to build on Instagram than in a WeChat group.

**Producer-as-person brands.** Small growers whose social presence is a recognisable individual convert followers into mailing-list buyers efficiently, because the parasocial relationship supplies the attitudinal component that a corporate brand struggles to manufacture.

## Where it does not

A supermarket brand at £7. The retailer owns the shopper relationship and the data; the brand cannot identify, contact or reward its buyers; the purchase is driven by promotional depth and shelf position. Social spend there buys reach and salience, both worth having, but the loyalty claim is unfounded. The same applies to most on-trade-led brands, where the sommelier, not the drinker, chooses.

## How I would judge whether it is working

Engagement rate, follower count and impressions are vanity metrics and their popularity is why this question is worth asking. The defensible measures are repeat purchase rate, club or subscription retention and churn, customer acquisition cost against lifetime value, share of category requirements, and — the sharpest test — whether identified social-acquired customers buy at full price rather than on promotion. If a brand cannot report those, it does not know whether it has loyalty.

## Conclusion

Yes, social media can drive brand loyalty in wine, but only for the minority of the category that has a direct commercial relationship with the drinker, and only when social is treated as the top of an owned-channel funnel rather than as the destination. For the large majority of wine, sold through retailers who own the customer, social media builds mental availability — valuable, but a different thing. The practical recommendation follows: before investing in content, invest in the mechanism that lets you sell to the same person twice. Without it, the engagement is rented and the loyalty is imaginary.
