## What an enzyme is, and where they come from

An enzyme is a protein that catalyses a specific biochemical reaction, lowering its activation energy without being consumed. It acts on one substrate at an active site, and its rate depends on temperature, pH, substrate concentration and the presence of inhibitors. Two practical consequences follow immediately: enzymes work slowly at cellar temperatures, so timing matters, and because they are proteins they are themselves adsorbed and denatured by bentonite — which is why enzyme should be added before fining, not after.

Three sources matter in a winery. **Endogenous grape enzymes**, some useful and some destructive. **Microbial enzymes** from yeast and lactic acid bacteria, which do most of the chemistry of fermentation. And **added commercial preparations**, most of them produced from *Aspergillus niger*, sold as pectinases with defined side activities and permitted under national additive lists. I take the winemaking process in order.

## Reception and pressing: the spoilage enzymes first

The grape's own oxidative enzymes are the first thing a winemaker must control. **Tyrosinase**, a polyphenol oxidase present in healthy fruit, oxidises hydroxycinnamic acids and is largely inactivated by modest SO2 and removed with the solids. **Laccase**, produced by *Botrytis cinerea*, is the serious one: it is far more resistant to sulphur dioxide than tyrosinase, remains active at wine pH and in alcohol, and attacks a much wider substrate range including anthocyanins, so a rotten load will brown and lose colour in tank despite normal sulphiting. The management is therefore not chemical but physical — sort rot out, press quickly, hyperoxidise the juice so the substrate is consumed and precipitated before fermentation, or denature the enzyme with heat, as flash détente does by heating must to around 85C. Any candidate who discusses only added enzymes has missed the enzyme that most often costs money.

## Pre-fermentation in white and rosé: clarification

Grape pectin holds colloids in suspension and makes juice viscous. Added **pectolytic enzymes** — polygalacturonase, pectin lyase and pectin methylesterase — depolymerise it, giving faster and more complete cold settling, better free-run yield, easier pressing and a juice that will filter later. In rosé the requirement is subtly different: the winemaker wants rapid settling and a delicate colour, so the preparation must be free of anthocyanase activity, since a glycosidase that liberates aroma will also strip the little colour a rosé has.

**Aroma-release enzymes** with beta-glucosidase side activity hydrolyse the odourless glycosidic precursors of monoterpenes, freeing free terpenes in Muscat, Gewurztraminer and Riesling. They are used on skin contact or post-fermentation, and their limitation is that they release everything, including compounds better left bound.

## Red maceration: the neglected half

Enzymes are used at least as much in red winemaking. **Extraction preparations** combine pectinase with cellulase and hemicellulase side activities to degrade the skin cell walls, releasing anthocyanins and tannin faster and more completely. The practical results are a shorter maceration for the same extraction, better press yields, improved colour intensity in the first days, and the release of cell-wall polysaccharides that help stabilise colour and soften the mid-palate. The trade-off is loss of control: an over-dosed red extracts harsh seed and skin tannin along with the colour.

## Alcoholic fermentation: the yeast's own enzyme suite

Fermentation is enzymology. Yeast invertase hydrolyses sucrose — which is what actually happens when a must is chaptalised — and the glycolytic enzymes convert hexoses to pyruvate, with pyruvate decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase completing the conversion to ethanol. Strain differences that winemakers describe as aromatic are enzymatic: **beta-lyase** cleaves cysteine-bound precursors to release the volatile thiols of Sauvignon Blanc, and alcohol acetyltransferases form the acetate esters, balanced against esterases that hydrolyse them back. Yeast beta-glucosidase also contributes to terpene release.

## Malolactic fermentation and lees ageing

The malolactic enzyme of *Oenococcus oeni* decarboxylates L-malic acid to L-lactic acid; the same organism's citrate pathway generates diacetyl, which its diacetyl reductase then reduces to acetoin and butanediol — which is why leaving a wine on lees after malolactic reduces the buttery character rather than increasing it.

On lees, autolysis is enzymatic: yeast proteases and glucanases break down the dead cells and release mannoproteins and polysaccharides, giving texture, protein and tartrate stabilisation and colour stabilisation. Added **beta-glucanase** preparations accelerate this, and also degrade the botrytis glucans that would otherwise blind a filter.

## Pre-bottling and the risks

Before bottling, pectinase and glucanase preparations are used to restore filterability. **Urease** is permitted in some markets to hydrolyse urea and reduce ethyl carbamate risk. **Lysozyme**, itself an enzyme, hydrolyses the cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria and is used to inhibit or delay lactic acid bacteria, allowing a lower SO2 regime.

Three risks are worth stating. Preparations are proteins and add to the protein haze load. Over-dosing pectin methylesterase liberates methanol, which is legally capped. And preparations carrying **cinnamyl esterase** side activity release free hydroxycinnamic acids, the exact substrate *Brettanomyces* converts into 4-ethylphenol — so a cinnamyl-esterase-free preparation must be specified for any red destined for long élevage.

## Conclusion

Enzymes act at every stage, but their role is consistent: they accelerate or complete reactions that would otherwise happen slowly or not at all. They save time, yield and energy, and they can rescue filterability or aroma release. What they do not do is create style — and the enzymes with the largest effect on quality are the ones the winemaker never adds, but must exclude.
