## Why: three jobs, one molecule

SO2 does three distinct things, and the dose is set by whichever job dominates. It is **antioxidant**, though indirectly: it scavenges hydrogen peroxide produced as phenolics oxidise, so it intercepts the oxidation cascade rather than reacting with oxygen quickly itself. It is **antioxidasic**, denaturing polyphenol oxidase and, more importantly, botrytis laccase. And it is **antimicrobial** against wild yeast, lactic acid bacteria, acetic acid bacteria and Brettanomyces. It also binds acetaldehyde, which both removes a stale aroma and permanently ties up that portion of the SO2.

Which is why the only figure that matters is molecular SO2, not total or even free. Molecular SO2 is the antimicrobial form, and the target is around 0.5-0.8 mg/L, but the proportion of free SO2 in that form is governed by pH: at pH 3.0 roughly 6% of free SO2 is molecular, at pH 3.5 about 2% and at pH 4.0 under 1%. A high-pH Grenache therefore needs several times the free SO2 of a low-pH Riesling for the same protection — and may not be able to reach it inside EU totals of 150 mg/L for dry red and 200 mg/L for dry white, with 400 mg/L for some botrytised sweet wines. That single calculation should drive every addition, and in practice it often does not.

## When, and at what rate

**Receival and crush:** 20-30 mg/L on clean, cool, hand-picked fruit and 50 mg/L or more where fruit is split, rotten or machine-harvested warm, principally to knock down laccase and spoilage flora. Omitted entirely for carbonic maceration or an intended ambient ferment.

**Cold soak:** the stage most people forget. A red held at 8-12C for three or four days is an ideal medium for acetic acid bacteria and Kloeckera, so 30-50 mg/L, or the deliberate decision to run with none and accept the microbial risk for aromatic complexity.

**Juice settling in whites:** 30-50 mg/L after settling, which also suppresses indigenous yeast and buys clean juice for a chosen inoculum.

**Fermentation:** none. It would inhibit the yeast and drive H2S formation.

**Post-MLF:** the critical addition, because the wine is at its most vulnerable — no sugar, no competitive flora, warm. An addition of 30-60 mg/L to reach 25-35 mg/L free, made immediately malic acid tests clear.

**Barrel ageing:** the other forgotten stage. Oxygen ingress and evaporation erode free SO2 continuously, so check and adjust at every racking, topping up to hold 25-35 mg/L free, and sterilise empty barrels with a burnt sulphur disc.

**Bottling:** adjust to 30-40 mg/L free for a dry white and 25-35 mg/L for a dry red; sweet wines need considerably more, at 0.8 mg/L molecular or above, to hold the sugar.

## How: forms, dosing and measurement

Potassium metabisulphite is about 57% available SO2, and is the small-winery default — but it must be dissolved in water or wine first and added during a pump-over or racking, because dumped dry into a tank it stratifies and locally over-sulphurs. A 5-10% aqueous PMS solution dosed volumetrically is more accurate. Larger wineries use liquid SO2 from a cylinder, metered by an inline dosing pump, and a sulphitometer on the crush pad to dose incoming fruit proportionally to flow. Barrels take sulphur discs or effervescent tablets. Measurement is by aeration-oxidation for free and total SO2 — the reliable method — with Ripper titration acceptable for whites but distorted in reds by phenolics; molecular SO2 is then calculated from free SO2 and pH.

Safety is the part almost nobody writes about. SO2 gas is acutely toxic, corrosive to eyes and airways, denser than air, and pools in tank pits, sumps and empty vessels — it is immediately dangerous to life at around 100 ppm, and occupational limits are in the low parts per million. Sulphuring a barrel room, or entering a recently sulphured or CO2-purged tank without atmosphere testing, forced ventilation and a confined-space permit, kills people. PMS powder is itself a respiratory irritant. And SO2 is not an inert gas: blanketing fruit or headspace is a job for CO2, nitrogen or argon, never for SO2.

## Are winemakers playing it too safe?

My view is that the profession is not over-dosing so much as dosing badly. Total SO2 usage has fallen substantially, and the real fault is imprecision: additions made to a habitual number rather than calculated from measured pH and free SO2, which simultaneously over-sulphurs low-pH whites and leaves high-pH reds unprotected. Timing is the second fault — small early additions are far more efficient than a large corrective one, which mostly binds.

Where I do think caution is excessive is at bottling, where fear of a stray refermentation produces sulphurous young whites that would show better fruit at 25 mg/L free. But the alternatives are partial, not substitutes. Rigorous hygiene, inert gas handling, temperature control and sterile filtration are the genuine ones. Ascorbic acid is antioxidant only and pro-oxidant without SO2 present; lysozyme acts on lactic bacteria alone; chitosan targets Brettanomyces; DMDC sterilises at bottling but has no residual effect; bioprotective non-Saccharomyces yeasts at receival occupy the niche without protecting the finished wine. None combines all three functions at a few cents per bottle. The low-sulphur category proves the point — its best wines are exhilarating and its failure rate, in VA and mousiness, is uncomfortably high.
