## Loyalty, and the two things I am answering

Loyalty has two components and wine performs badly on both. Behavioural loyalty is repeat purchase; attitudinal loyalty is the commitment that makes a consumer refuse the substitute. Wine buyers show occasional repeat behaviour and almost no attachment, because they shop the category by grape, country, price point and occasion rather than by brand — most consumers cannot name the producer of the last bottle they drank. Brand managers are therefore not defending loyalty; they are trying to manufacture it in a category structurally hostile to it.

I confine myself to the two elements the question sets: the conditions of today's market, and their effect on loyalty. Questions of production, appellation law or long-run category history are outside it. And because loyalty is built quite differently at 8 euros and at 80, I segment before I assess.

## Mainstream and mass-market brands

Here the challenge is that the retailer, not the brand, owns the customer. A large supermarket may carry several hundred wines and rationalises that range annually, so a brand can be delisted out of a consumer's life regardless of how the consumer feels about it. Brands such as Yellow Tail, Barefoot and Casillero del Diablo have solved recognition at scale, which is real and rare, but recognition is not commitment.

The deeper problem is that the tool used to defend volume destroys the asset. Deep multibuy promotion in UK, Australian and US grocery has trained consumers to buy whatever is on deal this week; the loyalty being built is to the discount. Own-label compounds it, because the retailer can copy a successful style at a lower price without the brand's cost base. And private-label growth in a cost-of-living market is currently the strongest force in mainstream wine.

Alongside this sits category contraction. Volumes are falling in most mature markets, younger consumers drink less, and moderation, no-and-low alternatives, hard seltzers and premium spirits take share of occasion. A brand manager is trying to build loyalty in a shrinking pool of drinkers who are increasingly loyal to a *behaviour* — drinking less — rather than to a competing product.

## Premium and craft brands

At 15 to 40 euros the consumer is engaged but promiscuous, and engagement itself is the problem: discovery is the pleasure. Wine media, retail and sommelier culture all reward novelty, so the interested drinker who bought your Etna Rosso last month buys Jura this month, and considers that loyalty to a taste, not disloyalty to you.

The second challenge is acquisition economics. Reaching this consumer now means paid social and search, where costs have risen sharply, targeting has been degraded by privacy changes on mobile platforms, and organic reach on any platform can be cut by an algorithm change overnight. Brands rent an audience they never own. Influencer and sommelier advocacy is effective but rents too: the advocate moves on. 19 Crimes recruited younger drinkers largely through packaging and an app, and the lesson is instructive — novelty mechanics recruit brilliantly and retain poorly.

## Luxury and allocated brands

Counter-intuitively, the strongest brands face the sharpest loyalty risk, because their challenge is supply and price rather than attention. When a wine's price runs ahead of its drinkers, the loyal customer is priced out and replaced by an investor or a status buyer who leaves as soon as the market turns — that is trade, not loyalty. Allocation cuts after small vintages force the same choice: cutting a long-standing private client to serve a new market permanently damages the relationship. Secondary-market speculation and counterfeit exposure sit on top. Whispering Angel shows the opposite risk at the fashionable end: rapid recruitment on a category trend that the brand does not control.

## The cross-cutting shift: direct to consumer and buy local

The most consequential development for loyalty is that brands of every size are trying to reach the consumer directly — cellar door, club, e-commerce, marketplace — because a distributor relationship gives the brand a shipment number and no customer. DTC is the only route that yields a name, a purchase history and a reason to make a second sale, and it is where genuine attitudinal loyalty is actually being built: wine clubs, allocation lists and membership models. It is also the hardest thing on this list to execute, requiring CRM capability, fulfilment, and in the United States a shipping compliance regime state by state.

Running with it is the buy-local trend — English sparkling, US state wines, Nordic and Japanese domestic drinking. For a domestic producer it is the cheapest loyalty available. For an imported brand it is a direct threat, because provenance loyalty beats brand loyalty, and channel conflict makes DTC dangerous for anyone dependent on a distributor.

## Conclusion

The biggest challenge is that in today's market brand managers do not own the customer relationship: retailers, platforms and distributors sit between them, and each of those intermediaries profits from substitution. That is why promotion-trained switching in mainstream, rented attention in premium, and price-driven churn in luxury are all symptoms of one problem, and why the direct channel — expensive, slow, and the only route that produces a name — is where I would put the effort.
