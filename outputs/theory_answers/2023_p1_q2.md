## Scope: an event, not a climate

Excessive precipitation is rainfall that exceeds what the soil can drain and the vine can use, judged against the phenological stage it falls in. Defined that way it is not a property of wet regions at all. Mendoza can lose a crop to summer hail and downpour; Marlborough's threat is a tropical depression in March; the Douro's is a wet flowering. A dry region that takes a quarter of its annual rainfall in the week of flowering has an excess; a wet region taking the same total spread through winter does not. My position is that the threat is severe but sharply asymmetric: the damage is determined almost entirely by timing and by soil drainage, not by seasonal total, and the manageable share is smaller than growers assume because the two decisive levers — drainage and canopy architecture — are set years before the rain falls.

## Structure and soil

Heavy rain on slopes moves soil. Erosion is the permanent form of the damage: Douro terraces, Mosel and Priorat slopes and the Northern Rhône's granite lose topsoil and, in extreme events, whole walls and rows. On flat sites the equivalent is waterlogging. Saturated soil is anaerobic, root respiration stops, fine roots die, and the vine shows water stress symptoms in a wet season — a diagnosis growers routinely get wrong. Compaction is the insidious one: tractor passes on wet soil destroy structure, reduce infiltration, and make the next wet season worse.

Almost all of this is answered before planting rather than during the season. Subsurface tile drainage, deep ripping and planting on raised mounds are standard in Oregon and across New Zealand for exactly this reason. On slopes, contour planting, terracing, grassed alleys and permanent cover hold the soil in place. Rootstock choice matters too: Riparia Gloire and SO4 tolerate a moist profile far better than the drought-adapted 110R or 140Ru, which are the wrong choice on a site that floods.

## Leaching

Free-draining soils under heavy rain lose mobile nutrients. Nitrate is the most mobile and the first to go, which is why a wet spring on Marlborough's stony Wairau soils or Médoc gravel produces low yeast assimilable nitrogen and sluggish fermentations months later. Sulphur, boron, magnesium and potassium follow on sandy soils, and sustained leaching of calcium and magnesium acidifies the soil over years, reducing availability of other nutrients. Management is straightforward once anticipated: split nitrogen into smaller applications timed to demand, use cover crops to capture nitrate over winter, build organic matter to raise exchange capacity, lime to correct drift in pH, and use foliar applications when the root zone is not delivering.

## Operations — the compounding failure

The gravest risk is not the rain but the fact that it arrives exactly when work is needed. Disease pressure peaks and the sprayer cannot enter the block; spray intervals stretch past protection and a preventive programme becomes a curative one. Mowing, shoot positioning and leaf removal are delayed while vigour is at its highest. Hand harvesting stops, machines rut the rows, and fruit sits. Any management plan must therefore be built around access: grassed alleys or permanent sward for trafficability, narrow or over-row tractors, and, in wet regions, aerial or high-clearance spraying capacity.

## Timing decides the severity

**Dormancy to budburst.** Largely benign and often beneficial — it recharges the profile. Risks are erosion, waterlogging and pruning access.

**Budburst to flowering.** The most damaging window. Free water on green tissue is required for downy mildew primary infection, and repeated wetting drives secondary cycles; black rot and phomopsis follow. Rain and cool weather at flowering cause poor fruit set, coulure and millerandage, and that yield loss is irreversible. Management: preventive spray timing keyed to a disease model rather than the calendar, systemic and translaminar products with resistance rotation, open canopies, shoot thinning early.

**Set to veraison.** Abundant water drives vigour: long shoots, laterals, a dense shaded canopy. The consequences are shaded fruit with high pyrazine and low anthocyanin, poor bud fruitfulness for next year, a humid microclimate favouring botrytis, and delayed ripening. Management: shoot thinning, leaf removal in the fruit zone, trimming, devigorating cover crops sown mid-row to compete for water and nitrogen, and, at replanting, devigorating rootstocks.

**Veraison to harvest.** Dilution of sugar, acid and flavour; berry splitting when a dry spell is broken by heavy rain; botrytis and sour rot with vinegar fly. Management: fruit-zone leaf removal and loose-clustered clones before the fact, botryticide timing at bunch closure, sorting at picking, and the willingness to pick early and accept lower ripeness rather than lose the crop.

## Conclusion

Excessive precipitation is a first-order threat in any climate, but its severity is set by two weeks of the season rather than by the season. It is manageable only in part. Drainage, site, rootstock, spacing, trellis and clone determine most of the outcome and are fixed at planting; within the season the grower has spray timing, canopy work and harvest decisions, and each of those depends on being able to get into the vineyard — which is precisely what the rain removes.
