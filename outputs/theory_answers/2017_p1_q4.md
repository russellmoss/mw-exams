## What I mean by dessert wine, and how I am choosing

A dessert wine is a wine made with the assumption of sweetness, spanning perhaps 30 g/L of residual sugar in a Kabinett-weight wine through 100–150 g/L in a Sauternes to well over 400 g/L in a Tokaji Eszencia or a Pedro Ximénez. That spread matters because the concentration route dictates the climate required, and the climate then dictates the variety. So I choose varieties by four criteria: retained acidity at very high sugar, the behaviour of the skin under the relevant concentration route, aromatic compound levels strong enough to survive concentration or fortification, and compatibility with the production method the climate allows. On those criteria my choices are Semillon, Riesling, Furmint, Chenin Blanc, Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains, Pedro Ximénez and the Douro's Touriga Nacional-led field blend. I discuss them by route rather than one by one.

## Humid autumn mornings, dry afternoons: noble rot

Botrytis cinerea concentrates sugar and acid while adding glycerol and botrytis character, but it needs a specific and rare climate: morning mist or fog to raise humidity, followed by warm, dry, breezy afternoons to check the infection. Continuous humidity gives grey rot instead. Sauternes has this from the confluence of the cool Ciron with the warmer Garonne; Tokaj from the Bodrog and Tisza; the Layon from its tributary valleys; the Rheingau and Mosel from river fog.

The varietal requirement is not one trait but several, and this is where candidates go wrong. **Semillon** is genuinely thin-skinned and permeable, so it rots readily and evenly, but it is only moderately acidic — which is why Sauternes blends it with Sauvignon Blanc for acid and aroma. **Furmint** is thin-skinned and very high in acid, so it carries 150 g/L of sugar without cloying, and its firm skin holds shape through the aszú soak. **Riesling** and **Chenin Blanc** succeed for a different reason: exceptional natural acidity and late, uneven ripening that lets pickers work successive tries through a long autumn. Icewine requires a reliable, sharp winter freeze after a long enough autumn to leave sound fruit on the vine, and in Germany, Austria and Canada the law defines it by a minimum must weight reached only once the grapes have frozen naturally -- in practice usually at about -7C or below -- rather than by a specific legal temperature threshold. The climatic requirement is continental: Ontario, British Columbia, Germany's warmer river valleys and Austria. The varietal requirement is thick, tough skin and a resilient stalk to survive months of hanging without rotting or dropping, plus high acidity to balance the sugar. **Riesling** is the benchmark; Vidal, a hybrid, is used across Ontario precisely because its skins are tougher still. Botrytis varieties chosen for permeability are the wrong plants for this job — the same trait that makes Semillon ideal in Sauternes makes it a liability hanging until January.

## Hot and dry: raisining on the vine and on the mat

Where autumn is hot, dry and sunny, water is lost by evaporation rather than fungus. Passerillage on the vine and drying on mats or in lofts both demand varieties with thick, resistant skins that will dehydrate without splitting or rotting, and enough acidity that the concentrate is not flat. **Pedro Ximénez** in Montilla-Moriles is the clearest case of climate dictating both variety and method: a neutral, high-sugar-accumulating grape laid on esparto mats under a reliably rainless Andalusian sun, producing a wine of enormous sweetness whose interest is raisin and fig rather than varietal aroma. Italy's dried-grape tradition works the same way with **Garganega** for Recioto di Soave and **Corvina** for Recioto della Valpolicella, both chosen for loose bunches and thick skins that resist rot in the drying loft.

## Fortification: a different demand again

Ignoring fortified wine here would be a serious omission, since much of the world's sweet wine is fortified. Arresting fermentation with spirit means the wine keeps grape sugar, so the variety must supply intense flavour and, for reds, colour and tannin extracted very quickly before the spirit goes in. **Touriga Nacional**, with Touriga Franca and Tinta Roriz, gives the Douro small berries, thick skins and high anthocyanin — exactly what a two-day maceration requires — and the Douro's hot, dry summers ripen them to the necessary sugar. **Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains** is the white counterpart: its terpene concentration is high enough to survive fortification and years of oxidative ageing, which is why it appears in Rivesaltes, Beaumes-de-Venise, Samos and, as the red-berried Brown Muscat, in Rutherglen — all hot, dry places where it would rot if the autumn were damp.

## Conclusion

The relationship runs in both directions, and the best choices show it. Semillon adapted to the Sauternes niche over centuries, while in Montilla-Moriles the climate dictated a method and Pedro Ximénez was adapted to it. Judged strictly on suitability, I would name Riesling and Furmint as the most versatile — high acid, capable of botrytis, passerillage and, for Riesling, freeze concentration — Semillon as the best specialist for noble rot, Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains and Pedro Ximénez for hot, dry fortified and raisined styles, and Touriga Nacional for sweet reds.
