## Two different operations, confused at the candidate's peril

**Fining** is a chemical and colloidal operation: an agent is added that binds a target compound by adsorption or opposite charge and is then removed with it. Bentonite adsorbs heat-unstable grape proteins; gelatin, isinglass, casein, egg albumen and pea or potato protein bind astringent and oxidisable phenolics; PVPP removes browning precursors; activated carbon removes colour and some taints. **Filtration** is a physical separation of suspended particles by size: depth filtration through diatomaceous earth or sheets, crossflow, and membrane filtration down to a nominal 0.45 micron for a sterile filtration. Neither substitutes for the other. No filter removes soluble protein, and no fining agent removes a yeast population reliably.

Assessing the claim on the label therefore means asking what the winemaker must do instead, and I read the question as a technical one, from vineyard through bottling.

## What the winemaker is giving up

Fining and filtration between them manage two risks. The first is **instability**: protein haze in white wine, tartrate precipitation, colloidal and colour instability in reds, and microbial instability from *Brettanomyces*, lactic acid bacteria, or residual sugar refermented by *Zygosaccharomyces bailii*. The second is **clarity and palate**: turbidity, sediment in bottle, and excess astringency or bitterness. Forgoing both moves those risks earlier in the process, where they must be designed out, and later into the bottle, where they cannot be corrected.

## Yield and fruit condition

The first substitution is in the vineyard. Lower yields and fully ripe, sound fruit give lower grape protein, softer and more polymerised tannin needing no protein fining, and less nitrogen-driven vegetal character. Rot must be excluded absolutely: botrytis brings laccase and beta-glucans, and glucans blind a filter and destabilise the wine, so a winemaker who intends not to filter must sort ruthlessly. Ripe phenolics also mean the wine will not need gelatin or albumen to soften it.

## Juice and must handling

Clarification techniques that are neither fining nor filtration remain available and become central: long cold settling, flotation, and centrifugation. Pectolytic enzymes added at the crusher degrade pectin so that solids settle rather than stay in suspension. Press fractions are separated and the hard, phenolic-rich later fractions declassified rather than fined out later. Gentler whole-bunch pressing lowers the solids load in the first place.

## Fermentation choices

The wine must be taken absolutely dry, because residual sugar is the substrate for refermentation in a bottle with no sterile filter. Malolactic fermentation must be complete and confirmed analytically, since residual malic acid in bottle is a slow bomb. Yeast selection matters for flocculation and for low polysaccharide production; a strain that settles compactly leaves a cleaner wine off lees.

## Elevage does the clarifying

This is the substitution the examiners look for. Time in vat or barrel replaces the filter. Successive rackings off gross and then fine lees, each with settling time, progressively clarify the wine; a cold winter in an unheated cellar precipitates potassium bitartrate as effectively as a chilled tank; and extended ageing polymerises tannin and pigment so that colloidal material falls out rather than remaining suspended. The implications are practical: tank and barrel space occupied for far longer, capital tied up, more handling labour, greater topping and racking losses, and more cumulative oxygen exposure, which itself has to be managed with careful topping, inert gas and sound cooperage.

## The sulphur dioxide regime becomes the whole microbial defence

Without a sterile membrane, molecular SO2 is the only barrier between the wine and *Brettanomyces*. The winemaker must hold roughly 0.5 to 0.8 mg/L of molecular SO2 throughout élevage, and because the molecular fraction collapses as pH rises, this forces attention onto pH: a wine at pH 3.9 needs several times the free SO2 of one at pH 3.4. Picking earlier, acidifying where legal, and blending to a lower pH therefore become part of the unfined, unfiltered decision. Supporting tools include lysozyme against lactic bacteria, DMDC at bottling, and scrupulous hygiene — steam or ozone sanitation, closed transfers, low dissolved oxygen — because an infection can no longer be filtered out.

## What is legal where

Permitted treatments are jurisdictional. Fining agents are set by permitted lists under OIV, EU and national rules; potassium ferrocyanide is permitted under strict oenologist supervision in the EU (no residue may remain), while in the United States it is essentially unused and is being formally delisted by TTB rather than ever having had a blanket in-force prohibition. Animal-derived agents — isinglass, casein and egg albumen — trigger allergen declaration in the EU, which is one reason vegetable proteins have displaced them. Organic and biodynamic certification narrows the list further, and some appellations and traditional codes discourage or effectively prohibit heavy processing. A winemaker choosing not to fine or filter is not escaping regulation, but the regulation now bears on additives such as DMDC, lysozyme and SO2 limits instead.

## Assessment

The implication is a different risk profile, not simply an omission. Decisions are pulled forward — yield, fruit health, pH, complete dryness — and cost is added in the form of extended élevage and a stricter SO2 and hygiene regime. My judgement is that this is a sound choice for a dry, low-pH, tannic red with two years in wood, where time genuinely substitutes for intervention. For an aromatic white with residual sugar, or for any high-pH warm-climate wine facing a container crossing the tropics, I would not forgo at least a coarse polishing filtration; the risk transferred to the bottle is too large to accept for a stylistic claim.
