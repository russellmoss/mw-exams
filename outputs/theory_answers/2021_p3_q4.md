## Definitions and scope

Quality assurance is the system built to prevent a defect: the HACCP plan, the written procedures, the supplier approval and audit regime, the training and the records that prove all of it happened. Quality control is the measurement that detects a defect: inspection, analysis and the sampling plan that governs them. An investigation of this kind uses both, and in a specific order — QC establishes the facts about this bottle and this batch, QA establishes whether the system that should have prevented it was working and where it failed.

The question asks only about investigating the complaint. I therefore do not write a recall plan; whether to withdraw or recall product is a decision that this investigation informs and that follows it. Nor is this the place to catalogue how glass gets into bottles: the sources of contamination matter only once the investigation points at one. Physical contamination by glass is a food safety hazard, not a quality preference, so the investigation is also a legal obligation — traceability and the ability to act on unsafe food are required of every food business, in the EU under Regulation (EC) 178/2002 and equivalently elsewhere — and there is a reputational exposure alongside it.

## Stage 1 — secure and verify the evidence (QC)

The first duty is to establish that there is in fact a glass shard in the bottle, because complaints are frequently tartrate crystals, closure fragments or capsule material.

I log the complaint immediately in the complaints register: date received, complainant, place and date of purchase, the lot code and best-before printed on the bottle and any code on the closure, and a description in the customer's own words. I ask for photographs before anything moves. I then retrieve the physical evidence — the bottle, its contents, the closure and, ideally, the packaging — by courier with a documented chain of custody and a signed receipt, rather than allowing it to be posted loose or discarded. The complainant is handled courteously and without admission of liability, and asked to retain any other bottles from the same purchase.

In the laboratory the object is examined and photographed under magnification, weighed and measured. Two questions decide the direction of the whole investigation. Is it glass at all, or crystal, closure or plastic? And if it is glass, is it from this bottle or foreign to it? The bottle is inspected for a corresponding fracture at the base, heel, shoulder or internal neck surface; the fill level, closure seating and any evidence of prior opening are recorded. Where the answer is not obvious, the shard's composition and colour are compared against the bottle by the glass supplier's own laboratory, which can usually determine whether the two are the same melt.

## Stage 2 — trace the batch (QA)

The lot code is the key to everything. It resolves to a bottling record, and from there I pull the complete file for that run: date, time, line, shift and operator; the glass supplier, delivery and pallet lot with its certificate of conformity; the depalletiser, rinser and bottle-inverter settings; in-line inspection or camera records; and the line clearance checks at the start and end of the run.

The critical documents are the HACCP records. Foreign body contamination by glass is a recognised hazard, so the plan should carry a control point covering it, with monitoring records, defined critical limits and a documented glass breakage procedure — typically requiring that any breakage on the line is logged and that a defined number of bottles either side is isolated and destroyed. I check whether that procedure was followed on the day, and whether any breakage was recorded near the time this bottle was filled. Complementing this, I check the glass register for damage to lighting, guards and instruments in the filling hall.

## Stage 3 — is it systemic? (QC and QA together)

I next establish scale, because a single random event and a batch defect demand different responses. I retrieve and inspect the library samples of the same lot, then arrange inspection of a meaningful sample of remaining stock in the winery's own warehouse and, if justified, at the distributor. I interrogate the complaints register for other reports against the same lot, supplier delivery or line, and I review the glass supplier's audit status and certification.

This is where practical logic matters. If the run finished months ago and the wine is in consumers' hands, stopping the bottling line achieves nothing; the useful actions are stock inspection and traceability. If the same glass delivery or the same line is still in use today, then quarantining that glass and halting the run are proportionate and urgent.

## Stage 4 — root cause, corrective action and closing out (QA)

The investigation ends in a documented report: the evidence, the findings, a formal root cause analysis using five-whys or a cause-and-effect diagram, and a conclusion on whether the failure lay with the glass supplier, the line, the procedure or the customer's account of events. From that follow corrective actions, a revision of the HACCP plan if the hazard analysis or its control limits proved inadequate, re-training or equipment change where indicated, and a verification step confirming the action worked. The complainant receives a substantive reply.

The report is also what the winery's management, its retail customers and, if necessary, the regulator will use to decide whether any wider action on product in trade is required — the decision this investigation exists to inform.
