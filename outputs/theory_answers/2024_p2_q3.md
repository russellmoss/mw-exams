## What the vessel has to do

A fermentation vessel is the container in which grape must is converted to wine by yeast. It is never a neutral box: it determines the wine's thermal behaviour, its oxygen exposure, the geometry of skin contact, what compounds it gains or loses from the walls, and how much labour a tonne of fruit costs to process. So the choice is made backwards, from the intended wine. For an aromatic, reductively handled Sauvignon Blanc the vessel must be inert, gas-tight and precisely temperature-controlled. For a structured Nebbiolo it must permit controlled oxygen and manageable cap work. For a high-volume commercial red it must be large, automated and quick to turn round. I take material first, then the factors that cut across all materials.

## Material, judged on oxygen and on flavour

**Stainless steel** is inert, gas-tight, weldable to any shape, and conducts heat well, so it takes cooling jackets and gives the tightest temperature control available. It preserves thiols and esters and is the default for aromatic whites and rosés and for clean, fruit-driven reds. Its weaknesses are the mirror image: no oxygen ingress at all, so reductive sulphides must be managed by nutrient additions, aeration at pump-over and lees stirring; and no textural softening, so the wine can taste linear.

**Oak** — barrel, vat or large foudre — supplies slow oxygen through staves and bung, plus hydrolysable tannin, vanillin and toasting-derived compounds if new. Oxygen polymerises tannin and stabilises colour; the wine gains breadth and ageing potential. In a 225 to 228 litre barrique the surface-to-volume ratio is high, so a barrel-fermented white gains lees contact, integrated oak and a naturally slow, cool ferment; in a large foudre the ratio is low, so the effect is oxygen without flavour, which is why traditional Barolo and Rhône producers use them. Costs are high, life is limited and cleaning is difficult.

**Concrete** combines oak's thermal inertia with near-neutral flavour. Unlined concrete allows slight oxygen transmission and buffers temperature so ferments run warm and even; the thick walls make the vessel slow to heat and slow to cool. Epoxy-lined concrete removes both the oxygen and the alkalinity risk but also the character. Egg-shaped tanks are chosen for the convection currents their form generates, which keeps lees in suspension without stirring. Concrete is very long-lived but immovable and hard to repair.

**Clay-based vessels** — Georgian qvevri buried to the neck, Iberian tinajas and talhas, modern terracotta amphorae — are porous unless lined with beeswax or resin, so they give oxygen ingress with no oak flavour, and burial gives excellent thermal stability. The small volumes and high surface-to-volume ratio give intense skin contact, which is why they suit textural skin-contact whites. They are fragile, hard to clean and awkward to fill and empty.

**Fibreglass** (glass-reinforced plastic) is cheap, light, inert and reasonably insulating, and remains widespread in Iberia and South America. Its weakness is durability: the gel coat scratches and crazes, and scratches harbour spoilage organisms.

**Breathable plastic**, typically high-density polyethylene formulated for a controlled oxygen transmission rate, is designed to deliver barrel-like oxygen ingress at a fraction of barrel cost and with no oak flavour. It is a good tool for mid-priced reds that need tannin softening but not oak. Small polyethylene bins serve the opposite purpose: cheap, movable fermenters for small lots.

## Thermal behaviour and fermentation kinetics

Fermentation generates substantial heat, and the vessel decides what happens to it. Conductive, jacketed stainless steel allows a white to be held cool for aroma retention or a red to be run warm for extraction and then dropped to protect it. Concrete and oak buffer rather than control, giving a smooth, slow curve that rarely stalls but cannot be dictated. Small vessels lose heat through their surface, so a barrel ferment stays cool without refrigeration, while a large tank of red needs active cooling or risks running away, sticking, and losing volatile aroma.

## Size, shape and extraction

For reds, geometry is extraction. A wide, shallow vessel — the granite lagar of the Douro is the archetype — presents a large cap area to a modest volume, so gentle foot treading or punching down achieves deep extraction. A tall, narrow tank has a small cap relative to volume and greater hydrostatic pressure, so it needs pumping over or rack-and-return to work the cap. Open tops allow punching down and let carbon dioxide escape; closed tanks retain it and protect against oxidation and acetic acid bacteria. Vessel size also determines how finely a winemaker can select: many small vessels allow parcel-by-parcel blending decisions later, at the cost of labour and floor space.

## Logistics, labour and cleaning

Every vessel must be filled, emptied, monitored and cleaned. Stainless steel accepts automated cleaning-in-place, sanitises easily and is the safest choice where microbial risk is high. Oak, concrete and clay all need manual entry, hot water or steam, and carry higher spoilage risk. Automation matters at volume: rotary fermenters and automated pump-over systems remove almost all cap-management labour and shorten cycle times, decisive for a winery turning tanks several times in a vintage.

## Cost, durability and sustainability

Stainless steel is a high capital cost with a very long life and full recyclability, but it commits the winery to refrigeration energy. Oak is a recurring cost with a working life of years, not decades. Concrete has high embodied carbon but may outlast the winery. Plastics and fibreglass are cheap and short-lived, and are a waste stream. Water consumption in cleaning is a real and growing consideration across all of them.

## Conclusion

The key factors are the vessel's oxygen transmission, its flavour contribution, its thermal behaviour, its geometry as it governs extraction, and its cost in capital, labour and cleaning. Choosing well means starting from the wine — aromatic preservation, textural build, tannin polymerisation or throughput — and selecting the vessel that delivers it most reliably at the price the wine can bear.
