## What pH is, and the ranges that frame the decisions

pH is the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion concentration, so it measures the strength of a wine's acidity rather than its quantity — a logarithmic scale on which a shift of one unit is a tenfold change in acid strength. That is worth one sentence and no more; the point of the question is what pH makes the winemaker do. Working ranges by style are approximately 2.9-3.1 for sparkling base wines, 3.1-3.4 for dry whites and 3.3-3.8 for reds, with fortified and warm-climate reds sitting at or above the top of that band. I take the stages in order, because pH is not a single decision but a constraint that reappears, differently, at every one — and it moves as the wine is made, so a measurement at crush does not describe the wine at bottling.

## Harvest and juice handling

pH determines picking date as much as sugar does. In warm regions potassium accumulation and malic respiration push pH up rapidly in the final weeks, so a Barossa Shiraz block may be picked at a compromise between phenolic ripeness and a pH already at 3.7, while Champagne and Mosel growers pick early precisely to hold pH near 3.0. pH then dictates handling. Low-pH juice is intrinsically protected: polyphenol oxidase activity declines as pH falls, and molecular SO2 is abundant, so a Chablis or Marlborough must can be handled with modest additions. High-pH juice oxidises faster and is microbially vulnerable, which forces colder settling, tighter oxygen exclusion, higher SO2 doses and a decision on whether to acidify before fermentation. Pre-fermentation tartaric addition is preferable to post-fermentation correction: it integrates better, protects the ferment, and improves colour extraction in reds. In the opposite case — a marginal-climate red at pH 3.1 with aggressive malic acid — deacidification with potassium or calcium carbonate, or an encouraged malolactic conversion, is the answer. Whole-bunch pressing decisions also follow, since prolonged skin contact leaches potassium and raises pH further.

## Fermentation

Fermentation kinetics and microbial competition are pH-governed. *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* tolerates the full wine range, but its competitors do not: at pH below about 3.2 spoilage lactic acid bacteria and most wild organisms are substantially inhibited, giving a clean ferment with minimal intervention, whereas above 3.5 the winemaker must decide between higher SO2, a heavy inoculation to dominate the medium quickly, cooler temperatures, and closer monitoring for volatile acidity. Very low pH has its own costs — it slows fermentation and stresses yeast, so a sparkling base at pH 2.9 needs attentive nutrition. In reds, low pH also improves extraction of stable colour, so maceration is planned around it.

## Malolactic conversion — encourage or suppress

This is the clearest pH-driven fork in the process. *Oenococcus oeni* works reliably between about pH 3.2 and 3.5; below 3.2 it is inhibited, and above 3.5 the conversion may proceed but *Lactobacillus* and *Pediococcus* are equally favoured, bringing biogenic amines, ropiness and mousiness. Malolactic conversion typically raises pH by around 0.1-0.3 units, so a red starting at 3.7 will finish near 3.9 — a genuinely difficult wine to protect thereafter, which is why acidification before or immediately after conversion is standard in warm-climate reds. Where suppression is wanted, as in Mosel Riesling or an unoaked Chablis holding tension, the levers are early SO2, cool storage, lysozyme and early sterile filtration; the low pH of those wines makes suppression easy, which is not a coincidence.

## Maturation and SO2 efficacy

Only molecular SO2 is antimicrobial and antioxidant, and its proportion of free SO2 is a direct function of pH: at pH 3.0 roughly 20 mg/L of free SO2 delivers the customary 0.5 mg/L molecular target, at pH 3.5 something near 40 mg/L is needed, and at pH 3.8 the requirement approaches 80 mg/L. This single relationship explains most of the divergence between low- and high-pH winemaking. A high-pH wine needs so much SO2 that legal totals and sensory acceptability become binding constraints — which is why acidification is often justified as a stability decision rather than a taste one. pH also governs colour during maturation: anthocyanins sit in a pH-dependent equilibrium in which the red flavylium cation predominates at low pH and gives way to colourless carbinol forms as pH rises, so a Douro or Rioja red held below 3.6 keeps more colour and polymerises it more effectively. Barrel regimes and racking frequency follow, since a high-pH wine cannot afford much oxygen exposure at a low molecular SO2.

## Stability, fining and pre-bottling

pH is decisive in the stability work that is too often ignored. Bentonite fining for protein stability works better at lower pH, because heat-unstable proteins carry more positive charge further below their isoelectric points and adsorb more readily, so a high-pH white needs a larger dose with greater flavour stripping. Tartrate behaviour is not linear: bitartrate instability is greatest around pH 3.6, because that is where the bitartrate ion is most abundant, so a wine there needs cold stabilisation, electrodialysis or a metatartaric or CMC addition, while a wine well away from that value may need none. Fining trials with gelatin, egg white or PVPP behave differently across the range because phenolic charge is pH-dependent. Finally, filtration and the closing SO2 calculation: a wine at pH 3.8 with residual sugar effectively requires sterile membrane filtration and possibly DMDC, since chemical protection alone is unachievable, whereas a dry Chablis at pH 3.1 can be bottled through a coarser pad with modest free SO2.

## Conclusion

pH is not one decision among many; it sets the cost and feasibility of most of the others. Every step up the scale narrows the winemaker's options — more SO2 for less effect, less colour, more microbial risk, more aggressive fining, more filtration — which is why the most consequential pH decisions are made early, in the vineyard and at the press, where they are cheapest.
