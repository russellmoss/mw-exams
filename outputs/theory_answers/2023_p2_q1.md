## What colour means here, and how I am reading the question

Red wine colour is anthocyanin-derived, and three properties can be manipulated: intensity, hue (purple, ruby, garnet, tawny) and stability. Free anthocyanins are fugitive and highly pH-sensitive; durable colour comes from polymeric pigments, in which anthocyanin is bound to tannin either directly or through acetaldehyde bridges. Every technique below therefore serves one of two ends — extracting anthocyanin, or converting it into a stable form — and which end is wanted depends entirely on the style. I take three styles as the spine of the answer: an early-drinking primary-fruit red, a deep ageworthy red, and a pale delicate red. Rosé is a separate question and I leave it aside.

## The material you start with

Colour is capped by the fruit. Thick-skinned, anthocyanin-rich varieties — Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Tannat, Saperavi — give far more to work with than thin-skinned Pinot Noir or Grenache, and small berries off a moderate crop raise the skin-to-juice ratio. Pinot Noir and Nebbiolo carry essentially non-acylated anthocyanins, which are less stable than the acylated forms found in most other varieties; no maceration regime overturns that.

pH is the most under-used lever. Anthocyanins are red only in the flavylium form, which dominates at low pH, so the same wine at pH 3.4 reads brighter and more crimson than at pH 3.8, which looks duller and browner. Tartaric acid at crush, earlier picking, or ion exchange where permitted, all lower pH and lift both hue and apparent intensity; deacidification does the reverse. Blending is the bluntest lever of all — Petit Verdot into a Bordeaux blend, or a teinturier such as Alicante Bouschet, adds colour directly rather than extracting it.

## Style one: early-drinking, primary-fruit red

Here the winemaker wants vivid purple now and does not need it to last. Carbonic and semi-carbonic maceration, as in Beaujolais, ferments intracellularly inside whole berries and releases anthocyanin with very little tannin: bright colour, almost no polymeric pigment, so it fades within a few years — which suits a wine drunk young. Thermovinification and flash détente reach a similar commercial target industrially. Heating must to roughly 70C, or heating and then flashing it into a vacuum to rupture the skin cells, liberates anthocyanin in minutes, after which juice can be pressed off and fermented white-style for colour without tannin. Both also denature laccase in botrytis-affected fruit, protecting colour that would otherwise be enzymatically destroyed. Pectolytic maceration enzymes work to the same end more gently, degrading cell walls to raise early anthocyanin release and press yield.

## Style two: deep, ageworthy premium red

The aim now is not maximum extraction but stable extraction. Cold soak exploits anthocyanin's water solubility, pulling colour at low temperature before alcohol starts extracting seed tannin. Cap management meters the rest: pump-overs are the workhorse and, when aerated, deliver oxygen along with the mixing; punch-downs are gentler; rack-and-return and rotary fermenters are aggressive and reserved for under-extracted lots. Saignée bleeds off perhaps a tenth of the juice early to raise the skin-to-juice ratio — effective, but crude, since it concentrates everything including alcohol and acid. Extended post-fermentation maceration initially loses colour to adsorption onto skins and lees, yet builds polymeric pigment, trading present intensity for long-term stability.

Oxygen management is what fixes that colour in place. Aerative pump-overs during fermentation, and micro-oxygenation afterwards, supply the acetaldehyde that bridges anthocyanin to tannin; too little and the pigment stays labile and the wine reduces, too much and phenolics oxidise toward brown. Press fractions carry more colour and tannin than free run, so how much press wine goes back into the blend is itself a colour decision. Oak continues the work — barrels give slow oxygen ingress plus ellagitannins that act as co-factors in polymerisation, and chips or staves combined with micro-oxygenation are the low-cost equivalent. Yeast choice matters too, since strains differ in how much anthocyanin their cell walls adsorb.

## Style three: pale, delicate red

For Pinot Noir, Nebbiolo or a light Cinsault, colour is protected rather than extracted. Whole bunches, gentle infusion-style punch-downs, a short cold soak aimed at aroma more than pigment, an early press before extended maceration strips what little colour exists, and no saignée. Because the pigment is intrinsically unstable, oxygen is restricted, older barrels are preferred to new, and an early garnet hue is accepted as typicity rather than fought.

## Sulphur dioxide and oxidation

SO2 bleaches anthocyanin reversibly, forming colourless bisulphite adducts, so a heavily sulphured young red looks paler than it is and recovers as that SO2 binds. Against this, SO2 at crush inhibits oxidative enzymes and so preserves the very pigment it temporarily masks; the judgement is between short-term appearance and medium-term protection. Oxidation itself is not the enemy but the mechanism — controlled, it polymerises pigment and stabilises colour; uncontrolled, it browns the wine and drops pigment out as sediment.

## Conclusion

No technique is simply good for colour. Carbonic maceration and flash détente maximise immediate intensity and are right for a wine drunk within two years; cold soak, aerative cap management and oak maximise stability and are right for a wine still being sold at ten; restraint and oxygen exclusion are right for a pale variety whose pigment cannot be made to last. The winemaker's first colour decision is deciding which of those three wines is being made.
