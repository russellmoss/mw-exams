## What I mean by "great wine"

By great wine I mean wine that offers sensory complexity, proportion and persistence markedly beyond competence; that is recognisably of a place rather than merely of a grape; that rewards time, in the glass and usually in bottle; and whose standing is sustained by informed judgement outside the producer's own marketing. The last clause is doing real work and I return to it, because greatness is partly conferred and not only made.

The term is contested rather than technical, and the disagreement is instructive. A grower in Vosne-Romanée means by great the capacity of one plot to say something the plot beside it cannot. A large branded business means a wine that outperforms its price bracket consistently and at volume. A collector usually means scarcity, provenance and a liquid secondary market. A sommelier may mean a wine that changes the shape of a table's evening. These are different memberships of one word, and an answer that assumes a single objective bar is answering a question nobody asked.

Two things follow. Greatness is not simply the top rung of the balance–length–intensity–complexity ladder: those criteria separate good from very good, and most serious wines on a specialist merchant's list clear them. And the bar is emphatically not sanitary condition. Clean, physiologically sound fruit is a precondition of competence; every wine discussed below is made from it, so it distinguishes nothing.

My position is that I agree with the proposition only in a weaker and more precise form. Great wine is born in the vineyard: the site sets a ceiling that nothing downstream can raise. But the vineyard is necessary and not sufficient; the winery's contribution is asymmetric rather than equivalent; and market perception is doing more work in the aphorism than it admits.

## Terroir and viticulture are two different claims

The slogan silently merges two contributions that behave quite differently. Terroir is the fixed endowment of a delimited plot — soil depth, water-holding and drainage, parent rock, aspect, slope, altitude, exposure, mesoclimate — which the grower inherits and cannot substitute. Viticultural management is the set of annual and semi-permanent decisions taken on that endowment: variety and clone, rootstock, density, training, canopy, crop load, nutrition, water, picking date.

Their relationship is hierarchical, not additive. A grand cru farmed carelessly makes ordinary wine; a village site farmed superbly rarely makes a great one. Site sets the ceiling; management determines what fraction of it is realised. Conflating the two is what makes most versions of this argument muddled, because it lets the speaker claim credit for the plot while describing the work.

## How the vineyard raises quality and how it imbues character

Two distinct effects are involved, and only the second is specific to place.

The quality-raising mechanisms are physiological and portable. A moderate water deficit from véraison restrains berry expansion and lifts the skin-to-pulp ratio, concentrating phenolics and aroma precursors. Balanced vigour gives an open canopy, so bunches ripen tannins and aromatics rather than merely accumulating sugar. Crop matched to vine capacity, and old vines' self-regulating yields, deliver even ripening rather than a mixture of green and raisined berries. These raise quality wherever they are applied — which is exactly why they cannot by themselves confer greatness. They are available to anyone with skill and capital.

Character comes from the interaction of the fixed endowment with the season, and its signature is repeatability. The Côte d'Or's climat system exists because growers observed, over centuries, that the same variety, the same clonal material, comparable farming and comparable cellars produce measurably and consistently different wine across a few metres of slope — Chambolle-Musigny Les Amoureuses against the village land immediately below it, or the way Corton's wines swing in weight as the aspect rotates around the hill. The Mosel makes the same argument in another key: a steep slate amphitheatre such as Wehlener Sonnenuhr concentrates radiation, drains freely and holds night warmth, so it ripens Riesling to a filigree that the flat valley floor a kilometre away does not reach in any vintage. Because that difference survives changes of owner, winemaker and vintage, it is evidence that the site, not the farming, is the author of the character.

## The winery's contribution is real but different in kind

Whole categories owe their identity to process. A non-vintage Champagne exists to erase site and vintage through assemblage and reserve wine, and its complexity is built by autolysis over years on lees. Sherry's character is made by flor, oxidation and fractional blending through a solera; Madeira's by controlled heat and long oxidative ageing; tawny Port's by wood. Nobody could taste these and locate a plot.

This is not, however, the refutation it appears to be, because even these categories prize superior sites: Champagne's grand cru villages command the highest grape prices, Jerez growers value the albariza pagos above the sandier soils, and the Douro grades its vineyards for quality of raw material. Process wines are built from a hierarchy of fruit.

Nor are vineyard and winery two equivalent sides of a coin. The winery's power is overwhelmingly negative and coarse: it can destroy greatness through poor picking decisions, crude extraction, dominant oak, oxidation or microbial spoilage, but its positive contribution is calibration — expressing what arrived, at a granularity of vessel, temperature and time. Terroir works continuously, non-substitutably and at the granularity of metres. A better press can be bought; Montrachet cannot.

## Greatness is also conferred

The most serious omission in the aphorism is everything after bottling. Two wines of near-identical sensory merit diverge permanently in status because one carries a classified name, a documented history, sustained critical endorsement, controlled allocation and a functioning secondary market, and the other does not. The 1855 Médoc classification still shapes relative pricing among the wines it ranked, well over a century after the commercial conditions that produced it disappeared. Scarcity is manufactured as much as discovered; distribution control converts scarcity into desire; critics and auction houses supply the third-party validation my definition requires. The system is not fixed — Sassicaia's rise from an unclassified estate wine to a wine granted its own denomination shows merit can force the door — but it is a gatekeeper, and greatness that no one recognises does not function as greatness.

## Conclusion

I agree that great wine begins in the vineyard, and that the site rather than the farming is the decisive part of that claim: terroir sets a ceiling, and the mechanisms by which it does so are physiological, repeatable and non-substitutable. But as usually deployed the slogan is wrong in three ways. It flatters viticulture by borrowing terroir's authority. It ignores categories whose identity is authored in the cellar, while overlooking that even those rest on graded sites. And it treats greatness as an intrinsic property of liquid when it is in part a social judgement, made by critics, collectors and markets. The accurate formulation is that great wine is grown in a great site, protected in the winery, and confirmed by the market.
