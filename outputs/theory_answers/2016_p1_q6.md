## When

Hail forms when a strong updraught in a deep, unstable convective cell carries water droplets repeatedly above the freezing level, accreting ice until the stone outweighs the updraught that supports it. That is as much mechanism as the question needs; what matters viticulturally is when and where those conditions occur.

They occur where warm, moist air is forced over relief or meets a cold front — the Rhone valley and the Bordeaux and Burgundy edge of the Massif Central, northern Italy and the Alpine foreland, the Andean foothills at Mendoza, the continental interiors of North America and South Africa's Breede River valley. The season is late spring through summer, with a strong afternoon and early-evening peak, and events are highly localised: a corridor a few hundred metres wide can be destroyed while the adjacent commune is untouched.

Hail becomes a serious issue at two moments in particular: the spring window from budburst to fruit set, and the run from veraison to harvest. Between those, the vine's capacity to compensate is greatest and the loss smallest.

## How

The timing dictates the damage. At budburst and early shoot growth, hail strips swelling buds and snaps soft green shoots. The primary buds carrying this season's inflorescences are destroyed; secondary and latent buds push, but they are less fruitful, later and uneven. Because the vine also loses the leaf area that builds carbohydrate reserves, and because next year's inflorescences are initiated in this year's buds, severe spring hail carries quantitative repercussions for up to two years — the delayed effect most candidates miss. Burgundy and Chablis have repeatedly lost consecutive vintages this way.

At flowering, hail knocks off inflorescences and directly removes yield, with no possibility of compensation. Between set and veraison, the dominant damage is to leaves: shredded canopy reduces photosynthetic capacity, so ripening is delayed and sugar accumulation depressed even where fruit survives. Bruised berries either desiccate and drop, or scar. Wounds on shoots and trunks are entry points for botrytis and, over the longer term, for trunk-disease pathogens.

From veraison to harvest, hail is at its most damaging commercially. Softened, sugar-rich berries split, and if warm humid weather follows, the vineyard converts to botrytis and sour rot within days; a crop can move from healthy to unsalvageable in under a week, and mouldy or earthy taints follow into the wine. Post-harvest hail matters least, though damage to canes reduces bud quality for the following spring.

## Prevention

Assessed on evidence rather than popularity, the options separate sharply.

Netting is the only physical prevention with reliable efficacy, and it is what I would fund. It is proven, and in hot regions it doubles as sun protection — the reason it is close to standard in parts of Mendoza. The costs are real: high capital outlay, annual labour to deploy and retrieve, some shading of the canopy which is a disadvantage in cooler regions, and the fact that netting is restricted or prohibited under some European appellation rules on landscape and typicity grounds. That prohibition is worth challenging: the rationale appears to rest more on appearance than on evidence about wine quality, and it deserves proper study.

Anti-hail cannons are widely deployed and have no robust evidence of efficacy; they are also a source of neighbour disputes over noise. I would not invest in them. Cloud seeding with silver iodide is more credible in principle and regional cloud-seeding programmes operate in Mendoza, in parts of France and in Alberta, but effect sizes are genuinely hard to demonstrate against natural variability. I treat seeding as a collective, quasi-insurance measure rather than something a single estate can rely on.

The most effective prevention is not meteorological at all. Because hail is so localised, dispersing holdings across several parcels and communes — the pattern Burgundian growers arrived at by inheritance and now defend deliberately — is genuine risk mitigation. Crop insurance, where a market exists, is the other real instrument, and in many regions it is the only one that reliably converts a total loss into a survivable one.

## Response

Response is where most answers stop short, and it is where the recoverable value lies. The first step in the days after an event is triage: assess severity block by block, then apply a protectant fungicide, typically copper-based, to wounded tissue, because open wounds on green tissue in warm weather are a botrytis and bacterial infection route.

After early-season damage the key treatment is re-pruning: cutting damaged shoots back to a clean node to provoke an even secondary flush, rebuilding leaf area to restore reserves for the following season, and accepting a token or zero crop this year. Modest nitrogen and water support that regrowth without inviting excess vigour.

Mid-season damage calls for the opposite of protection: open the canopy by leaf removal to speed drying, drop damaged bunches in a green harvest so rot does not spread, and move to more frequent, gentler botrytis control. Close to harvest the response is commercial — pick early, sort ruthlessly in the vineyard and again at the winery, and consider declassification, bulk sale, or diverting fruit to rosé or a sparkling base where the style permits.

The following season, expect reduced bud fruitfulness, prune to more buds to compensate, and monitor for trunk disease entering through old wounds. Ranked overall: netting and parcel dispersal for prevention, speed of response afterwards, and insurance behind both.
