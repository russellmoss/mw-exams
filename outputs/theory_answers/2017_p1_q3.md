## Scope

This is a viticultural question, and I treat it as one: labour cost enters only where it decides which viticultural technique is viable. The real subject is which vineyard tasks can be done by machine, which cannot, and how a vineyard should be designed and staffed on the assumption that hands will be scarce.

## The global picture

The shortage is not uniform, and the responses differ with the cause. In the United States, tighter immigration enforcement and the cost and administrative load of the H-2A visa have shrunk the Latin American farmhand pool that California and Washington were built on, while an ageing existing workforce is not being replaced. In Western Europe, France, Germany and Italy depend on Central and Eastern European seasonal crews whose home economies now compete for them; the United Kingdom lost free movement of labour altogether. South Africa's vineyards have moved steadily toward mechanisation since minimum-wage reform and the 2012–13 farmworker disputes. New Zealand runs a managed Pacific-island scheme, the Recognised Seasonal Employer scheme, precisely because domestic labour will not do the work. In parts of the Middle East, growers in Lebanon have had to rely on displaced populations rather than a settled workforce. China and Japan face rural depopulation and ageing.

The common thread is that grape growing must remain profitable against these costs. Where fruit sells for a few hundred units a tonne, a task that can only be done by hand simply stops being done; where it sells for thousands, hand work survives. So the shortage does not affect all viticulture equally — it squeezes the middle hardest.

## Mechanisation through the vineyard's life

**Establishment.** Land clearing, deep ripping, levelling, laser-guided post driving and GPS-guided planting machines are all mechanised and were among the first tasks to be so. Grafting is done in the nursery. But training a young vine through its first three years — tying, desuckering, establishing the cordon — is hand work, and it comes at the point when the vineyard is earning nothing.

**Winter pruning.** The largest single labour item of the year, and the one that decides the answer. Spur-pruned cordons can be pruned mechanically, either fully or as a mechanical pre-prune followed by a light hand pass, which is standard practice across inland Australia and California. Cane pruning — Guyot in Burgundy and Bordeaux, the two-cane systems of Marlborough — resists mechanisation because cane selection and laying-down are judgement tasks. Converting a vineyard from cane to spur pruning, or to minimal pruning, is therefore a genuine viticultural response to a labour shortage, with real consequences for yield, bunch size and fruit exposure that must be accepted deliberately.

**The growing season.** Spraying, mowing, mid-row cultivation and under-vine weeding are fully mechanised, and autonomous and GPS-guided tractors are extending this. Trimming and leaf removal are mechanised, the latter by air-pulse and roller machines that now do a respectable job in the fruit zone. Shoot positioning and wire-lifting on VSP is hand work, which argues for movable foliage wires or a sprawl system. Bud rubbing, shoot thinning and green harvesting remain manual, and can partly be designed out by adjusting winter pruning load instead.

**Harvest.** Machine harvesting with on-board destemming and optical sorting now produces fruit good enough for serious wine, and it also delivers cool fruit at night, which hand picking rarely does. Grubbing up at the end of the vineyard's life is mechanical.

## What cannot be mechanised

Steep and terraced sites — the Mosel, the Douro, Côte-Rôtie, Priorat, the Wachau — cannot take a harvester, and monorails and winch tractors only partly close the gap. Untrellised bush vines and old, irregularly planted parcels defeat machines. Selective picking of botrytised berries in Sauternes and Tokaj, successive tries in the Layon, and whole-bunch delivery where the appellation demands it are irreducibly manual: Champagne requires whole bunches to be delivered intact to the press, which rules out machine harvesting there as a matter of law rather than preference.

The effect on viticulture, if the trend continues, follows directly. Plantings migrate to flat, uniform, machine-compatible sites; training systems simplify; marginal steep vineyards are abandoned, as vineyard area in the Mosel and parts of the Douro already shows; capital intensity rises and holdings consolidate; and the gap widens between fully mechanised volume production and a shrinking hand-tended premium tier that can carry the cost.

## Handling the shortage without mechanising everything

The second half of the answer is labour management. The best-placed vineyards employ a smaller permanent crew year-round rather than bidding for seasonal peaks: staggering pruning from early winter, filling gaps with trellis repair, soil work and machinery maintenance, and training each worker across pruning, tractor driving, spraying and machine operation. Cross-training converts an unskilled seasonal cost into a skilled retained one, and it is necessary anyway — mechanisation does not remove the labour problem so much as move it from unskilled hands to skilled operators, who are also scarce. Piece-rate and quality-linked pay, housing, and progression retain people once trained.

## Conclusion

Mechanisation answers most of the annual round and all of establishment and removal; it does not answer steep slopes, selective picking or the judgement tasks of pruning and canopy work. The decisive preparation is therefore made at replanting — row width, trellis, training system and site chosen for machine access — supported by a small, cross-trained, permanent crew for what machines still cannot do.
