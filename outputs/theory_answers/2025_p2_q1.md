## Complexity and texture: what is being built, and by what mechanisms

Complexity is the number and layering of distinct aroma and flavour sources a wine carries — fruit, fermentation, autolysis, controlled oxidation, dosage — and texture is the tactile impression: mousse, viscosity, mid-palate weight, and the degree to which acidity reads as creamy rather than sharp. In bottle-fermented sparkling wine both are engineered, and the mechanisms are chemical (esters and thiols, Maillard reactions between amino acids and sugars, phenolic and polysaccharide load), microbiological (yeast strain, autolysis, malolactic conversion) and physical (dissolved CO2, bubble size, surface tension). I assess them chronologically, because the commonest weakness here is to spend the answer on dosage and oak while skipping base-wine production, where most of the texture is actually decided.

## Base wine and pressing

Everything downstream is constrained by a base wine picked at low potential alcohol and high acidity. Whole-bunch pressing at low pressure is the first textural decision: it limits phenolic and potassium extraction, holding pH down and keeping the palate clean. Fractionation matters more than is usually allowed. The cuvée, the first-run juice, is lower in phenolics and potassium and delivers length and precision; the taille carries more colour, phenolics and aromatic weight, and can be used deliberately for early-drinking richness or excluded entirely from a long-ageing prestige wine. Débourbage then sets the solids level, and this is a genuine texture lever: very clean juice ferments slowly and gives a linear wine, while retaining a little more solids supplies lipids and sterols that support yeast health and yield a rounder base. Cava's Xarel·lo and Franciacorta's Chardonnay are handled on the same logic from different varietal starting points.

## Fermentation and malolactic conversion

Yeast strain and vessel are stylistic levers, not defaults. A neutral, low-nitrogen-demand strain preserves varietal definition; fermentation or ageing in old oak or foudre adds slow oxygen ingress, aldehydic complexity and a light phenolic grip; bâtonnage on the base wine adds polysaccharides ahead of tirage. Malolactic conversion should be argued rather than assumed. It converts a dicarboxylic acid to a monocarboxylic one, lowering titratable acidity and raising pH, which softens the attack; where citrate metabolism is allowed to run it also yields diacetyl and a buttery mid-palate weight. Blocking it — routine in much of Cava, and selectively in Champagne — preserves nervy acidity and ageing capacity but leaves the wine microbiologically exposed if pH drifts up later. The choice is therefore a trade between immediate textural gain and long-term stability.

## Blending and reserve wines

Assemblage is where complexity is deliberately multiplied. Blending across villages, soils and varieties layers sources that no single parcel supplies, and reserve wines add a second axis: older material carries oxidative, nutty and dried-fruit notes plus higher polymeric content, so it contributes both flavour depth and palate weight while anchoring house style. A perpetual reserve intensifies this by compounding many vintages into one lot.

## Tirage, the second fermentation and mousse

Mousse is the defining textural element and deserves explicit treatment. Fine, persistent bubbles increase the surface area over which CO2 leaves the wine slowly, which lowers the perception of prickle and softens the impression of acidity; they carry aromatics to the nose gradually, and they create a creamy mid-palate weight and support length and persistence on the finish. Bubble size is a physical outcome of dissolved CO2 concentration and of surface tension, which falls as autolytic macromolecules accumulate. The winemaker's levers are the composition of the liqueur de tirage (sugar dose setting final pressure; adjuvants such as bentonite and the choice of riddling aid), the yeast strain — selected for pressure and alcohol tolerance, low H2S production and good autolytic character — and the temperature of the second fermentation. A slow, cool second fermentation, of the order of 12°C, is widely held to give a finer and more persistent bead than a fast warm one.

## Autolysis and extended lees ageing

Autolysis is the principal source of both complexity and texture in this category. After cell death, enzymatic breakdown of the yeast releases mannoproteins from the cell wall, plus amino acids, peptides, nucleotides and lipids. Mannoproteins bind at the gas–liquid interface, lowering surface tension and stabilising smaller bubbles, and add colloidal weight to the palate; amino acids feed Maillard chemistry with residual sugars, giving the toast, brioche and biscuit spectrum; lipid degradation adds further aromatic layers. Legal minima institutionalise this: Champagne requires roughly fifteen months of total ageing for a non-vintage wine and three years for a vintage, Cava Gran Reserva demands around thirty months, Franciacorta more than most New World traditional-method wines, which is why long-aged Tasmanian and Cape sparkling wines have converged on the same practice voluntarily.

## Disgorgement and dosage

Dosage does more than sweeten. It balances residual acidity, but the liqueur d'expédition is also a complexity tool: sugar dissolved in aged reserve wine, or in oak-matured wine, carries its own flavour, and time between dosage and release allows the addition to integrate and the wine to knit. A high-acid, long-aged wine can carry a brut, up to roughly 12 g/L, without reading as sweet, whereas the same dose on a lower-acid base is clumsy.

## Conclusion

The techniques are not equal. Base-wine composition, press fractionation and extended autolysis are the levers with real mechanistic power over both complexity and texture; blending and reserve wines multiply sources; malolactic conversion, oak and dosage are adjustments that can refine or ruin what has already been built but cannot substitute for it.
