Three separate problems, each given roughly a third of the answer, and in each case I work from diagnosis to remedies ranked by cost and by the damage each does to the wine.

## a) Stuck fermentation

**Definition and detection.** A stuck fermentation is one that has ceased, or slowed to a negligible rate, with fermentable sugar above roughly 2-4 g/L remaining — distinct from a sluggish ferment, which is merely slow. It is detected by daily density or Brix and temperature logging, plotted as a curve: a flattening line identifies the problem days before any sensory change. Waiting for off-odours is a failure of cellar practice, not a diagnosis.

**Why it matters.** Sugar in a wine with no active yeast, no protective CO2 blanket and no SO2 is an open invitation. Acetic acid bacteria produce volatile acidity and ethyl acetate; *Lactobacillus* metabolises it to acetic acid; *Brettanomyces* establishes itself. Every day of delay makes the wine more alcoholic, more toxic to yeast and harder to restart.

**Remedies, in order of cost.** First establish the cause: nitrogen deficiency, a temperature excursion above roughly 32-35C or a chilled tank, osmotic stress from very high sugar, alcohol beyond the strain's tolerance, excessive juice clarification stripping the sterols yeast need, or an inhibitory residue. Then act cheaply first: correct temperature, restored to roughly 20-25C, and aerate by pump-over, which disperses inhibitory CO2 and supplies the oxygen yeast use for sterol synthesis. Add thiamine and a complex nutrient if nitrogen is short — DAP alone is poorly used late — and yeast hulls, which adsorb the toxic medium-chain fatty acids and supply survival factors. If viable population is genuinely lost, restart properly: build an alcohol-tolerant strain such as *S. bayanus* in a starter, acclimatise it by stepwise addition of the stuck wine, then pitch. Blending a portion of the stuck wine into a healthy, actively fermenting tank is often the simplest and cheapest solution where capacity allows. Meanwhile protect the wine with inert gas and cool storage; adding SO2 would inhibit the restart it needs.

## b) Undesirable oxidative characters, including mouse taint

**Identify which oxidation.** *Enzymatic* oxidation acts on must, by tyrosinase from healthy fruit or the far more aggressive laccase from botrytis, browning juice within minutes. *Chemical* oxidation acts on wine: iron and copper catalyse the reduction of oxygen to hydrogen peroxide, which oxidises ethanol to acetaldehyde and phenolics to quinones, giving bruised apple and nutty notes, browning, and loss of thiols and terpenes. *Microbial* oxidation is separate again: acetic acid bacteria need oxygen and produce volatile acidity and ethyl acetate, and film yeasts produce a flor-like veil. **Mouse taint** is not strictly oxidative at all — it is *N*-heterocycles produced by *Brettanomyces* and some lactic acid bacteria — but its enabling conditions are low SO2, high pH and oxygen exposure, which is why it appears in low-intervention winemaking. It is perceived only retronasally, once saliva raises the pH, so a wine can smell clean and taste mousy.

**Find the entry point first.** Ullage in a part-empty tank, a leaking barrel and a lax topping regime, splash racking, a failing pump seal, oxygen pickup at bottling, and warm storage. Measure free and total SO2, dissolved oxygen, VA and acetaldehyde before treating anything.

**Remedies, weighed.** Prevention is the only cheap option: correct free SO2 to deliver the molecular level appropriate to the wine's pH, top up, blanket headspace with nitrogen or argon, refrigerate — cold slows both chemical oxidation and acetic bacteria — and repair the equipment. For damage already done, blending a fresh reductive lot below the sensory threshold is usually the most cost-effective and least destructive route. Fining with PVPP or casein reduces browning and some oxidised aroma but strips fruit with it; activated charcoal removes colour and aroma indiscriminately and is a last resort; membrane treatments for acetaldehyde or VA are expensive and legally restricted in several markets. For mouse taint there is no reliable cure: filtration and SO2 stop it worsening, blending dilutes, and a badly affected lot is a write-off.

## c) Early-stage Brettanomyces infection

*Brettanomyces bruxellensis* converts hydroxycinnamic acids to volatile phenols — 4-ethylphenol, giving barnyard and plaster, and 4-ethylguaiacol, giving smoke and bacon — living on trace sugars a dry wine still contains. It is overwhelmingly a red-wine problem, because red wines have higher pH and the skin contact that supplies the precursors; it is rare in whites and rosés.

Caught early, the aim is to stop population growth before volatile phenols cross threshold:

1. **Quantify** by plating or qPCR and measure 4-EP and 4-EG, so the decision is made against a number.
2. **Lower pH** with tartaric acid where the wine tolerates it, then set free SO2 to give a molecular SO2 of roughly 0.6-0.8 mg/L and *maintain* it. SO2 does not destroy Brettanomyces; it suppresses it into a viable but non-culturable state from which it recovers when SO2 falls, so this is a standing regime, not a single dose.
3. **Remove the habitat**: rack off gross lees, which feed it, and cool the cellar, since growth slows markedly in the cold.
4. **Move the wine out of infected cooperage.** Brett colonises the staves; steam or hot-water treatment helps, but a heavily infected barrel should be retired.
5. **Filter**: cross-flow or membrane filtration at 0.45 microns removes cells, and sterile filtration before bottling is the definitive step. It costs some texture in a premium red, which is the trade-off to accept.
6. **Blend** below threshold only once the population is controlled, and consider bottling earlier to shorten the exposure.

## Conclusion

All three problems reward early measurement and cheap intervention. In each case the expensive remedies damage the wine, so the winemaker's real work is the monitoring that makes them unnecessary.
