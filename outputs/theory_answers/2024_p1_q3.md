## Quality, defined before it is defended

The statement cannot be tested without saying what quality means, and it means at least five different things depending on who is speaking. Organoleptically, quality is concentration, balance, complexity and ageing potential. In terms of origin, it is typicity — a wine that tastes of where it comes from. Commercially, it is fitness for purpose at a price: consistency, cleanliness and availability, which is what quality means for a wine sold by the pallet. Legally, quality is a category — appellation status — and yield is one of the conditions attached to it. And in the market, quality is what critics and consumers say it is. Yield relates differently to each. My argument is that the statement conflates the organoleptic sense with the others, and that even within the organoleptic sense it assumes a relationship that was always cruder than it appeared.

## What has changed, and what the statement is reacting to

The "no longer" points at a real history. Post-war European viticulture combined high-yielding clonal selections, generous nitrogen fertilisation and improving disease control, and yields rose sharply; the wines thinned. Appellation systems codified maximum yields — the rendement de base of French AOCs, the resa in Italian DOCs, the beneficio allocation in the Douro — as the enforceable guarantee against exactly that. By the 1980s and 1990s the green harvest had become orthodoxy in Bordeaux, Barolo and beyond, and low yield became a marketing claim in its own right.

Four things have since weakened the equation. First, plant material: clonal and massal selections are now chosen for berry size, bunch architecture and quality rather than productivity, and rootstocks are matched to soil vigour. Second, canopy management: divided and vertically extended canopies, correct shoot density and disciplined leaf removal allow a vine to ripen a larger crop fully, because ripening depends on functional leaf area per unit of fruit rather than on tonnes per hectare. Third, precision viticulture: yield mapping, canopy sensing and zonal harvest let a grower manage variability rather than crudely cut the crop. Fourth, and least discussed, climate: in regions where heat and drought now threaten to over-ripen rather than under-ripen fruit, deliberately reducing crop can accelerate sugar accumulation and produce exactly the high-alcohol, low-acid wine the grower was trying to avoid.

## Balance, not tonnage

The concept that replaces yield is vine balance: the crop a vine carries should match the capacity of its canopy and root system to ripen it. A common rule of thumb is a ratio of roughly five to ten kilograms of fruit per kilogram of pruning wood, and the point of that measure is that it is a ratio — the correct crop for a large, vigorous vine is large.

Lower is therefore not automatically better. Severely thinning a vigorous vine on a deep, fertile soil redirects growth into laterals, shades the fruit zone, delays ripening, raises pH and produces green, unbalanced wine — the opposite of the intention. Champagne is the standing counter-example: its permitted yield is set annually, based on the quality and quantity of the harvest, through a process led by its interprofession, because the style needs high acidity, moderate potential alcohol and delicacy, and a bigger crop delivers all three. German Riesling at Kabinett weight makes the same point at a lower alcohol. In Jerez, Palomino is cropped generously on albariza and the quality is built in the solera, not by restriction. Old dry-grown bush vines in Barossa or the Swartland self-regulate to low yields because of vine age and water availability, not because anyone thinned them — and cutting their crop further would gain nothing.

## Where control is still necessary

Legally, always. A grower cannot sell fruit as Chablis, Barolo or Rioja above the permitted yield; declassification is the penalty, and the cap also functions as a supply-management tool protecting price. That alone makes the statement false as written for a large share of world production.

Viticulturally, control remains necessary wherever the vine is out of balance. Young vines on fertile, irrigated alluvial soils will over-crop and dilute. Vigorous sites with high water-holding capacity need either crop retained on a divided canopy or crop removed. Uneven fruit set produces second-crop bunches that will never ripen with the first, and their removal is a quality intervention even though it is measured in tonnes. In drought, the crop a vine can ripen falls with the water available, so yield control becomes water-balance management. And in a cool, wet vintage, reducing crop restores the leaf-area-to-fruit ratio needed to finish ripening.

Note too that the control can be exercised elsewhere: pruning to a bud number, planting density, rootstock and irrigation set yield before any bunch is dropped, and severe selection at the sorting table lowers the effective yield of the finished wine.

## Conclusion

I reject the statement as an absolute, while accepting the belief behind it. Yield control is no longer sufficient for quality and never was a reliable proxy for it: low yield alone guarantees nothing, and in some styles and climates it actively harms the wine. But it remains necessary — legally, wherever appellation law applies; commercially, wherever supply and price must be managed; and viticulturally, wherever a vine's crop exceeds what its canopy and water supply can ripen. The correct modern formulation is that growers should no longer control yield to a number, but must still control the vine to a balance — and in a fertile, wet or young vineyard, that still means dropping fruit.
