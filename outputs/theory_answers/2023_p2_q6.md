## Defining the terms

A wild or spontaneous fermentation is one begun without an inoculum of selected active dried yeast, relying on the yeast population resident on the fruit and, far more importantly, on the winery's surfaces and equipment. "Uninoculated" is the more honest word than "wild", since in an established cellar the resident *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* population is effectively a house strain.

"Risk" here means three distinct things and they need separating: sensory risk (the wine is different from intended), microbiological risk (the wine is spoiled), and commercial risk (the tank is late, blocked or lost). Only the last two are genuinely dangerous.

One clarification: wild fermentation is a technique available to any winemaker and is not the same thing as natural wine. Many premium Burgundy and Californian cellars ferment uninoculated while sulphuring, fining and filtering normally. Natural wine combines uninoculated fermentation with very low sulphur dioxide, and it is that second variable which carries most of the spoilage risk.

## What actually happens: the kinetics

An uninoculated must begins with a mixed population. Apiculate and other non-*Saccharomyces* yeasts dominate first — *Hanseniaspora uvarum* (anamorph *Kloeckera apiculata*), *Metschnikowia pulcherrima*, *Starmerella bacillaris*, *Torulaspora delbrueckii* and *Pichia* species. They are poorly ethanol-tolerant, so they dominate the first few days and are largely eliminated by about 4 to 6 per cent alcohol, after which *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* completes the fermentation. This succession is the whole story: the sensory contribution comes from the first phase, the security of finishing comes from the second.

The kinetic consequences are a longer lag phase, a lower peak rate, a longer overall fermentation and, critically, a greater risk of a sluggish or stuck finish. Every hazard below follows from those extended windows.

## The rewards

The first phase yields metabolites a single *S. cerevisiae* strain does not make. *Starmerella bacillaris* produces high glycerol and converts sugar to ethanol less efficiently, so it can shave alcohol and add mid-palate weight; *Torulaspora delbrueckii* contributes esters and lowers volatile acidity; several non-*Saccharomyces* yeasts have beta-glucosidase activity that liberates bound terpenes, lifting aroma in Muscat and Riesling. Mannoproteins from a mixed population add texture.

Second, lot-to-lot difference: a cellar fermenting fifty barrels uninoculated has fifty slightly different wines to blend from, which is a winemaking advantage, not a marketing one. Third, the slower, cooler curve retains volatiles a fast inoculated ferment blows off with the carbon dioxide. Fourth, and only fourth, provenance narrative — worth something at the high end, nothing on a supermarket shelf.

## The risks, and how far each can be managed

**Stuck or sluggish fermentation.** The core hazard. Residual sugar in an unprotected wine opens a window for everything else. *Mitigation:* measure yeast assimilable nitrogen and feed it, keep fermentation temperature moderate, and hold active dried yeast on site for a rescue restart, which is difficult but not impossible if attempted early.

**Volatile acidity and ethyl acetate.** *Hanseniaspora* produces acetic acid and ethyl acetate; at low levels the latter lifts aroma, above threshold it is nail varnish. *Mitigation:* sound fruit, cool pre-fermentation handling, daily volatile acidity monitoring rather than weekly.

**Film yeasts.** *Pichia membranifaciens* and *Candida* species form a film on ullaged surfaces, producing acetaldehyde. *Mitigation:* full vessels and inert gas cover, which is elementary.

**Bacterial spoilage.** A long lag at high pH invites *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum* and *Pediococcus* to ferment sugar directly — *piqûre lactique*, giving volatile acidity and mannitol — and *Acetobacter* to work any oxygenated surface. This is where low sulphur dioxide genuinely compounds the problem, and it is why the natural-wine confound matters.

**Brettanomyces bruxellensis.** The extended low-alcohol, low-sulphur window is exactly its opportunity, and once established in a cellar it is effectively permanent.

**Reduction.** Nitrogen-starved mixed populations generate hydrogen sulphide readily. *Mitigation:* nutrition and aeration during fermentation.

The examiners are right that these outcomes are not inevitable. A pied de cuve started from a healthy early-picked lot, sound sorted fruit, 30 to 50 mg/L at crush, adequate nutrition, temperature control and a tight monitoring regime reduce the failure rate to something a professional cellar can live with.

## The economics and logistics

The cost saving is not the argument. Active dried yeast at typical rates costs a fraction of a cent per litre — immaterial against the value of the wine, let alone the cost of losing a tank. The real economics run the other way: uninoculated fermentation ties up tank space for longer at the peak of harvest, requires more frequent analysis and more skilled attention, and demands the capacity to isolate and treat a failing lot. That capacity is a function of winery scale and cellar discipline, not philosophy.

## Conclusion: where I would, and would not

Yes, on sound fruit, in small high-value lots, in a cellar with tank flexibility, staff to monitor daily and a blending safety net — the sensory gain is real and a failure is contained to one barrel. No, on compromised or high-pH fruit, on large single-tank volumes with fixed shipping dates, or in a cellar already carrying *Brettanomyces*. The sensible middle course is to inoculate a proportion of lots and run the rest uninoculated, or to co-inoculate *Torulaspora delbrueckii* with *S. cerevisiae* and buy most of the complexity with little of the risk. Wild ferments are worth the risk where the risk can be isolated and the wine is worth the attention; the question is not whether spontaneous fermentation is better, but whether this winery can afford this tank to go wrong.
