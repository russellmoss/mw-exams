## Defining a healthy lifestyle

A healthy lifestyle is a pattern of behaviour that maximises healthy life expectancy. It has four components: physical health (diet, exercise, weight, avoidance of carcinogens and toxins), mental health, social connection, and the sustainability of the pattern itself — a regime nobody can keep is not a lifestyle. Public health tends to score only the first; the World Health Organization's own definition of health as complete physical, mental and social wellbeing includes all four.

That definition decides the shape of my answer. Wine has no significant role in the first component and cannot be defended there. It has a real but modest role in the third and fourth. My position is therefore: **wine has no significant role to play in physical health, and should never be recommended for one; but for many adults it can occupy a legitimate, minor place within a healthy lifestyle broadly defined, and for a substantial minority it has no place at all.** That is a nuanced answer, not a fence-sitting one: I am rejecting the trade's health claim outright while defending wine's compatibility with wellbeing.

## What the evidence actually says

**For.** The observational literature has for four decades produced a J- or U-shaped curve, with light-to-moderate drinkers showing lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than abstainers. Mechanisms were proposed — raised HDL cholesterol, reduced platelet aggregation, polyphenols including resveratrol. The Mediterranean-diet literature supports a related and weaker claim: The PREDIMED trial of the Mediterranean diet included moderate wine with meals as part of a dietary pattern with cardiovascular benefit, though it did not isolate wine as the active component.

**Against.** The J-curve has been badly damaged by three lines of work. First, confounding: many "abstainers" in cohort studies are former drinkers who stopped for health reasons, and moderate drinkers in wealthy countries are systematically better educated, wealthier and healthier before they drink anything. Second, Mendelian randomisation: studies using genetic variants in ALDH2 and ADH1B, which determine tolerance rather than lifestyle, find risk relationships for stroke and hypertension that rise linearly from zero and show no protective floor. Third, cancer. IARC has classified alcoholic beverages as a Group 1 carcinogen, with the strongest associations in mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, liver, colorectum and female breast, and the breast-cancer relationship has no observed threshold. The Global Burden of Disease analysis published in The Lancet in 2018 concluded that the consumption level minimising total health loss is zero.

Two claims of the trade's should be abandoned outright. The first is that red wine is protective because of polyphenols: the resveratrol doses used in laboratory work are not achievable by drinking, and no health authority recommends starting to drink for cardiovascular benefit. The second is the implication that wine is a health product. It is a pleasure product containing a carcinogen, and the industry's credibility on every other issue depends on saying so.

## What moderation means

Moderation has no single definition, which is itself instructive. The UK's Chief Medical Officers set a single guideline of fourteen units a week for both sexes in 2016 and explicitly stated there is no safe level. France revised its advice in 2017 to no more than ten standard drinks a week and no more than two in any day, with drink-free days. By contrast the United States has held a looser line of up to two drinks a day for men and one for women. National guidelines vary by roughly a factor of three, and they have moved in only one direction.

More useful than the number is the pattern. Volume and pattern are different risks: fourteen units spread across the week with food is not the same exposure as fourteen units on a Saturday, and the second is where acute harm, injury and dependency concentrate. Moderation therefore means a low weekly total, no episodes of heavy intake, drink-free days, and consumption with food. On any of those measures, the trade's habit of using "moderate" to mean "not visibly drunk" is indefensible.

## Different people, different answers

Nuance here is a matter of who is drinking, not of hedging. There are groups for whom the answer is a flat no: pregnant women, adolescents whose brains are still developing, anyone with alcohol dependency or a family history of it, people with liver disease, pancreatitis or certain psychiatric conditions, people taking medications that interact with ethanol, and anyone about to drive or operate machinery. There is a large group in East Asia carrying the ALDH2 variant for whom ethanol clears slowly and carcinogenic acetaldehyde accumulates; the risk profile for them is materially worse.

For women, risk arrives at lower doses than for men — body water, first-pass metabolism, and the breast-cancer association together justify the lower guideline. For older adults, falls, polypharmacy and reduced metabolic capacity change the calculation again. And circumstance matters as much as physiology: a glass with dinner in a culture with high food integration and low intoxication norms is a different behaviour from the same volume drunk alone or fast.

## Wine among the other drinks

Wine must be judged as one alcoholic drink among several, because the agent of harm is ethanol and a unit is a unit. Physiologically there is no meaningful case that wine is safer than beer or spirits at equal ethanol; the polyphenol argument does not survive dose scrutiny.

The genuine differences are behavioural, and they are the honest part of wine's case. Wine is consumed predominantly with food, which slows absorption; it is consumed in social and slow settings; and it is typically lower in alcohol by volume than spirits, though the drift upward in still wine over thirty years has eroded that. Against this, wine has drifted the wrong way on strength while beer has innovated hard on low and no alcohol, and wine's home-poured serve is not a measured one.

The industry's real exposure is that "wine is different" is a cultural claim being made against a regulatory framework that treats all ethanol alike — and increasingly, against a consumer generation that already does too. Minimum unit pricing, as adopted in Scotland, is the clearest illustration: it prices by ethanol content and is indifferent to the cultural claim entirely. Wine will be regulated as alcohol, and the sooner the trade argues on that basis rather than on exceptionalism, the more credible its remaining case will be.

## Conclusion

Does wine have a significant role to play in a healthy lifestyle? On physical health, no — the protective case has not survived the last decade of evidence, wine contains a Group 1 carcinogen, and nobody should be advised to drink for their health. On a healthy lifestyle properly defined, wine has a modest and defensible role for many adults: taken in genuinely moderate amounts, with food, in a social setting, it contributes to the pleasure and connection that make a sustainable life, and the residual physical risk at that level is small in the context of a life that is otherwise well run. For a substantial and identifiable minority of people, it has no role at all. The industry's task is not to defend the health claim it has lost, but to earn the place it can still legitimately hold.
