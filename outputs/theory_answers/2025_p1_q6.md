## Which effects of climate change are we mitigating?

The answer depends entirely on how the effects are defined, and defining them narrowly is the commonest way to get this question wrong. I take eight: higher mean and extreme heat, which compresses phenology and decouples sugar from acid and phenolic ripeness; **drought** and less reliable rainfall; **higher evaporative demand**, which raises water use even when rainfall is unchanged; **soil salinity**, from irrigating with poorer water and from seawater intrusion in coastal aquifers; **flood and waterlogging** from heavy rain events; **frost**, whose risk is rising rather than falling because budburst has advanced into a largely unchanged frost window; **shifting pest and disease pressure**, including nematode populations, vector ranges and wet-warm mildew events; and **shortened vine life**, as stress interacts with trunk disease. Only some of these have a root-system component at all, which is where the "to what extent" answer begins.

## Where rootstocks genuinely mitigate

**Water.** This is the strongest case. Drought tolerance operates two ways — avoidance, through deep and exploratory rooting, and tolerance, through the stock's influence on the scion's stomatal behaviour and water-use efficiency. 110R, 140 Ruggeri and 1103 Paulsen are the classic drought-tolerant choices, with Ramsey adding vigour and nematode tolerance in sandy soils; Riparia Gloire sits at the opposite pole, shallow-rooted and the first to suffer. Choosing the right one at planting can be the difference between a dry-farmed block surviving a rainless summer and one that shuts down at veraison. Two qualifications matter. Drought tolerance and high vigour are correlated, so a stock chosen for drought may build a canopy that transpires more, and the benefit is realised only if crop load and canopy are managed to match. And a rootstock cannot create water that is not in the profile; it extends the period before deficit becomes damaging, which in a severe year buys weeks, not a season.

**Salinity.** As irrigation water quality declines this becomes a first-order problem, and rootstocks are the primary defence: chloride excluders such as Ramsey and 1103 Paulsen versus poor excluders like Riparia Gloire. There is no canopy or irrigation substitute for the genetics here beyond leaching, which needs the water that is short in the first place.

**Waterlogging and heavy rain.** Riparia-derived stocks tolerate wet feet far better than rupestris and berlandieri crosses; a stock chosen for drought will drown in a flood year. This is the clearest illustration that climate change presents *opposing* stresses in the same vineyard, and that a rootstock optimises for one.

**Soil pH and lime.** Where evaporation concentrates carbonates, 41B, Fercal and 140 Ruggeri tolerate active lime, preventing the chlorosis that would otherwise cost yield and ripening.

**Soil-borne pests.** Nematode-tolerant stocks and the newer bred selections address populations that expand with warmer soils, and they remain the only control since soil fumigation was curtailed.

## Where rootstocks do very little

Ripening date is the effect growers most want to fix, and rootstock is a weak lever on it: vigour and vine water status shift maturity by days, where site, variety, clone, canopy and crop load shift it by weeks. Rootstock does nothing about heat spikes, sunburn, hail, smoke or the loss of acid at high temperature. On frost it is close to irrelevant — budburst date is a scion characteristic, marginally modulated by vigour — so a candidate offering rootstock as a frost answer has misunderstood the mechanism. And against fungal disease pressure a rootstock helps only indirectly, by moderating vigour and canopy density.

## The "to what extent": practicalities

The practical arithmetic is what decides this question. At the moment of planting, the rootstock decision is close to free: it costs no more to specify 1103 Paulsen than Riparia Gloire, and getting it right is one of the highest-return decisions in viticulture. **As a retrofit it is expensive and slow.** Replanting means three years to first crop and five or more to full yield, with the establishment cost carried throughout; scion material can be changed by field grafting, but the rootstock cannot be changed without pulling the vine. So the grower is committing to a 25-to-40-year asset on a forecast of conditions decades ahead — a genuine gamble, made harder by the fact that the changes are not unidirectional, since the same vineyard may face drought in one year and flooding in the next. Nursery supply is a real constraint: newer selections exist in trial quantities, and orders are placed a year or two ahead. And rootstock behaviour is soil-specific, so a stock that performs on one block underperforms on the next.

The cautionary example is the collapse of AXR#1 in California from the 1980s, planted on partial phylloxera tolerance and replanted at enormous cost. It is exactly the failure mode this question invites: a long-lived decision made on an optimistic reading of a pressure that then changed.

**Do you need a rootstock at all?** Where phylloxera is absent — sands, Chile, South Australia under quarantine, Santorini — vines are own-rooted, and adopting a rootstock for climate reasons means importing phylloxera-era economics and losing own-rooted longevity for a partial water benefit. That trade is often not worth making. Where phylloxera is present, grafting is compulsory and the question is only which stock, which is why rootstock is such an attractive adaptation lever: unlike changing variety or irrigating, it is permitted under essentially every appellation code.

## Conclusion

Rootstocks are a significant but second-order tool. They are genuinely powerful against water deficit, salinity, waterlogging, lime and soil-borne pests, near-useless against frost, heat extremes and hail, and weak on ripening date. They are the single best-value adaptation decision at planting and a poor one as a retrofit. My judgement is that they belong in the portfolio — behind site selection, variety and clone, and behind canopy, floor and irrigation management for an existing vineyard — and that a grower who treats rootstock choice as the climate strategy rather than one component of it will have paid thirty years for a partial answer.
