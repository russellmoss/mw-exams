## What the wine education industry is

By "the wine education industry" I mean the organised, fee-earning supply of structured wine learning to two distinct audiences: the **trade** — people who earn a living from wine and need a credential or a skill — and **consumers**, who buy learning as leisure or self-improvement. It is global and unevenly institutionalised, and it has four tiers.

First, product-focused certification: the international awarding bodies and their national counterparts — Italy's AIS, Japan's sommelier system, the various national sommelier associations, the specialist regional schools. Second, professional-skills education in viticulture and oenology at degree level: UC Davis, Geisenheim, Adelaide, Montpellier, Bordeaux. Third, wine business education — the wine MBAs and specialist masters at Bordeaux, Adelaide and Sonoma State. Fourth, the informal channel: producer- and retailer-led tastings, free digital content, social media. I treat the first three as the industry proper, because they have revenue, staff, premises and a business model to defend; the fourth matters chiefly as competition for attention and as a recruitment funnel into the paid tiers. The largest awarding bodies operate at a scale of the order of 100,000 candidates a year across more than 70 countries, which is what makes this an industry rather than a cottage activity.

## Where I come out

My view, stated before the evidence: the main challenge over the next decade is **demand-side and financial — the collapsing return on investment for the individual self-funding student, compounded by a loss of social legitimacy for alcohol education**. The main opportunity is **the shift of the payer from the student to the employer and the producer, coupled with genuine localisation outside the anglophone core**. The fashionable answers — digital delivery, competition from influencers — are neither.

## Challenges

**1. The student's economics, not the provider's.** Most trade study is self-funded by people in hospitality and retail earning low wages. The post-pandemic flight of staff from hospitality in the UK, the US and Australia removed a large slice of the natural student base and it has not fully returned. Entry-level enrolment is resilient because employers push it; the profitable upper levels depend on individuals betting two or three thousand pounds on a career return, and that bet has got worse. This is the single most serious structural risk and it is rarely discussed because it is invisible in headline candidate numbers.

**2. Regulatory and geopolitical concentration.** Growth over the last decade came from a small number of markets, China above all. The reported suspension of WSET course delivery in mainland China during 2021, following a regulatory review of foreign-linked private education, showed how quickly a market can close for reasons nothing to do with wine. Russia closed in 2022 for different reasons again. India constrains alcohol promotion state by state; the Gulf constrains it absolutely. Market variation has become a governance problem rather than a marketing one, and a provider dependent on two or three geographies is fragile.

**3. Health policy and the legitimacy of teaching alcohol.** The World Health Organization's European action plan treats reducing alcohol consumption as a policy target, and Ireland legislated to require health warnings on alcohol labels. Over ten years this determines whether universities are comfortable hosting wine programmes, whether non-wine employers will fund staff study, and how consumer courses may be advertised. No major curriculum yet teaches alcohol risk honestly, and the first that does will be better protected than those that do not.

**4. Linguistic and cultural parochialism.** The dominant curricula are written in English around a tasting lexicon of gooseberry, blackcurrant and damson — references that are inert for a student in Chengdu, Lagos or Lima. Translation is not localisation, and a translated syllabus taught by a local school is exactly the product a well-funded domestic competitor can beat. This is the most under-priced challenge on my list.

**5. Disintermediation of the classroom.** Remote invigilation, normalised by Covid, decouples the awarding body from the local approved provider. The awarding body gains reach; the local school loses its geographic monopoly and its margin, and it is the local schools that employ most of the industry's people. Expect consolidation and a squeeze on small providers, particularly in mature markets.

**6. Assessment that does not scale.** Blind tasting is the credential's differentiator and its logistical bottleneck: sample sourcing, alcohol importation, invigilation. Every push into a new or restrictive market runs into it.

## Opportunities

**1. Recurring learning rather than one-off qualification.** A certificate is a poor business; maintained, updateable professional learning is a good one. Sustainability accounting, packaging regulation, no- and low-alcohol production and DTC e-commerce all change faster than a syllabus revision cycle, which is precisely why they suit a subscription model.

**2. Subject matter the incumbents do not yet own.** The technical schools — Geisenheim and UC Davis in particular — are strongly placed on climate adaptation and disease-resistant varieties. The commercial gap is larger and more valuable: carbon accounting, sustainability certification literacy and e-commerce data are barely taught outside the wine MBAs.

**3. Real localisation.** Curricula authored in Mandarin, Portuguese, Spanish and Japanese around local flavours, foods and drinking occasions. Japan's sommelier system and Italy's AIS demonstrate that a strong domestic body out-recruits an international one on home ground; Brazil and Mexico are the obvious next tests.

**4. Reframing consumer education as a commercial instrument.** WSET's own study, Consumer Wine Education: should the wine trade care?, argues that educated consumers trade up and buy across a wider repertoire. If that link is evidenced from retailers' own transaction data, consumer education becomes a marketing line funded by producers and retailers instead of a hobby cost paid by enthusiasts. That is the largest single revenue opportunity available to this industry.

**5. Reach into markets that never had a provider** — sub-Saharan Africa, South-East Asia, Latin America — at a marginal cost that finally makes small markets viable.

## Which of these are the main ones

Digital delivery is usually offered as the great opportunity. I disagree: it has already happened, it is now table stakes, and its net effect on the industry's existing businesses is deflationary. Competition from influencers and free content is likewise overstated — they compete for attention, not for fee revenue, and they are the most efficient recruitment funnel the paid tiers have ever had.

Rank instead by what can materially change revenue by 2032. Student ROI decides the size of the market. The payer shift decides who funds it. Localisation decides who captures the growth. Health policy decides the environment all three operate in. Geopolitical concentration is the tail risk that can remove a quarter of the business in a single regulatory decision.

## Conclusion

Over ten years the wine education industry will grow in headcount but change in shape: fewer, larger providers; more employer-funded and less self-funded study; more non-English curricula authored locally rather than translated; and a syllabus that has to teach sustainability and alcohol risk as competently as it teaches Burgundy. The industry's main challenge is that its historic customer — the self-funding hospitality professional — is getting poorer; its main opportunity is that its historic non-customer, the producer and the retailer, has the strongest commercial reason to start paying.
