## The decision being evaluated

The question is a planting decision taken now: whether to establish a new block on its own roots rather than on American or hybrid rootstock. That framing excludes the romance of surviving pre-phylloxera vineyards, which tell us that ungrafted vines *can* live, not that planting them today is sound. I evaluate the decision on five axes — pest exposure, everything else a rootstock does, propagation, commercial exposure, and law and marketing — and my position is that it is defensible only in a narrow set of conditions, because the rewards are modest and reversible while the principal risk is catastrophic and permanent.

## Rewards

**Lower and simpler establishment.** Own-rooted planting material is cheaper than a bench graft and can be produced on site from cuttings of proven vines. There is no graft union, so no incompatibility between scion and stock, no union failure years later, and one fewer entry point for trunk pathogens and, in cold regions, crown gall.

**Propagation and stand maintenance.** Layering lets a grower fill a gap with a cane from a neighbouring vine, restoring stand density without planting a young vine into a mature block or field-grafting. Over decades this holds vine age and uniformity in a way grafted blocks cannot, and it allows a self-perpetuating massal selection independent of nursery supply — no small advantage given documented trunk-disease contamination and virus status problems in commercial nursery stock.

**Recovery after winter kill.** In continental regions where trunks are periodically killed by extreme cold, an own-rooted vine can be re-established from a sucker and comes back true to variety, whereas a grafted vine that dies below the union regrows as rootstock and must be replanted or field-grafted. Against that, vinifera roots are themselves less cold-hardy than the American species used for stocks, so the advantage only holds where the soil does not freeze deeply.

**Site match without an imposed intermediary.** The rootstock dictates vigour, phenology and rooting depth. On a low-vigour granitic sand or a poor volcanic soil, a rootstock chosen from a short commercial list may devigorate a vine that is already balanced, or push vigour where none is wanted. Own roots remove that mismatch.

**Marketing.** Franc de pied, pie franco and *ungrafted* carry a genuine premium in a market that rewards scarcity and narrative — Chile, Santorini, Colares, sand blocks in South Australia and Washington. This is a real and monetisable reward, and I would rate it above the quality argument.

## Risks

**Phylloxera, which is the asset-destroying one.** The root-feeding form attacks vinifera roots directly, and there is no cure once established. Sand-dominant soils genuinely suppress it, which is why the exceptions are sandy or volcanic, but "phylloxera-free region" is a status, not a property: the insect travels on machinery, boots, water and planting material, and the collapse of AXR-1 plantings in California from the 1980s is the standing lesson in relying on a resistance assumption. A block lost at year twelve is a loss of the establishment capital, the intervening cash flow, and three further years of fallow and replanting.

**Nematodes, and the mistake of thinking only about phylloxera.** Root-knot and citrus nematodes reduce vigour and yield, and dagger nematode transmits fanleaf virus. Nematode-resistant rootstocks — Ramsey, Freedom, Harmony and the newer Californian selections — exist precisely because vinifera roots have no resistance. On replant ground, this is often the more probable failure.

**Everything else the rootstock does.** Lime tolerance on calcareous soils, where own-rooted vinifera becomes chlorotic and is effectively unviable in Champagne, Cognac or Jerez; salinity and chloride exclusion under irrigation in inland Australia, Argentina and California; drought tolerance and rooting depth from 110R or 140Ru; waterlogging tolerance; and the deliberate matching of vigour to soil fertility. Choosing own roots forfeits all of these simultaneously.

**The quality claim is unproven.** Comparisons of ungrafted and grafted wine are confounded by vine age, low yields, massal selection material and site. I would not capitalise a quality premium into the planting decision; I would capitalise the marketing premium, which is real whether or not the wine is better.

## Commercial and legal reality

The financial shape of the risk is what should decide it. Establishment is a multi-year cash outflow recovered over the block's life; own roots reduce the outflow slightly and put the whole recovery at risk. Lenders and insurers may not accept an unrooted block as security. And the choice may not be the grower's: quarantine regimes such as Australia's phylloxera exclusion and infested zones restrict movement of planting material, EU plant-passport rules govern propagation and sale, and some appellations and certification schemes constrain material. Any candidate answer must check legality before viticulture.

## Conclusion

Plant ungrafted only where four conditions hold together: the soil is genuinely sand-dominated or the region has an enforced quarantine, nematode pressure is low, the soil chemistry does not require a rootstock's lime, salt or drought tolerance, and the business can absorb losing the block outright. Where they hold — Santorini, Chilean sands, Colares, isolated South Australian and Washington sites — the propagation and marketing rewards are real. Everywhere else the rootstock is the cheapest insurance policy in viticulture, and the rational hedge is a small own-rooted trial block inside a grafted planting rather than the whole vineyard.
