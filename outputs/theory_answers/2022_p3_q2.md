## The decision and the framework

Filtration is not a default setting; it is a risk decision, and in each case the real question is whether *not* filtering is an option. That turns on how vulnerable the packaged wine is to post-bottling change — above all re-fermentation by *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, growth of a spoilage yeast such as *Brettanomyces*, or malolactic bacteria acting on residual malic acid — and secondarily on whether it will throw a deposit or haze that the buyer will reject.

Against that sits the cost to quality. Every filtration removes something: tight sheets and membranes strip colour, aroma and palate weight; the operation adds shear and dissolved oxygen; and a blinded filter forces higher differential pressure, which damages the wine further while slowing the line.

Two principles follow and apply to all three wines. First, clarify before you filter — settling, fining, centrifugation or crossflow — so the final filter does the minimum work. That saves media, time and money, and it protects the wine. Second, match the medium and pore size to the purpose: kieselguhr or crossflow for gross clarification, lenticular sheets in the 5 to 1 μm range for polishing, membranes at 0.65 μm as a prefilter and 0.45 μm for sterility. And a sterile filter is worthless without sterile hygiene downstream of it: CIP of the line, a sterilised filler and bottle, and a bubble-point integrity test on the membrane before and after the run.

## a) White wine with 180 g/L residual sugar

The high-risk case, and the only one where I regard filtration as non-negotiable. That much fermentable sugar at a modest alcohol — a Sauternes, a Beerenauslese, a late-harvest Chenin — will restart if a single viable yeast cell survives. The outcome is a gassy, cloudy bottle or a pushed cork: a recall, not a stylistic debate.

So sterile membrane filtration at 0.45 μm, protected by a prefilter cascade, and a sterile bottling environment. Sulphur dioxide supports the decision but cannot replace it: sweet wines are held to a higher molecular SO2 target, of the order of 0.8 mg/L, which at typical pH means free SO2 well above a dry white's. DMDC at bottling is an adjunct where permitted, but it has no residual effect and does not substitute for the membrane.

The complication the question points at is filterability. Botrytised fruit brings β-glucan, a colloid that blinds filters almost immediately. The answer is a β-glucanase enzyme treatment with adequate contact time, then a filterability index or Vmax test on the treated wine to size the train before bottling day rather than discovering the problem on the line. Thorough prior clarification here is a quality measure, not only an economic one: it lets an already concentrated wine pass a tight membrane once, cleanly.

## b) Ruby port

Ruby is always filtered, and the reason is not microbial. At around 19 to 20% abv, fortification has put the wine beyond the ethanol tolerance of *Saccharomyces* and of *Brettanomyces*, and there is no malic conversion left to fear, so the roughly 100 g/L of residual sugar is stable in bottle.

The risk is physical. A young, deeply coloured, phenolic-rich wine will deposit colouring matter, tartrates and colloidal material, and basic ruby is a commercial style bought to be opened and poured without decanting. A deposit that is a mark of authenticity in a crusted or vintage port — styles bottled unfiltered precisely so that they throw a crust — is a fault in ruby.

The handling is two-stage. Fining and cold stabilisation first, then a diatomaceous earth filtration to remove the bulk of the solids, followed by a cellulose sheet filtration to give brilliance. Sterile membrane filtration is unnecessary and would cost colour for no benefit. Here the choice is not whether to filter but how coarsely one can afford to.

## c) Bourgogne Pinot Noir

Here not filtering is a genuine option, and I would take it if the analysis supports it. A Bourgogne rouge is dry, has completed malolactic fermentation in cask, and has spent ten to sixteen months in barrel with rackings. It is naturally close to bright and has almost nothing left to ferment.

The decision must rest on numbers rather than romance: residual sugar below about 2 g/L, malic acid effectively absent, free SO2 adequate for the pH, low turbidity after settling, and a microbiological screen — plating or PCR — clear of *Brettanomyces*, which lives on trace sugars and is the one real in-bottle risk in an oak-aged Pinot Noir. If those are clean, bottling unfiltered preserves texture, aromatic lift and mid-palate weight, and a light deposit in a red Burgundy is accepted by its buyers.

If any of them is not clean — or if this is a négociant volume going into non-temperature-controlled retail, where a haze becomes a customer complaint — I would take the lightest option the style tolerates: a polishing filtration at around 1.2 μm, or a lenticular sheet of equivalent rating, which removes haze-forming particles with minimal stripping.

## Conclusion

One principle produces three different answers: filter to the wine's risk, not to habit. The sweet wine must be sterile-filtered because it can ferment; the ruby must be brightly filtered because it will deposit; the Pinot Noir needs only polishing, or nothing at all, because it can do neither.
