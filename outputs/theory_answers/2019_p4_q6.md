## Scope

By the investment market I mean wine bought to be resold rather than drunk: a narrow set of wines with deep secondary-market liquidity, verifiable provenance, proven ageing capacity and sufficient production to trade, held in bond and valued by reference to an observable price. That excludes almost all fine wine, which is bought to drink. The distinction matters because the drivers are financial — liquidity, transparency, transaction cost, correlation — not organoleptic.

## Size and shape, 2009 against 2019

Estimates of the annual tradeable fine wine market cluster around five billion US dollars, perhaps double the figure of a decade earlier, though every such figure is an estimate and should be treated as one. Better evidence sits in market structure. The Liv-ex Fine Wine 100 peaked in mid-2011 and then fell by around a third into 2014 — a Bordeaux bubble inflated by Chinese and Hong Kong demand after Hong Kong abolished its wine duty in 2008, and punctured by aggressive en primeur release pricing on the 2010 and 2011 vintages. The recovery from 2015 was of a different character: broader, slower and less Bordeaux-dependent.

The most striking single measure of change is breadth. The number of distinct wines traded on the exchange rose several-fold over the decade, and the indices multiplied to track the regions that emerged.

## Production: the end of Bordeaux's monopoly

This is the decade's defining shift. Bordeaux's share of secondary-market trade by value fell sharply over the decade, from a large majority around 2010 to a minority by 2019. Burgundy took the prestige and much of the price appreciation, with the top domaines producing tiny volumes against demand that grew globally — scarcity that delivered spectacular returns but poor liquidity, since a market cannot trade what barely exists. Champagne emerged as a genuine investment category with the advantage Burgundy lacks: volume, and therefore liquidity. Italy grew on Piedmont and Tuscany, and the Rest of the World index brought in Californian cult wines and a small number of Australian and Spanish names.

Two structural causes underlie it. First, Bordeaux priced its en primeur releases so close to, or above, mature back vintages that the investment case disappeared; buyers went looking elsewhere. Second, price transparency through Liv-ex and Wine-Searcher made comparison across regions trivial, which allowed capital to move.

## Destination: from Hong Kong to a distributed base

The decade opened with demand concentrated in Hong Kong and mainland China and dominated by first-growth Bordeaux. It closed with a distributed buyer base — the US as a large and growing buyer, continental Europe, and Asian demand spread across Singapore, South Korea, Japan and the mainland, with a marked Burgundy bias. Concentration risk fell accordingly, which is why the post-2015 market has been less volatile than the pre-2011 one.

## Other structural changes

Authority diffused: Robert Parker's withdrawal from reviewing Bordeaux in the mid-2010s ended the era in which one score moved a price, replaced by a plural critical landscape whose effect on prices is weaker and slower. Product structure changed too: alongside merchant case-trading came managed portfolios, funds, fractional-ownership platforms and app-based investing aimed at retail investors. And the decade produced a run of fraud — both counterfeit bottles and investment schemes that collapsed — which drove the adoption of bonded storage, provenance tagging and authentication technology, and reminded buyers that wine is an unregulated investment in the UK with no compensation scheme behind it.

## The next ten years

**1. Further broadening, but with liquidity as the constraint.** Supply is fixed by appellation and cannot respond to demand, so the scarcest names keep appreciating while the tradeable market grows at the second tier, where there is enough wine to make a market. I expect Champagne, Piedmont, Tuscany and Napa to gain share, and a handful of Rhône, Spanish and Australian names to enter. Burgundy will retain its prestige and its prices but will become less relevant as a market, because illiquidity is a defect in an investment asset.

**2. En primeur shrinks to a few names.** The arithmetic is unavoidable: if the release price exceeds the back-vintage price, there is no return. Bordeaux has repeatedly declined to leave margin, and the trade has already shifted toward physically available stock. I expect en primeur to survive only where scarcity genuinely rations allocation.

**3. Climate becomes a valuation input.** Vintage variation is compressing in some regions and rising in others; frost and fire years create genuine scarcity and will be priced as such. Provenance and storage records will move from good practice to price determinant, because heat exposure in transit is now a live risk.

**4. Regulation arrives, probably after a scandal.** Retail fractional platforms selling an unregulated asset to inexperienced investors is a familiar precedent from other alternative assets. I expect a mis-selling episode and a regulatory response within the period.

**5. Returns moderate.** The 2005-2011 Bordeaux run was a one-off repricing driven by a new buyer nation. Absent an equivalent, fine wine should behave as what it is: a low-correlation diversifier delivering modest real returns with high transaction costs — attractive as a portfolio component, not as a growth asset.
