## Scope

By "packing or bottling in the local market of consumption" I mean shipping wine in bulk — flexitank or ISO tank — and packaging it at destination, whether into glass, bag-in-box, PET or can. I answer only the commercial case, as the question asks: costs, prices, margins, working capital, bargaining power and regulation. The technical arguments about oxygen ingress, stability and handling are real but are a different question, and I set them aside deliberately.

## Commercial advantages

**Freight economics, which drive everything else.** A twenty-foot container holds roughly 9,000 to 10,000 litres of wine in bottle, but around 24,000 litres in a flexitank. Shipping the liquid alone therefore moves more than twice the wine for one freight cost, and the difference is larger still when the glass, carton and pallet are also being carried across the world to no purpose. For wine selling below about £8 or $10 retail, freight is a material share of landed cost, so this saving is often the difference between a viable listing and no listing.

**Packaging bought locally.** Glass is the heaviest and usually the most expensive component. Buying it in market removes the freight on the empty bottle, lets the packer use lightweight and high-recycled-content glass to suit local deposit and packaging-tax regimes, and matches the carton and pallet configuration to the retailer's warehouse.

**Working capital and agility.** The bulk stock is a single undifferentiated asset until the moment of packaging, so it can be committed late. A packer can respond to a promotion in weeks rather than months, avoids obsolete finished stock in the wrong SKU, and carries far less write-off risk. For retailer own-label, where forecast error is punished immediately, this is decisive.

**Format flexibility and market entry.** Bag-in-box, cans, 187ml and retailer-specific pack sizes rarely justify a dedicated production run at origin. Local packing also lowers the minimum order quantity for a first listing, so entry costs less.

**Carbon as a commercial factor.** Retailers now carry published supply-chain emissions targets, and bulk shipping is one of the largest single reductions available to them. That converts an environmental argument into a listing criterion.

## Commercial disadvantages

**It forfeits provenance, and with it price.** "Bottled in the UK" is a weaker proposition than estate bottling, and the law makes the point for us: Champagne, Rioja and Chianti Classico among many others require bottling within the delimited zone. The model is therefore confined by regulation as well as by consumer perception to the commodity end of the market.

**It destroys pricing power.** Bulk is a brokered, spot-priced, specification-driven market with published indications. Once a wine is a specification rather than a bottle, the buyer can substitute origin at every renewal, and the producer becomes a price-taker in a market where he was previously at least partly a price-setter. This is the central commercial cost and it is structural.

**Value migrates out of the producing country.** The bottling margin, the packaging spend and the employment move to the consuming market, and the export value per litre falls. Spain exports more wine by volume than any other country and realises a markedly lower average price per litre than the major exporters, a difference driven substantially by the bulk share of its trade.

**Bargaining power concentrates.** Each major market has only a handful of large contract packers, so the producer swaps a diversified customer base for dependence on a small number of intermediaries who also serve his competitors.

**Brand and integrity risk.** Quality control at arm's length in another jurisdiction, with the liquid arriving unpackaged, raises the commercial exposure to blending, substitution and counterfeit — and the reputational cost of any such incident falls on the origin, not the packer.

## Is it a good thing for the industry? No

Individually rational, collectively damaging — that is my verdict, and I will defend it.

The test I apply is simple: where did the freight saving go? If local bottling made the industry better off, we would see it in producer margins. We do not. It has shown up in retail price points and in retailer margin, because the party that captured the saving is the party with the negotiating power, and bulk shipping increases that power by making origins substitutable. The trend is excellent for retailers, good for large brand owners who can arbitrage supply, and good for consumers. It is bad for producing regions, which lose value-added activity and see their average export price fall, and bad for the industry as a whole because it accelerates the commoditisation of the bottom two-thirds of the market — the two-thirds that most needs to escape it.

The exception I would concede is genuinely commodity wine. For a £5 bottle, shipping glass around the world is indefensible on cost and on carbon, and resisting bulk there is wasted effort. The industry's real task is not to fight the trend but to hold a firm line above it, and appellation bottling rules — often criticised as protectionist — are in fact the most effective commercial defence the producing regions have.
