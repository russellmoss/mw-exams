## The comparative frame

These four regions occupy the extremes of two axes, and comparing them along those axes is more useful than describing each in turn. The first axis is heat: the Mosel must gather every available degree, Chateauneuf-du-Pape must shed heat and conserve water, and Alsace and Marlborough sit between. The second is vigour and water: Marlborough has too much of both, Chateauneuf-du-Pape too little, and the training system in each place is largely a solution to that imbalance. Topography then overrides everything in the Mosel, and variety complicates the picture at Chateauneuf. I take these drivers in turn.

## Slope and mechanisation

The Mosel is the only one of the four where terrain, not physiology, dictates the system. On the steepest slate parcels — the steepest Mosel parcels exceed sixty per cent gradient — running wires across the slope is impractical and a tractor is impossible, so vines are trained individually on a single tall stake and worked up and down the slope on foot or by monorail. The cane is bent into the characteristic Mosel arch around the stake, which concentrates the shoots into a compact plume, holds the fruit low against slate that radiates stored heat at night, and keeps the canopy presented to the sun on a slope where light is the scarce resource.

The other three regions have terrain that permits wire trellising, and all three use it. That is the first real comparison: the Mosel's stake training is not a stylistic preference but the only option on ground the other three do not have, and where Mosel slopes are gentler or have been reconfigured into wider terraces, growers have moved to conventional wire-trained vertical shoot positioning precisely so they can mechanise.

## The same system doing different jobs

Vertical shoot positioning appears in the Mosel, in Alsace and in Marlborough, and it is doing something different in each. In the Mosel it serves mechanisation and uniform ripening on the accessible sites. In Alsace it is a disease and frost solution: on the fertile lower slopes and valley floor, VSP lifts the fruit clear of frost-prone ground and opens the canopy for air movement and spray penetration, which matters for thin-skinned, rot-prone Riesling and Gewurztraminer in a region whose dryness is not a guarantee. Alsace also varies the system within itself — single or double guyot with a straight or arched cane on vigorous sites to fill the fruiting wire and even out budburst, spur-pruned cordon on the poorer granite and limestone hillsides, with different leaf-removal and trimming strategies for the aromatic whites.

In Marlborough, VSP is a vigour-management system. Deep, fertile, well-watered alluvial soils on the Wairau plains produce far more shoot growth than the crop needs, so the answer is a tall trellis, long four-cane pruning to spread that vigour over more buds, wide rows — Marlborough's row spacing is commonly around 2.4 metres — to admit light into a big canopy, and full mechanisation of pruning, trimming and harvest to keep cost per tonne low. On the most vigorous blocks growers divide the canopy vertically, using systems of the Scott Henry type, to expose more leaf area without shading the fruit. The comparison with Alsace is direct: the same VSP skeleton, but Alsace uses it to open a moderate canopy and Marlborough uses it to house an excessive one.

## Heat, wind and water at Chateauneuf-du-Pape

Chateauneuf inverts the Mosel's problem. Free-standing gobelet needs no wire, which matters where the mistral would flog a trellised canopy; the low, self-supporting head of old wood is stable in that wind; the bowl of shoots shades its own fruit against sunburn and slows ripening; and the low, unirrigated vine on galet-covered, drought-prone soils has neither the vigour nor the water to fill a trellis in any case. Where the Mosel arch is designed to expose fruit, the gobelet is designed to hide it.

But Chateauneuf is not simply bush vines. Grenache, with upright growth and strong wood, suits the free-standing head; Syrah has a trailing habit, needs support, and is generally trained to guyot on wire; Mourvedre, needing heat and late ripening, is usually kept as gobelet. The best comparison in this question is therefore internal: one appellation running two systems because two varieties have different growth habits.

## Why the alternatives fail

Test each system in the others' conditions and the logic closes. Gobelet in Marlborough would give too little leaf area for the crop, trap humidity in a wet maritime spring, and forfeit mechanisation in a high-labour-cost country. VSP at Chateauneuf would expose fruit to sunburn and the mistral and demand water the site does not have. Bush vines in Alsace would hold humidity around thin-skinned aromatic whites and block the tractor in narrow rows. And wire systems on the Mosel's steepest slate simply cannot be installed or worked.

## Conclusion

Ranked by force, the drivers are topography first — it removes all choice in the Mosel — then the vigour-and-water balance, which explains why Marlborough spreads a canopy and Chateauneuf contains one, then variety, which is why Chateauneuf runs two systems at once. Heat management cuts across all of them, and it explains the sharpest contrast in the group: the Mosel arch and the Provencal gobelet are opposite answers to the same question about where the sun should fall.
