## Scope, and how I am reading the question

"Prior to alcoholic fermentation" spans three moments: pre-harvest maturity sampling, the must at receival, and the clarified juice immediately before yeast is pitched. Each supports a different decision — when to pick, how to process, what to correct — so I organise by the decision each analysis serves rather than by a list of tests. The governing principle is that an analysis is worth running only if a different result would change what the winemaker does.

## Sampling comes before analysis

Sampling error dwarfs analytical error. A berry sample must span rows, vine positions and bunch shoulders and tips; a tank sample must be drawn after homogenisation, not off a valve into which solids have settled. A precise number on an unrepresentative sample is worse than a rough number on a good one, because it is trusted.

## Ripeness and potential alcohol

Sugar is measured in the field by refractometer as degrees Brix and in the winery by hydrometer as density or Baumé. Both are indirect: they read total dissolved solids, so on botrytis-affected or raisined fruit they overstate fermentable sugar. Where the number carries a real cost — a fortified wine's cut-off, or an alcohol declaration near a tax band — an enzymatic glucose and fructose assay in g/L gives the true fermentable load and hence potential alcohol.

The glucose-to-fructose ratio is worth having on high-sugar musts. Saccharomyces cerevisiae is glucophilic, so a must that starts fructose-dominant, as botrytised fruit does, finishes on the sugar the yeast likes least and is likelier to stick — which changes the strain chosen and the nutrition regime.

## Acid balance

Titratable acidity, in g/L as tartaric acid, and pH must both be measured, because potassium buffering decouples them: a must can be high in acid and high in pH at once. pH targets are style-specific: a sparkling base is typically picked at the low end of the 2.9 to 3.4 range that AWRI cites for white and sparkling base wines together, and a premium red at roughly 3.4 to 3.7. A pH of 3.8 is high for a sparkling base but unremarkable in a fortified Muscat. Acidification or deacidification is decided here, because it integrates better before fermentation than after.

Two supporting analyses complete the picture. Potassium, in mg/L, predicts how far pH will climb during fermentation and after malolactic conversion, and forewarns of tartrate instability. Malic acid, in g/L, determines whether malolactic conversion is planned and how much further pH will rise if it happens.

## Fermentation insurance

Yeast assimilable nitrogen (YAN) — ammonium plus primary amino nitrogen, as mg N/L — is the single most valuable pre-fermentation number. A deficiency gives a sluggish or stuck fermentation and hydrogen sulphide; an excess gives surplus biomass, heat and residual nitrogen for spoilage organisms. Broadly, a fermentation needs of the order of 150 to 220 mg N/L for a must at around 22 to 23 Brix, scaling upward with sugar, and the result sets the nutrient addition, its timing and its form.

Juice turbidity, in nephelometric turbidity units (NTU), is the companion measure for whites. Over-clarified juice ferments slowly and finishes lean; under-clarified juice turns coarse and sulphidic. White juice is usually settled to something like 100 to 250 NTU, higher for a barrel-fermented wine and lower for a delicately aromatic one.

## Protection and fruit health

Free and total sulphur dioxide (SO2), in mg/L, tells the winemaker what protection is already in place before more is added: it prevents oxidation of the juice during settling and suppresses spontaneous fermentation until a ferment is deliberately started. Molecular SO2 is what acts, so the free figure must be read against pH — the same 25 mg/L free is protective at pH 3.2 and nearly inert at pH 3.7. Botrytised fruit binds SO2 heavily, so total minus free shows how much has already been consumed.

Fruit condition adds three more where warranted. A laccase assay on botrytis-affected fruit determines whether the juice can meet oxygen at all. Microbial analysis, by plating or quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), quantifies Brettanomyces bruxellensis, acetic acid bacteria and spoilage yeasts on damaged fruit, with volatile acidity in g/L as a cheap proxy. In a fire year, free and bound smoke-taint volatile phenols in micrograms per litre are measured on the juice, because the bound glycosides hydrolyse later; that result decides whether to press hard, avoid skin contact, or decline the fruit. Copper and iron, in mg/L, matter where late copper sprays or old equipment are in play: copper above roughly 0.5 mg/L brings haze and reductive risk, and iron catalyses oxidation.

## What is worth running

Infrared spectroscopy delivers sugar, pH, titratable acidity, malic acid and YAN in a minute at negligible marginal cost, and belongs in any winery of scale. Enzymatic and chromatographic work is sent out and costs meaningfully more per sample, so it should be reserved for decisions that justify it; smoke taint panels are slow and expensive and are ordered only after fire. The discipline is not to run everything, but to run what will change a decision, on a sample worth trusting.
