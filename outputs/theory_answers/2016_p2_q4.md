## What malolactic fermentation is

Malolactic fermentation is the bacterial decarboxylation of the dicarboxylic L-malic acid to the monocarboxylic L-lactic acid, with the release of carbon dioxide, converting one gram per litre of malic acid into roughly 0.67 grams per litre of lactic acid. Because one acid group is lost, the wine becomes both less acidic and softer in the mouth: lactic acid is perceived as rounder than the sharp, green malic acid it replaces. Titratable acidity falls, typically by 1 to 3 g/L, and pH rises, typically by 0.1 to 0.3 units. It is not a fermentation in the yeast sense at all — no sugar is consumed and no alcohol produced.

The organism is Oenococcus oeni, a lactic acid bacterium renamed from Leuconostoc oenos in the late 1990s. It occurs naturally on grapes and in cellar surfaces and equipment, and spontaneous MLF is entirely normal; winemakers also inoculate with cultured strains for speed and control. It is preferred over the other wine lactic acid bacteria, Lactobacillus and Pediococcus, because it tolerates pH below 3.2 and ethanol to around 14 per cent while producing fewer off-flavours.

## Controlling it

MLF is conducted at 18 to 22 degrees Celsius; below about 16 it stalls, and there is no wine bacterium working happily at 36. It requires low free SO2, a pH above roughly 3.1 and warmth. Conversely, blocking it is achieved by cold, by free SO2 held above roughly 15 mg/L, by lysozyme, and finally by sterile filtration at bottling. Blocking is not free: it commits the producer to a microbially fragile wine that must be filtered and kept cool, so the style decision carries a handling cost.

## White wines and sparkling bases

This is where the largest stylistic swing occurs, and where most of the marks lie. In a barrel-fermented white, full MLF lowers acidity, adds body through the bacterium's own metabolism, and generates diacetyl from citric acid — the buttery, hazelnut note that defines the style. Diacetyl is perceptible from around 1 to 4 mg/L, and it is not fixed: extended lees contact reduces it to near-odourless acetoin and butanediol, so the same completed MLF can taste buttery or merely creamy depending on what follows it.

Blocking MLF is the default for aromatic whites — Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Albariño, Grüner Veltliner — because the crisp malic acidity and the volatile thiols and terpenes are the product. Partial MLF, on a fraction of the blend, is the most useful tool of all: it gives a continuous dial between the two poles, widely used in Chablis, Sancerre and cool-climate New World whites.

In sparkling base wines the decision is different again, because acidity is structural. Full MLF is used to soften a lean base and, crucially, to guarantee that MLF cannot occur in bottle after tirage, which would cause haze, extra gas and off-flavours. In warm-climate sparkling production, and by producers seeking maximum tension and ageing potential, MLF is deliberately blocked and the wine sterile-filtered instead.

## Red wines

It is wrong to wave reds through with "all reds go through MLF", even though most do. In red wine MLF is as much a stability decision as a style one: because the wines are usually bottled with low SO2 and higher pH, removing malic acid is the only practical way to prevent a bottle refermentation. The stylistic gain is softer acidity and a rounder texture; conducted in barrel, it also integrates oak better than tank MLF does.

The exceptions are real and instructive. Nouveau and other carbonic-macerated styles bottled within weeks of harvest do not have time for MLF and rely on sterile handling instead. Sweet reds — a growing commercial category — must have both malic acid and residual sugar protected from bacteria, so MLF is blocked and the wines are sterile-filtered. Some producers in hot regions block MLF to avoid pushing an already high pH higher, since above about pH 3.8 the wine becomes microbiologically vulnerable, and acidify instead.

## Fortified wines

In Port and other mutage styles the addition of spirit arrests yeast and bacteria alike, so the wine keeps its malic acid; the resulting freshness under high sugar and alcohol is part of the style, and the absence of MLF here is a structural rather than an incidental fact.

## Impacts beyond flavour

MLF also carries risks that shape whether it is used at all: volatile acidity if Lactobacillus or Pediococcus dominate, biogenic amines including histamine, mousiness, and geranium taint if sorbate meets lactic acid bacteria. Against that, a completed MLF is a microbiological insurance policy: a wine with no malic acid left has nothing for lactic acid bacteria to act on in bottle, which is why it can be bottled with less SO2 and looser filtration than a malic-retaining wine of the same style. That trade-off — flavour freedom bought with handling cost, or handling freedom bought with flavour change — is the real substance of the decision.

## Conclusion

MLF changes style in three ways at once — it lowers and softens acidity, it adds or masks aroma through diacetyl and lees, and it settles the wine's microbial fate. Its absence is an equally deliberate stylistic act, bought with sulphur, cold and filtration.
