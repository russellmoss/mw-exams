## The definition I propose

A fine wine is a wine that (i) comes from a delimited and named origin in restricted quantity, (ii) demonstrably rewards bottle age or at least holds its quality over a decade or more, (iii) commands and sustains a substantial price premium validated by parties other than its producer, and (iv) is traded, discussed and re-sold beyond its original route to market.

All four clauses are load-bearing, and I will justify each. Restricted quantity from a named origin excludes wines that can be made to order at volume, however well made; it is the condition that makes the other three possible. Ageing capacity is not an aesthetic preference but the mechanism that creates a secondary market at all — a wine that must be drunk on release cannot be stored, resold or collected. Third-party validation is what separates fine wine from expensive wine: the premium must be conferred by critics, sommeliers, auctioneers, collectors or the trade, not asserted by the producer's own price list. And trade beyond the original route to market is the observable test of the other three; if nobody will buy the wine second-hand, the market has declined the claim.

This is deliberately a socio-commercial definition rather than an organoleptic one. That is not evasion. "Fine" has never described a measurable property of liquid; it is a category the trade invented to differentiate its best wines and to justify their value to buyers, and a definition that ignores that function will fail the first boundary test put to it.

## Where the boundary falls

A definition is only useful if it excludes. Four tests.

Chablis Grand Cru Les Clos from a serious domaine against an excellent Petit Chablis from the same house: both are high-quality wines and the second may be better value, but only the first is produced in restricted quantity from a delimited site, ages for fifteen years, and trades at auction. Fine; not fine.

A benchmark Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc against a Napa Cabernet under allocation: the Sauvignon may be technically flawless and internationally admired, but it is made to demand, drunk within three years and has no resale market. The Cabernet is scarce, cellarable and re-traded. Quality does not decide this; structure of supply and market behaviour do.

Grower Champagne from a single lieu-dit against a large-house non-vintage brut: the second is often the better-made wine and certainly the more consistent, but the first satisfies the definition and the second, made in millions of bottles, does not. This is uncomfortable, and I accept the discomfort — my definition says explicitly that fineness is not a synonym for quality.

Finally, a serious Cornas or a Barolo from a modest producer against a heavily packaged, sustainably farmed, expensive new-release cuvée with no track record: the first is fine, the second is not. Packaging aligns with status but does not create it; organic or natural production is a method, not a rank; and price set unilaterally by a producer is precisely the claim my third clause refuses to accept at face value.

## Where the term came from

The category is a commercial artefact with a traceable history. It grew out of the London trade in claret, port and hock, and was formalised by classification — the 1855 classification of the Médoc and Sauternes was itself a ranking of brokers' prices, which tells you the criterion was market standing from the beginning. Christie's held wine sales from the eighteenth century, giving the category a secondary market and, crucially, a public price record. En primeur turned fine wine into a forward-traded commodity. Robert Parker's Wine Advocate, founded in the late 1970s, replaced merchant authority with a numerical, consumer-facing one. The Liv-ex exchange, founded around the turn of the millennium, gave the trade a screen price and completed the transformation of fine wine into an asset class with observable liquidity.

Historically the membership was far narrower and more consistent than today: predominantly French, with a handful of other famous European regions — Rhine and Mosel, Piedmont, Tuscany, Rioja, Douro — admitted on long-established reputation. Fineness was attached to region first and producer second, and the arbiters were a small number of merchants and critics in London and, later, the United States.

## What has changed

**From region-led to producer-led.** The classified name has weakened relative to the individual grower. Burgundy is the clearest case: a village-level wine from a sought-after domaine now outsells a classified growth, which would have been unthinkable when the category was defined by hierarchy of place.

**From French to global.** Napa, Barolo, Champagne as a still-wine-priced collectible, Australia, and increasingly the Rhône and Germany have taken share; Bordeaux's share of secondary-market trade by value has fallen sharply since its peak in about 2011.

**From cellar to asset.** Investment potential, provenance documentation, bonded storage and index pricing are now part of what "fine" means. This is the most consequential change, because it imports criteria — liquidity, condition, brand recognition — that have nothing to do with taste.

**From critic to network.** Authority has dispersed from a small number of scoring critics to sommeliers, importers, retailers and social platforms. That has admitted styles the old hierarchy excluded, notably lower-intervention and cooler-climate wines, and has made the category's boundaries genuinely contested rather than merely defended.

**Not, however, a generational retreat.** The claim that younger drinkers are indifferent to fine wine confuses two things. Overall alcohol consumption among younger cohorts is falling, but interest at the very top appears to be holding or rising: Sotheby's reported that around 30 per cent of its wine and spirits clientele in 2022 was under 40, and fine wine competes for the same wallet as watches and art among high-earning young buyers. Drinking less and collecting more are compatible behaviours.

## Fine wine and high-quality wine

The two have not merged, but the line has moved. Quality has become abundant: competent, characterful wine at every price is the achievement of the last forty years, and it has stripped quality of its power to discriminate. Fineness therefore rests more heavily than before on the scarce, non-replicable elements — site, history, restricted volume, provenance — and on market recognition. The parallel with the art market is exact: technical skill is widespread and cheap to acquire; attribution, provenance and scarcity are what set price.

## Conclusion

I propose that a fine wine is a scarce wine of delimited origin that ages, that carries a premium validated by third parties, and that trades on. The definition is contemporary precisely because it is functional rather than aesthetic, and because it survives the two changes that broke the older definitions: the globalisation of quality and the financialisation of the category. Great wine is a judgement about liquid; fine wine is a judgement made by a market about a wine. They overlap substantially and are not the same thing, and any definition that conflates them cannot explain why an outstanding Petit Chablis will never be called fine.
