I take (a), (b) and (c). Each note gives the mechanism, how the fault is confirmed, and what can be done.

## a. Mousiness

A retronasal-only fault: a stale, savoury taint likened to popcorn, cracker or rodent cage, absent on the nose and appearing several seconds after swallowing, with a long dirty finish. The responsible compounds are N-heterocycles — 2-acetyltetrahydropyridine, 2-ethyltetrahydropyridine and 2-acetylpyrroline — which at wine pH are protonated and effectively non-volatile. They become volatile only when the wine is diluted and neutralised by saliva at around pH 7, which is why the fault escapes a nose-only screening and why a routine bench tasting that does not swallow or hold the wine will pass a mousy lot. Sensitivity varies enormously between tasters and some are effectively blind to it, so a single palate is not a QC method.

The producers are Brettanomyces and heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria, working on the amino acids lysine and ornithine. The risk conditions are therefore predictable: little or no sulphur dioxide, pH above roughly 3.6, residual sugar, oxygen exposure, long unprotected lees ageing and no filtration — which is precisely the profile of much low-intervention winemaking, where the fault has become commercially significant enough that importers now screen for it. Confirmation is by tasting protocol plus GC-MS.

Management is prevention, because there is no reliable cure at threshold: molecular SO2 held at 0.5-0.8 mg/L for the wine's pH, acidification where pH allows, sugar fermented dry or the wine sterile filtered, strict cellar hygiene and no ullage. A rub of wine on the back of the hand, tasted as it dries, is the cheap field check before a lot moves. The practical implication for a buyer is that mousiness often appears after shipping in wines that tasted clean at the cellar door, because the fault develops wherever residual microbial population meets warmth and time — so the screening point has to be the arriving container, not the tank.

## b. Light strike in bottled wine

A photochemical fault, "goût de lumière", affecting whites, rosés and sparkling wines in clear or flint glass. Riboflavin absorbs in the near-UV and blue band, roughly 370-450 nm, and in its excited state oxidises the sulphur-containing amino acids methionine and cysteine, generating volatile sulphur compounds — principally methanethiol and dimethyl disulphide — which read as cooked cabbage, wet wool, onion and drain. It develops fast: days of bright retail display can be enough, and modern LED and fluorescent shop lighting emits in the damaging band.

Riboflavin is largely yeast-derived, so the winemaking levers act on the substrate: yeast strain selection, limiting extended gross-lees contact and autolysis, and knowing your riboflavin concentration — of the order of 80-100 micrograms per litre is commonly cited as the level above which risk rises sharply. The packaging levers are stronger and cheaper: green or amber glass, UV-absorbing coatings, sleeves, cartons rather than open display, and a specification agreed with the retailer about chiller and display lighting. A pale Provence rosé in clear glass on an unlit shelf is a different risk from the same wine in a lit chiller cabinet, and the second case justifies the packaging cost. Sulphur dioxide is not a defence here — it is the wrong chemistry — and once the volatile sulphur compounds have formed, copper treatment is a bottled-wine impossibility. The fault is designed out, not corrected.

## c. Elevated volatile acidity in bottled wine

Volatile acidity is the steam-distillable acid fraction, chiefly acetic acid, expressed as acetic acid. What matters commercially are the EU ceilings of 1.2 g/L for red and 1.08 g/L for white and rose, but the fault is usually obvious well below them, from around 0.7-0.9 g/L depending on matrix, and it travels with ethyl acetate, which smells of nail varnish and is perceptible at a far lower concentration.

Because the wine is already bottled, this is a QC investigation, and the first question is when the VA arose. Analyse the suspect bottles for VA and ethyl acetate against the retained pre-bottling tank sample and against library bottles from the same run: if the retain is clean and bottles vary between themselves, the fault developed after filling; if the retain is high, it came in with the blend and the failure was the release decision, not the bottling.

For a post-bottling origin, work the two mechanisms. Microbial: check residual sugar, pH, free and molecular SO2 and plate or qPCR the bottles for acetic acid bacteria, Brettanomyces and lactic acid bacteria. Oxidative: check dissolved oxygen and total package oxygen records, closure fitting and torque, leakers, and warehouse temperature history, since acetic acid bacteria need oxygen and warmth. Then audit the line: sterile filtration through a 0.45 micron membrane with pre- and post-use integrity tests, CIP and filler-bowl hygiene records, and bottle sterility swabs.

Nothing can be done to the wine in bottle — VA removal by reverse osmosis is a pre-bottling operation — so the decision is whether to withdraw stock. Prevention of recurrence is a specification: dry or sterile-filtered wine, molecular SO2 matched to pH, oxygen limits at the filler, integrity-tested membranes, and a temperature clause in the logistics contract.
