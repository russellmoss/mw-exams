I take (a), (b) and (d). Each is a control on the same underlying risk — that a wine changes between the tank and the consumer — and each is a trade-off between security and the character of the wine.

## a) Preservatives in wine

A preservative is any agent that inhibits microbial spoilage or chemical degradation. Sulphur dioxide is the most important but far from the only one.

**Alcohol** is the original preservative and is easy to overlook. Most spoilage yeasts and lactic acid bacteria are inhibited above roughly 15 to 16% abv, which is the whole principle of fortification: Port is fortified to around 19 to 20% abv and is microbially secure despite substantial residual sugar.

**Sulphur dioxide** works as an anti-oxidant, an anti-microbial through the molecular fraction, an anti-oxidasic inhibiting laccase and tyrosinase, and a binder of acetaldehyde. EU total ceilings are 150 mg/L for dry red and 200 mg/L for dry white and rose, and a working target is 0.5 to 0.6 mg/L molecular, which depends on pH.

**Ascorbic acid** is an antioxidant only, with no antimicrobial action whatever. It scavenges oxygen very fast but generates hydrogen peroxide, so it is pro-oxidant unless partnered with adequate free SO2; it is a bottling addition for whites and sparkling, to an EU ceiling of 250 mg/L. **Sorbic acid**, as potassium sorbate, is the mirror image: antifungal only, with no antioxidant or antibacterial action, used to stop refermentation in off-dry and sweet wines and capped at 200 mg/L in the EU. Its weakness is that lactic acid bacteria metabolise it to the geranium taint, so it must be paired with SO2. Confusing these two is a common and expensive error.

**Lysozyme**, from egg white, is active against Gram-positive lactic acid bacteria and is used to delay or block malolactic fermentation; capped at 500 mg/L and an allergen. **Dimethyl dicarbonate (Velcorin)** is a sterilant, dosed in-line at up to 200 mg/L, which kills a low yeast population at the filler and then hydrolyses to methanol and carbon dioxide, leaving no residual protection at all.

## b) Preventing deposits in bottled wine

Deposits matter because consumers read them as faults and retailers reject on them. Tartrates are only the first of five.

**Tartrate crystals**, potassium bitartrate and calcium tartrate, are prevented by cold stabilisation at minus 4 degrees C for several days with seeding, by electrodialysis or cation exchange, or by additives: metatartaric acid, which is cheap but hydrolyses and protects for only roughly 12 to 18 months; CMC, effective and energy-free but destabilising to colour in red wines; and potassium polyaspartate, which does work in reds. Calcium tartrate is the trap — it does not respond to cold and can precipitate years later.

**Protein haze** comes from heat-unstable pathogenesis-related proteins in white wines, is diagnosed by heat test and prevented by a trialled bentonite addition, at a cost in lees volume and aroma.

**Metal casse**: ferric casse gives a white phosphate or blue-black tannate haze above roughly 7 mg/L of iron, and copper above about 0.5 mg/L throws a reddish-brown colloid in reductive whites. Prevention is avoiding pick-up from brass fittings and pumps, then citric acid, PVPP or, where permitted and supervised, blue fining.

**Microbial haze and sediment** from yeast, *Brettanomyces* or lactic bacteria are prevented by sterile filtration, adequate molecular SO2 and a sterilised filler.

**Colouring-matter deposit** in mature red wine is not a fault at all: it is the expected polymerisation of anthocyanins and tannin. Treating it aggressively strips the wine, so the answer there is packaging and consumer education, not processing.

## d) Pre-bottling filtration

Pre-bottling filtration is the whole clarifying sequence before the filler, not the final membrane alone, and the choices differ in mechanism.

**Clarifying filtration** removes gross solids: rotary vacuum drum filters for lees, and increasingly **crossflow (tangential) filtration** for bulk wine, which is a closed, continuous system needing no diatomaceous earth — removing both the disposal cost and the crystalline-silica health hazard — and picking up much less oxygen.

**Depth filtration** through kieselguhr plate-and-frame or lenticular cartridges holds large solid loads and is nominally rated, so it is prefiltration rather than a microbial barrier. **Sheet or pad filtration** works by adsorption as well as sieving, is available from coarse to sterile-rated, and carries the risk of paper taint and of stripping aroma and colour.

**Final membrane filtration** is the microbial cut: a 0.45 micron absolute-rated membrane behind a 0.65 micron prefilter, integrity-tested by bubble point before and after the run, and worthless unless the filler and lines have been sterilised.

The judgement is how tight is tight enough. Tight filtration removes colloids and can dull aromatics and thin texture, particularly in red wine, so a dry red with no sugar, no malic acid and a clean plate count may legitimately go through at 0.65 micron or unfiltered. Unfiltered is a real style choice, but it transfers the entire microbial risk to the SO2 regime and the cold chain, and it should be taken with the plate counts on the desk.
