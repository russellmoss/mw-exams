## What "better quality wine" has to mean

Quality is fitness for purpose: a wine is better when it delivers more of what its category promises at its price. For a supermarket varietal that means cleanliness, consistency of style year on year, fruit definition and reliable supply; for a single-vineyard wine it means concentration, balance, typicity, complexity and ageing potential. The two definitions demand different things of the vineyard, and any answer that assesses vineyard work against only the second is assessing a small fraction of the wine world. I take quality to include cost-adjusted quality, because a euro spent on hand labour is a euro not spent elsewhere in the vineyard.

The question also covers all vineyard work, not just picking. Pruning, shoot and bunch thinning, canopy management, weed and soil management, spraying and harvest all have manual and mechanised versions, and they do not all resolve the same way.

## Where the hand wins

Manual work is superior wherever the task requires judgement applied to an individual vine or bunch, or where terrain excludes machinery.

Pruning is the clearest case. Cane and spur decisions determine yield, balance and — through the size and placement of wounds — the vine's exposure to trunk disease. Mechanical pre-pruning followed by hand finishing is common, but a fully mechanised prune leaves a compromised vine structure and shortens vineyard life. Since trunk disease is a first-order threat to quality and to the capital value of old vines, this is not a marginal point.

Selective harvesting is the second. Sweet wines made by successive tries through a botrytised vineyard — Sauternes, Tokaji, the Mosel's late-picked Rieslings — cannot be mechanised, because the selection is the product. Whole-bunch pressing for traditional-method sparkling and whole-bunch fermentation for reds require intact bunches and therefore hand-picking. So does any wine relying on sorting at the vine.

Terrain closes the argument for a small number of regions: the Mosel's steepest slate, Douro terraces, Côte-Rôtie, Priorat, Ribeira Sacra, terraced Etna. Here the alternative is not a machine but abandonment.

## Where the machine wins

The claim that mechanisation is a quality compromise is now largely obsolete, and in several operations it is the machine that delivers the better wine.

Timing is the strongest argument. A machine harvests a large block inside its optimal picking window and can pick at night, delivering cool, intact fruit with less oxidation and lower sulphur dioxide requirement — a genuine quality gain in aromatic whites in Australia, California, Chile and the Languedoc alike. A hand crew of the size most estates can now assemble takes days over the same block, and fruit picked on the wrong day is a quality loss no cellar can repair. Modern harvesters with on-board destemming and optical sorting deliver fruit closer to hand-picked than the received wisdom allows.

Consistency is the second. A machine performs identically on row four hundred at three in the morning; a tired or untrained crew does not. Mechanical leaf removal and trimming are more uniform than hand work done at speed, and uniformity of fruit exposure is exactly what phenolic and aromatic ripeness depend on.

Precision viticulture is the third and least appreciated. Canopy-sensing and yield mapping allow selective harvest by zone, variable-rate spraying and fertigation, and targeted rather than blanket intervention. Recycling and sensor-guided sprayers cut chemical use substantially. Under-vine cultivation machinery makes herbicide-free farming viable at a scale hand-hoeing never could — a mechanised route to an outcome usually presented as artisanal.

## Labour, cost and price point

The decisive constraint is that skilled seasonal labour is scarce and getting scarcer almost everywhere: in France, in Napa and Sonoma, in New Zealand and Australia. Mechanisation is often not a choice between two qualities but between a mechanised operation and an operation not done at all, or done late — and late canopy work costs more quality than the machine ever did.

Cost then sets what is affordable. Hand-picking commonly costs several times machine harvesting per tonne. At bulk and mid-market price points that difference is the entire margin, and insisting on hand work would mean cutting spend on fungicide programmes, canopy work or better fruit — all of which matter more to the wine in the glass. At the top of the market, where fruit cost is a small share of the bottle price, hand work is affordable and its marginal gains are worth buying.

It is worth rejecting the national stereotype explicitly. Mechanisation is not a New World habit: the Languedoc, Bordeaux's larger appellations and much of Spain and Italy are heavily mechanised, while Central Otago, parts of Sonoma and premium Barossa sites are hand-farmed. The variable is site, style and price, not country.

## Conclusion

No — manual work does not deliver better wine per se. It delivers better wine in specific, identifiable circumstances: where a decision must be made vine by vine or bunch by bunch (pruning, selective and sweet-wine harvesting), where intact bunches are required by the style, and where the ground defeats machinery. Elsewhere, and particularly where timing, uniformity and scale drive quality, the mechanised alternative is equal or better, and it releases capital for work that improves the wine more. The best vineyards I know of at every price level are hybrids — mechanised where the machine is more reliable than a person, hand-worked where judgement is the input. Framing this as a contest between two philosophies rather than a task-by-task allocation is the error.
