## Defining old vines, and setting the mystique aside

There is no legal definition, so the definition has to be argued from context. Since the average commercial vine is replanted somewhere in its twenties or thirties — the economic rather than the biological lifespan — a vineyard that has passed that point is old, because it has survived the moment at which its replacement was financially rational. Codified schemes agree: the Barossa Old Vine Charter, which begins at 35 years and rises through Survivor, Centenarian and Ancestor tiers, and South Africa's Old Vine Project, which certifies vines of 35 years and over with a seal stating the planting date. In a region whose viticulture is a century old, forty years is old; in Santorini, whose ungrafted, basket-trained kouloura vines are continually renewed from an ancient root system, age is measured in the rootstock's centuries rather than the canopy's decades. I use 35 years and upward throughout.

Whether old-vine wine is better is not the question and I set it aside. The question is why these vineyards are hard to keep alive and how it is done.

## The challenges

**1. Declining and erratic productivity.** Yield falls with age and, more awkwardly, becomes variable and uneven between vines, so a block cannot be managed to a target tonnage. Bud fruitfulness declines, and vines that have lost cordon arms carry irregular crops that ripen at different rates.

**2. Trunk disease and the accumulated wound burden.** This is the central agronomic problem. Every winter cut is an infection point, so an old vine carries decades of accumulated Esca, Eutypa and Botryosphaeria colonisation in its permanent wood. Dead arms, dieback and apoplectic collapse are the visible result, and there is no curative treatment.

**3. Missing vines and mixed ages.** Gaps accumulate. Interplanting young vines creates a block of two ages with different vigour, ripening and water demand, harvested and pruned as one — which is a management problem, not just an aesthetic one.

**4. Mechanisation is largely impossible.** This is the challenge most often understated. Old vineyards are typically bush-trained or on obsolete trellis, irregularly spaced with rows too narrow for modern machinery, often on slopes, and frequently with trunks too fragile and gappy for a harvester to straddle and shake. Pruning, harvesting and often spraying must be done by hand, and hand work has to be skilled: pruning an irregular old vine to preserve its remaining structure cannot be given to an inexperienced crew.

**5. Labour supply.** That skilled seasonal labour is scarce and rising in cost everywhere old vineyards exist.

**6. Virus load and, sometimes, own-rooted fragility.** Decades of accumulated virus depress vigour further, and ungrafted survivors in phylloxera-free sands carry their own risks.

It is worth correcting one common error. Old vines are generally **less** susceptible to drought and water stress, not more: their deep, established root systems reach reserves young vines cannot, which is precisely why the world's surviving old vineyards are concentrated in dry regions — South Africa, Australia and Santorini — where they have outlasted everything planted around them.

## The solutions

**Remedial surgery rather than replanting.** Trunk renewal is the key maintenance operation: cut back below the necrosis and re-train a watershoot or sucker from the base to rebuild a trunk from the vine's own established root system. Curettage removes decayed wood from a standing trunk. Both restore a productive vine at a fraction of replanting cost and, importantly, without losing the root system that makes the vine valuable.

**Pruning as preservation.** Prune late and in dry weather, keep cuts small, avoid opening old wood, respect the sap flow, and protect wounds promptly. On an old vine this is not optimisation; it is the difference between another decade and none.

**Fill gaps by layering, not interplanting.** Where the training system permits, layering a cane from a neighbouring vine into the gap fills it with wood of the same age and genetic material rather than introducing a young vine of different vigour.

**Nutrition and soil.** The temptation is to feed a declining vine back to health. This has to be weighed against the soil's own fertility and the rootstock's vigour: on a naturally vigorous soil or a vigorous stock, added nitrogen produces canopy the old vine's compromised vascular system cannot support, stressing it further and worsening disease expression. The correct approach is usually restrained, diagnosed feeding — leaf and soil analysis first — with organic matter and careful water management to sustain root function rather than force growth.

**Canopy and harvest.** Accept unevenness and manage it: selective hand harvest in two passes, and shoot thinning to even the crop between strong and weak vines.

## The economics

This is what actually decides whether an old vineyard survives. A modern trellised block can be mechanically pruned and harvested, and its cost per tonne keeps falling as machinery improves. An old block cannot follow, so its costs rise with labour while its yield declines — a widening gap, in the wrong direction, on both terms of the equation.

The only durable solution is commercial. The value of the fruit must be raised until it covers the hand labour: single-block bottlings, old-vine designations on the label, and long-term grower contracts at premium prices. This is exactly what the Barossa Charter and the Old Vine Project are for — they are not romance, they are the mechanism that converts age into a price high enough to pay the pruners.
