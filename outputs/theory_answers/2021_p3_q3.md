## Definitions and my standpoint

Quality assurance is the system that prevents defects — written specifications, approved supplier lists, audits, HACCP plans, procedures, training and records. Quality control is the measurement that detects them — the analyses, the microbiology and the sensory assessment on a defined sampling plan. QA asks whether the process is capable; QC asks whether this particular parcel conforms. Conflating them is what makes bulk programmes fail, because measurement without a system produces numbers nobody acts on.

My standpoint matters as much as the definitions. As the importer's QA/QC manager I own neither the cellar that made the wine nor the plant that will bottle it. My levers are the contractual specification, the audit, the approval sample and the right to reject. Every control below is one I can either execute myself or impose on a third party.

## a) Between completion of winemaking and the start of bulk transport

**QA.** I issue a written product specification for the parcel: analytical ranges, a sensory profile with agreed reference standards, and full legal compliance for the destination market — permitted additives, allergen declaration, residue and contaminant limits, and the import documentation the market requires. I maintain an approved supplier list covering the winery, the bulk logistics operator and the flexitank or ISO tank provider, and I audit each on a schedule, with the winery's HACCP plan and traceability system as audit scope. I require a nominated lot with one-up, one-down traceability. The controlling procedure is the pre-shipment approval sample: nothing loads until my laboratory and my tasting panel have released that sample against the specification in writing.

**QC before loading.** On the approval sample I run alcohol, pH, TA, volatile acidity, residual sugar, malic acid, free and total SO2, dissolved oxygen, dissolved CO2, turbidity, copper and iron, plus microbiology by plating and PCR for *Brettanomyces*, lactic and acetic acid bacteria and viable yeast. Stability is tested, not assumed: a heat test at 80°C for protein, a cold test at minus 4°C for 72 hours or a conductivity mini-contact test for tartrates, and a filterability index. The targets I write into the specification are a molecular SO2 of 0.5-0.8 mg/L, typically 20-40 mg/L free at pH 3.2-3.5 and more above that;

**Treatments I specify.** Bentonite to the heat-test minimum for whites and rosés; tartrate stability by chilling, electrodialysis or an inhibitor as the wine's certification allows; filtration to crossflow or a 0.65 micrometre membrane to drop viable counts; SO2 corrected upward at fill because the voyage will consume it; nitrogen sparging to hit the oxygen target. I also specify the vessel — a flexitank of the order of 24,000 litres is filled effectively brim-full and cannot be inerted or sampled in transit, whereas an ISO tank can be blanketed and sampled — and I require a temperature logger in the container, with loading temperature at or below 15°C.

## b) Following receipt at destination

**QC on discharge.** I draw a representative sample before the wine leaves the container, or immediately as it enters my holding tank, and I compare it directly with the retained loading sample and its certificate of analysis. The comparison is the whole point: free and total SO2, dissolved oxygen, volatile acidity, CO2, turbidity, colour density, microbiology, and a blind sensory assessment against the retained reference. ...dissolved oxygen above roughly 1 mg/L - the level AWRI associates with less-than-ideal handling - as an oxidation risk; The temperature logger download is read alongside.

**Treatments and bulk storage.** SO2 is corrected back to the molecular target before anything else. Reduction is treated by racking or splash-transfer and, if needed, a trace copper addition, remembering that copper is limited to 1 mg/L residual in the EU and under OIV limits - and to 0.5 mg/L in the United States. Elevated dissolved oxygen is stripped by nitrogen sparging. Any haze or microbiological positive triggers re-filtration, and tartrate stability is re-tested because the wine has seen both heat and cold. The holding tank must be pre-cleaned and swab-verified, nitrogen-blanketed, ullage-free and held at 12-15°C, with free SO2 and dissolved oxygen re-checked fortnightly until bottling.

**QA.** The wine sits in quarantine status until I release it, and non-conformances follow a documented decision tree: accept, accept after treatment, or reject and claim on the shipping insurance — which is only possible because paired samples exist. I audit the contract bottler, issue the bottling specification, and verify it afterwards on total package oxygen at bottling below about 1.5 mg/L, fill height, closure and label compliance. Retained samples are kept at each stage, an accelerated shelf-life trial is run, and the supplier scorecard is updated so the next shipment's specification is tighter than the last.

## Conclusion

The importer's authority is not in the cellar; it is in the specification, the audit and the paired sample. QA makes conformity likely, QC proves it, and the two together are what let me tell a bottler, with evidence, whether the wine that arrived is the wine I bought.
