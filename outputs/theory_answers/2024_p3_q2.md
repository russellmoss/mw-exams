## Scope: what actually differs

Both packages need the same headline controls — microbial stability, oxygen management, physical and chemical stability, fill accuracy, coding and traceability. Listing those gets no marks, because the question is comparative. What changes between bag-in-box and glass is the weight each control carries, and that follows from three physical facts: the bag is permeable to oxygen and glass is not; the bag cannot be sterilised in the winery and glass can; and the bag is flexible, so pressure, thermal expansion and abrasion become failure modes a bottle simply absorbs. I organise the answer around those three, and around the shorter, warmer and far less controlled life a box actually leads — supermarket pallet, kitchen worktop, weeks open in a fridge.

## Oxygen: the difference that sets the specification

No bag-in-box is impermeable. Oxygen enters through the film, through the seams and, most of all, through the tap fitment, so the pack has a continuous oxygen demand for its whole life where a screwcapped bottle has a small and near-constant one. Almost the entire fill specification follows from that.

Total package oxygen must be minimised at fill much more aggressively than for glass. Dissolved oxygen is monitored in line with an optical sensor and held below about 0.5 mg/L at the fill, and the bag is evacuated before filling so there is no air pocket to dissolve. Free SO2 is then set against the known ingress rate rather than against habit: for a commercial white I would fill at roughly 35-45 mg/L free rather than the 25-30 mg/L a screwcapped bottle of the same wine would carry, with the addition calculated from measured pH to give molecular SO2 of 0.5-0.8 mg/L. Vague instructions to add "enough SO2" are useless; the number and the pH behind it are the point.

The consequence to state explicitly is shelf life. A BiB has a working life of the order of six to nine months, which is why it carries a best-before date and a bottle usually does not, and why its QC is designed to reach a date rather than to permit ageing. Accelerated shelf-life trials at ambient and elevated temperature with a tasting panel at intervals belong to BiB QC and rarely to glass. Against all this the box wins one contest outright: the carton excludes light completely, so lightstrike, a real risk for wine in flint glass, is designed out.

## Dissolved CO2 and headspace

Glass tolerates dissolved CO2 that a bag does not. High DCO2 inflates the bag, stresses the seams and fitment, causes weeping taps and makes the pack look faulty on shelf. DCO2 therefore becomes a release specification for BiB, adjusted downwards by nitrogen sparging before filling; for a still red I would want it well under 500 mg/L.

The corollary is headspace, and the instinct to fill a bag brimful is wrong. A pack filled at cellar temperature and then stored in a warm warehouse or a car expands, and with no void the seams or the tap fail. The bag is filled to nominal volume with a small deliberate void, and fill weight is checked continuously against the average-quantity tolerance, because a flexible pack gives no fill-height reference of the kind a bottle gives.

## Microbiology: the pack that cannot be sterilised

Glass can be sterilised in the winery — hot water or steam rinse, or supplied clean, inverted, rinsed and optically inspected for foreign bodies. A bag cannot: it arrives pre-sterilised and sealed from the converter, and heat would destroy the film. Sterility control therefore moves upstream and sideways.

- Audit the bag supplier, take certificates of conformity per batch, retain samples, and inspect incoming bags for pinholes, crushed fitments and transit damage.
- Sanitise the filling head, the fitment-opening mechanism and the aseptic filler chamber on a validated schedule, and verify with swabs, ATP or plate counts.
- Make the wine itself the barrier: membrane filtration at 0.45 micron with bubble-point integrity testing before and after every run, low residual sugar, molecular SO2 as above, and sorbic acid up to the EU limit of 200 mg/L where sugar remains.

Secondary fermentation in a box is the classic failure, and the useful thing is to say how it is prevented rather than to describe the swollen pack: a wine with residual sugar, a filter integrity failure or a biofilm in the filler, and inadequate molecular SO2. Post-fill retention samples incubated at 25-30 degrees C for two to four weeks are routine for BiB and unusual for glass.

## Tartrate and protein stability

A crystal deposit in a bottle is cosmetic. In a bag it abrades the film, blocks or props open the tap and causes drips and leaks, so cold stability is a harder release criterion for BiB. Full cold stabilisation, contact process, electrodialysis or potassium polyaspartate all work. Metatartaric acid is the cheap route and its protection degrades within months — which for BiB is the right trade, because oxygen ingress has already capped shelf life below a year, whereas for a bottle that may sit for years it is not. Protein stability matters more too, since boxes are stored and displayed warm, so I would hold whites to a tighter heat-test specification than for glass.

## Conclusion

The same list, differently weighted. Glass QC concentrates on closure application, headspace inerting and foreign-body inspection. Bag-in-box QC concentrates on dissolved oxygen at fill and an SO2 level calculated against the pack's ingress rate, on DCO2 and a deliberate headspace, on supplier-controlled sterility replacing in-line sterilisation, and on a tighter cold-stability specification — everything aimed at reaching a stated best-before date. The wines that matter here are the cask wines of the format's Australian home, invented in Australia in the 1960s, the boxed rose and everyday reds of French and Nordic supermarkets, and monopoly-market listings; a classed-growth claret has no place in the discussion because it will never be filled this way.
