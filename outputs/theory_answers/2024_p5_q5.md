## What counts as a defined wine region

The phrase carries three different meanings and the answer changes depending which is meant. First, the legal sense: a geographical indication whose boundary is drawn in law and whose name may only be used by wine meeting codified rules on varieties, yields, viticulture, processing and often ageing — Barolo DOCG, Champagne AOC, Napa Valley AVA. Second, the commercial-reputational sense: a name that functions as a shared brand in the buyer's mind, which may be far larger or looser than any legal unit — "Marlborough", "the Rhône". Third, the vernacular or physical sense: a coherent growing area with no legal identity at all.

I weigh principally the first, because that is where the concept does enforceable work, while recognising that it earns its value only when the second follows. A legal boundary with no reputation is administratively real and commercially worthless; the AVA system contains many such. My position is that the concept remains extremely important, but its importance is now sharply uneven along the chain: greatest at the two ends — the grower of site-expressive wine, and the institution defending a name — and weakest in the middle, where style, brand and variety increasingly do the work regions used to do.

## Why regions were defined at all

Delimitation was originally a producer's defence against fraud, not a consumer's guide. The Douro was demarcated in 1756 to control quality and adulteration in a booming export trade. Champagne's boundaries were fixed by law in 1927 after riots in the Aube, provoked by merchants buying grapes from outside the area. France's AOC system and the INAO date from 1935, in the aftermath of phylloxera, overproduction and widespread mislabelling. The consumer-facing function came later. Reading the history the other way round — as though regions exist to help shoppers — produces exactly the thin, marketing-led answer that misses what the concept is for.

## To whom it matters

### The grower

Here it matters most, and for reasons that precede marketing. A defined region codifies observed differences in site, converting an unwritten local understanding into a defensible asset. Burgundy's climats and Barolo's menzioni geografiche aggiuntive, approved in 2010, formalise distinctions growers had recognised for generations: Les Amoureuses commands a multiple of village Chambolle-Musigny, and Cannubi a multiple of straight Barolo, because the boundary makes the difference sellable. Chianti Classico added its unità geografiche aggiuntive, appearing on Gran Selezione labels from 2023, for the same reason.

The boundary also capitalises land. The value of a hectare changes abruptly at an appellation line, and that value underwrites borrowing, succession and long-term investment in vineyard quality. It further sets the rules of production — permitted varieties, maximum yields, minimum ageing — which protect a grower from being undercut by a neighbour selling a cheaper thing under the same name. And it protects the name itself, which is the only durable asset most small producers own.

### The collective body

The clearest demonstration is Champagne. The CIVC sets a maximum yield and a maximum pressing rate of 102 litres per 160 kilograms of grapes, and operates a reserve that carries wine between vintages — instruments only available because the region is defined and its actors are bound by that definition. It also polices the name worldwide, litigating against its use on everything from other sparkling wines to unrelated goods. The result is a category that has held premium pricing for over a century while comparable sparkling wines have not. No individual house could have achieved that; the region did.

### The négociant and wholesaler

For the middle of the chain the region is chiefly a specification. A supermarket buyer contracting a hundred thousand cases writes "Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc" because it names a reproducible profile, a supply base and a price band — and, critically, gives a legal basis for rejecting a delivery. That is a real function, but note what it reduces the region to: a quality-assurance shorthand, entirely detachable from terroir.

### Retail and restaurant

Here importance is visibly declining. Discount and mainstream retailers increasingly organise fixtures by style and occasion — crisp whites, smooth reds — rather than by origin, because shoppers navigate that way; the discounters' own-label architecture is built on grape and style with origin in small type. Online recommendation engines work by taste profile. On restaurant lists, region survives more strongly as an organising axis, but sommeliers routinely use it to sell the unfamiliar rather than the famous. A defined region is now one cue competing with variety, brand, style and producer, and it is not always winning.

### The consumer

Region works as a heuristic in proportion to knowledge. In mature markets and among older, engaged drinkers it carries reliable meaning. For newer drinkers, and in emerging markets, brand and variety are more legible than a hierarchy that takes years to learn — and the more finely a region subdivides itself, the less useful it becomes to the person it was supposedly built for. This is the central tension: the granularity that serves the grower disserves the casual buyer.

### The regulator and trade negotiator

Geographical indications are now standard currency in trade agreements — the EU-Japan trade agreement of 2019 protected several hundred geographical indications on each side. The unresolved cases show the stakes: American "semi-generic" labels using European place names, and the long dispute over Prosecco, where the DOC was created in 2009 and the grape renamed Glera precisely so that Prosecco would become a place rather than a variety and could be defended as one.

## Where it matters less

The counter-case is substantial and must be met. Multi-region blending produces some of the world's most admired wines: Australia's most famous red is assembled across South Australian regions, and its label credibility rests on the producer, not the origin. Broad catch-all designations such as South Eastern Australia exist to make blending lawful at scale. Global brands built on variety and consistency have outgrown any region. And appellation rules can ossify: when they prohibit what good producers judge necessary, those producers leave — as Tuscan estates did for decades under the vino da tavola label, and as growers across France now do by declassifying to IGP. A region that cannot adapt loses its best members, which is the sharpest evidence that the concept's importance is contingent, not automatic.

## Verdict

The concept remains very important, but it is no longer importance of one kind. For the grower and the collective institution it is close to indispensable: it codifies terroir, sets enforceable rules, capitalises land and defends a name — functions nothing else performs. For the middle of the chain it has been reduced to a specification. For the mass-market consumer it is one cue among several and a fading one. The regions that will still matter in twenty years are those doing the first job properly — earning the boundary by policing it and by allowing it to evolve — rather than those relying on the name to sell wine that no longer justifies it.
