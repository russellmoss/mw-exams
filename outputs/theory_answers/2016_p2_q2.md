## The three physical variables

Shape, size and material act on wine through four measurable physical properties, and the whole argument follows from them.

*Surface-area-to-volume ratio.* As a vessel gets smaller, each litre of wine touches more wall. That governs three things at once: oxygen ingress through the material, extraction of whatever the wall is made of, and the proportion of lees in contact per litre. A barrique — 225 litres in Bordeaux and 228 in Burgundy — admits of the order of 20 to 30 mg/L of oxygen into a wine over a year; a 5,000-litre foudre of the same wood, far less per litre, and a 500-litre puncheon sits between.

*Thermal behaviour.* Stainless steel conducts, so temperature is externally controlled and can be held anywhere. Wood and concrete insulate, so a fermentation self-buffers and peaks are slower and broader; a small barrel also loses heat fast because of the same surface ratio, which is why barrel-fermented whites run cool without refrigeration.

*Geometry.* In red fermentation the ratio of cap surface to wine depth decides extraction. A wide, shallow, open-top vat gives a large cap surface, low hydrostatic pressure and gentle, even extraction by punching down; a tall narrow tank concentrates the cap, raises pressure and demands pumping over. In whites, a concrete egg or a rounded tank sets up convection that keeps fine lees suspended without stirring.

*Permeability.* Oak transmits oxygen and gives lactones, vanillin and toasty volatile phenols. Concrete and unglazed amphorae give micro-oxygenation with no flavour of their own. Stainless is inert on both counts.

## Cabernet Sauvignon

*Fermentation.* Cabernet is thick-skinned, high in anthocyanin and in tannin that is coarse if over-extracted, so vessel choice is an extraction-control decision. Open-top vats in Bordeaux and Napa allow punch-down and cap-wetting with low mechanical stress; taller stainless tanks with pump-over give a more aggressive, oxygenative regime that suits fruit needing tannin softening. Concrete and wooden fermenters buffer the temperature peak, which matters because Cabernet ferments hot and hot extraction pulls hard tannin.

*Maturation.* Here small oak is doing chemistry, not decoration. The steady low-dose oxygen of a barrique drives anthocyanin-tannin polymerisation, stabilising colour and softening the palate, while oak tannin adds to the structure the variety already has. Cabernet's weight and phenolic load tolerate new oak that would smother a lighter red, which is why the classed growths, Coonawarra, Maipo Alto and Ningxia's premium reds converge on small barrels — not fashion, but a variety with the substance to absorb them.

## Chardonnay

*Fermentation.* Chardonnay is neutral and phenolically light, so the vessel largely writes the style. Stainless steel preserves primary fruit and acidity: Chablis, unoaked Australian and Chilean Chardonnay. Barrel fermentation, by contrast, integrates oak far more seamlessly than post-ferment oak ageing does, and the yeast metabolises some of the harsher wood aldehydes. The larger 500-litre puncheon, now widespread in Margaret River and the Adelaide Hills, gives the textural benefits of wood with roughly half the oak signature per litre — a direct consequence of surface ratio, and a response to a market that moved away from heavy oak.

*Maturation.* Lees contact is the second lever, and small vessels maximise it per litre. Burgundian pièce ageing with bâtonnage builds mannoprotein-derived texture and mouthfeel; concrete eggs and amphorae in Limarí and elsewhere deliver that texture with micro-oxygenation but no oak flavour, which is the point of choosing them.

*Alternative materials.* Concrete and amphora are now used for both varieties, and for the same physical reason in each: slow oxygen ingress with no wood flavour. For Cabernet that means colour stabilisation without oak masking a site; for Chardonnay it means texture without vanillin. Large old oak — foudres and ovals — behaves differently again, giving low oxygen and almost no extractable flavour per litre, which is why it survives for wines wanting slow evolution and neutrality.

## Where the choice is variety-specific

The divergence is real. Chardonnay is routinely fermented in the vessel it matures in; Cabernet almost never is, because red fermentation needs cap access that a barrel cannot give. Chardonnay uses the vessel to add what the grape lacks; Cabernet uses it to moderate what the grape has too much of. And new oak levels that flatter Cabernet will erase Chardonnay.

## The commercial end

Most of both varieties is made far from this. Rotary and autovinifier fermenters extract Cabernet colour and tannin in two to four days rather than ten, which is precisely the right tool for a wine that must be bottled within months; large temperature-controlled stainless tanks with oak staves or chips deliver oak character at a fraction of barrel cost and with far better batch consistency. These are competent engineering answers to a genuine commercial specification, and plenty of excellent wine is made in unfashionable vessels — old large oak in Rioja and Barolo among them. Chardonnay at volume is fermented cool in large stainless tanks and, where oak character is wanted, given staves; the resulting wines are clean, consistent and exactly what the price point requires.

## Conclusion

Shape, size and material matter because they set oxygen exposure, thermal profile, extraction geometry and flavour transfer. They determine style directly; they determine quality only insofar as they are matched to the fruit, which is why the same barrique makes a great Cabernet and an oak-drowned Chardonnay.
