## Which four, and why

I take yeast nutrients, bentonite, carbon and dimethyl dicarbonate. I decline oenological tannins — exogenous tannins from oak, chestnut, quebracho or grape skins and seeds, added for colour stabilisation, mouthfeel or laccase inhibition — because four treated precisely beats five treated thinly, and the question asks for four.

## a. Yeast nutrients

**Source and types.** Inorganic: diammonium phosphate (DAP), less often ammonium sulphate, plus thiamine (vitamin B1). Organic: inactivated dry yeast, yeast autolysates and yeast hulls, supplying amino nitrogen, sterols, unsaturated fatty acids and vitamins, and adsorbing toxic medium-chain fatty acids. Most products are blends.

**Function.** To correct a shortfall in yeast assimilable nitrogen (YAN) — ammonium plus primary amino nitrogen, in mg N/L; proline is not assimilable. YAN drives biomass, fermentation rate and completion, and its absence causes hydrogen sulphide and stuck ferments.

**Application.** Measure YAN on the juice first. Split additions are best: part at inoculation, the balance at roughly one third of sugar depletion, while yeast can still assimilate it. DAP is roughly 21 per cent nitrogen by weight, so 10 g/hL raises YAN by about 21 mg N/L; the OIV ceiling for ammonium salts is 100 g/hL. Organic nutrients suit slow, cool aromatic ferments; DAP suits a fast, warm red.

**Quality impact.** Correctly dosed, it removes reduction and protects aroma. Overdosed, it gives excess biomass and heat, strips varietal thiols in whites, and leaves residual nitrogen for spoilage organisms. Additions after two thirds of fermentation are largely wasted.

**Legal and safety.** Permitted almost everywhere; thiamine is capped at 60 mg/hL in the EU. Some organic schemes restrict inorganic nitrogen, favouring yeast-derived products.

## c. Bentonite

**Source and types.** A montmorillonite clay, mined and food-grade certified. Sodium bentonite swells strongly and adsorbs the most protein per gram but leaves bulky lees; calcium bentonite swells less, adsorbs less, and compacts tightly. Sodium-activated calcium bentonite is the commercial compromise.

**Function.** Two jobs: clarification of juice or wine, and removal of heat-unstable proteins that would otherwise form haze in a warm warehouse. It works by cation exchange — its negatively charged platelets bind proteins, which at wine pH sit below their isoelectric point and carry a positive charge.

**Application.** Rehydrated at roughly ten times its weight in water and left to swell for 12 to 24 hours; added dry it clumps and underperforms. Doses run from about 20 to 100 g/hL, always set by a bench heat-stability trial rather than a rule of thumb. Efficacy is pH-dependent, improving as pH falls. Best used on juice, where lees can be recovered and aroma loss is smaller.

**Quality impact.** Non-selective: at high dose it strips aroma compounds, polysaccharides and some colour, thinning texture, and it causes lees losses of perhaps 3 to 10 per cent of volume.

**Legal and safety.** Permitted worldwide and required to meet purity specifications for arsenic and lead; the dry powder is a respirable dust hazard, and spent lees are a disposal cost.

## d. Carbon

**Source and types.** Activated charcoal from wood, peat or coconut shell, steam- or chemically activated to a very high surface area. Two oenological grades: decolourising and deodorising.

**Function.** Non-selective physical adsorption. Decolourising grades strip browning and pigment; deodorising grades remove off-odours — volatile phenols including smoke taint, geosmin, some mould taints, ochratoxin A and pesticide residues.

**Application.** Usually on juice or on a wine already identified as faulty, dosed after bench trials at the lowest effective rate and capped at 100 g/hL in the EU. Requires contact time with agitation, then filtration to remove it entirely.

**Quality impact.** The rescue is real: a smoke-tainted or oxidised parcel can be made saleable. The damage is equally real, because carbon cannot distinguish fault from varietal character, so overdosing leaves a hollow, aroma-stripped wine that is clean and worthless. It is a fault treatment, never a routine one.

**Legal and safety.** Permitted in the EU and US with declaration rules varying by market and prohibited or restricted in several organic schemes; the dry powder is a fire and dust hazard.

## e. Dimethyl dicarbonate

**Source and types.** A synthesised chemical sterilant, marketed under the trade name Velcorin, supplied as a liquid concentrate.

**Function.** Cold sterilisation at bottling. It inactivates yeasts by methoxycarbonylation of essential enzymes, then hydrolyses within hours to methanol and carbon dioxide, leaving no detectable residue. It protects against refermentation in off-dry wines and against spoilage yeasts including Brettanomyces bruxellensis and Zygosaccharomyces bailii, but is weak against bacteria and has no residual effect, so it kills only what is present at dosing.

**Application.** Dosed inline immediately before the filler, into wine already filtered and cool-stable, because efficacy collapses at high turbidity. The EU, OIV and US limit is 200 mg/L, which contributes of the order of 96 mg/L methanol — well below natural levels. It works reliably only where viable cell counts are already low, broadly under 500 colony-forming units per mL, and is more effective in warmer wine.

**Quality impact.** Sensorially neutral, and it permits lower free SO2 and looser filtration, so it can preserve texture in a sweet wine.

**Legal and safety.** Toxic and corrosive in concentrate, so a dedicated heated dosing unit is mandatory — DMDC solidifies below about 17C — and that unit is a five-figure capital item, which is why it suits medium and large wineries rather than small ones. It is not permitted in EU organic winemaking.
