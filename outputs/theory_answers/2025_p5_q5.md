## Two definitions

**Wine's cultural heritage** is four inheritances, and they behave very differently, so separating them is the whole argument. The *material and legal* inheritance: appellations, classifications, cru hierarchies, and the estates and families that hold them. The *practice* inheritance: transmitted technique — qvevri, solera, foot-treading, bush vines, ancestral-method sparkling. The *ritual* inheritance: wine's place at the table, in religion, in hospitality and celebration. And the *codified-knowledge* inheritance: the tasting vocabulary, the critic and sommelier apparatus, service etiquette, and the exam and certification culture I am writing inside.

**Younger consumers** I take as those between legal drinking age and roughly thirty-five: late Generation Z and younger millennials. That band is a demographic fact, not a market segment, and the difference matters. What actually predicts behaviour is three other axes: the maturity of the wine culture they were raised in, their disposable income relative to housing costs, and their level of category engagement.

My answer to the question, stated now and defended below: wine's cultural heritage remains highly relevant to younger consumers — but its function has inverted. Heritage as *narrative, craft and discovery* is more relevant to this cohort than it was to their parents. Heritage as *authority, hierarchy and status* is close to irrelevant, and in places actively repellent. The industry's problem is not that young people have rejected heritage; it is that the institutions holding the heritage keep offering it in the second mode.

## The segments, and what they actually want

Three engagement segments cut across every market. The *indifferent majority* drink little, choose by occasion and price, and have no relationship with heritage of any kind; for them the question is nearly moot. The *curious social drinker*, the largest addressable group, wants a story that makes a choice feel considered, discovery that can be shared, and no risk of being made to feel ignorant. The *enthusiast minority*, smaller but influential and now global rather than Western, wants exactly the depth heritage offers — but sourced directly, from the grower, not mediated by an institution.

Their stated values are legible: authenticity, provenance, environmental credibility, transparency about who made a thing, and a preference for the small over the corporate. Their contradictions are equally important, and a sweeping account misses them. The same cohort drinks less alcohol but pays more per bottle. It is anti-status in rhetoric and intensely status-conscious in practice, having simply moved the signal from the famous label to the obscure one. It says it wants sustainability and reliably chooses on taste and price. And within one age band a Lagos or Mumbai professional entering the category, a Parisian graduate leaving it, and a Shanghai enthusiast rebuilding it have almost nothing in common.

## The evidence from other cultural sectors

The clearest signal that heritage still works comes from outside wine. Specialty coffee took the most technical, origin-obsessed, hierarchical framework imaginable and sold it to this exact demographic at a large premium, by presenting it as craft and discovery rather than as connoisseurship. Vinyl records returned as a physical, effortful, deliberately obsolete format. Food went further: sourdough, fermentation, masa, and traditional Asian preserving techniques became aspirational for a generation whose grandparents abandoned them. Fashion's growth areas are vintage, repair and archive.

The pattern is consistent. Young consumers embrace heritage presented as *technique with a human author*, and reject heritage presented as *rules with a gatekeeper*. Natural wine is precisely this move performed within wine: old vines, indigenous varieties, pre-industrial methods and minimal intervention are heritage in substance, but they were marketed as counterculture, and they succeeded with the cohort that classified-growth marketing could not reach.

## How this varies around the globe

**China** is the sharpest case. Its status-driven Bordeaux boom collapsed after the anti-extravagance campaign, and younger Chinese consumers have shown little interest in inheriting a wine culture that was about gifting and hierarchy. What is emerging instead is domestic: Ningxia and Yunnan producing a heritage that is being made now, in their own lifetimes, which is a far more attractive proposition than being an audience for someone else's.

**Korea and Japan** show substitution rather than rejection: younger Korean drinkers have moved decisively towards whisky, highballs and craft spirits, categories that modernised their heritage storytelling faster than wine did. Japan's younger drinkers engage with wine through food pairing and through domestic Koshu, which is a heritage claim of their own.

**Latin America** shows heritage being locally owned rather than imported. Brazil's Serra Gaucha was settled by Italian immigrants and its young consumers can claim that lineage as theirs; Uruguay's Tannat belongs to the asado; Mexico's Valle de Guadalupe has built an identity around a food scene rather than a classification.

**India and Nigeria** show the limiting case: wine arrives with no inherited local ritual to attach to, so the ritual inheritance is unavailable and the material inheritance is meaningless. Here relevance depends entirely on the practice and story dimensions, and on whether wine can attach itself to local food and occasion.

**Georgia** is the most encouraging evidence in the question's favour. Georgian qvevri winemaking is on UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list, and amber wine has become genuinely fashionable among young urban drinkers in cities with no Georgian connection at all — heritage travelling successfully because it was received as strange and craft-led rather than as canonical.

**Europe** is where the material inheritance is strongest and youth engagement weakest. The Burgundy climats, the Champagne hillsides and cellars, the Alto Douro and Tokaj all hold UNESCO World Heritage status, and per-capita consumption among young French, Italian and Spanish drinkers has fallen furthest. Heritage as institution has the least traction where it is most concentrated, which is close to a proof of the argument.

**South Africa** adds the ownership dimension: heritage is only relevant to those permitted to claim it, and the emergence of Black-owned brands and young Black winemakers is doing more for wine's relevance to young South Africans than any appellation history could.

## Where heritage has to be reframed

Three reframings are available and mostly unexploited. *Sustainability*: old vines, dry farming, indigenous varieties and low-input traditional practice are simultaneously the oldest heritage and the best environmental story, and almost nobody sells them as one thing. *Inclusivity*: whose heritage is canonical is a live question, and expanding the canon beyond Western Europe is both honest and commercially useful. *Novelty*: obscure heritage varieties and revived methods are, to a new drinker, new — heritage is the industry's largest untapped source of novelty.

The competitive set determines the stakes. Against hard seltzer and RTDs, which have no heritage at all, wine's inheritance is a decisive advantage. Against agave spirits and whisky, which have modernised their heritage storytelling and built it around people and places, wine is losing on execution rather than on assets. Against not drinking, heritage is close to irrelevant.

## Conclusion

To a substantial but conditional extent. Graded against my four inheritances: the practice inheritance and the ritual inheritance remain highly relevant, and are wine's strongest assets with this cohort globally; the material and legal inheritance — classifications, hierarchies, château prestige — is of low and falling relevance outside a small enthusiast minority; and the codified-knowledge inheritance is the actively harmful one, because its function is to sort people into those who know and those who do not. Heritage is not the problem. Heritage delivered as authority is, and the regions with the most of it are the ones losing this cohort fastest.
