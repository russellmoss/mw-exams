## Definitions

Most answers to this question fail because they will not say what a great wine is, and an undefined subject cannot be tested against a requirement. So I will commit.

By great wine I mean a wine that satisfies four intrinsic criteria and one external one. Intrinsically it must have complexity that rewards attention rather than merely pleasing; balance that holds at every stage of its life; length and persistence; and the capacity to develop, so that it is a different and better thing in fifteen years than at release. The external criterion is that greatness must be recognised and sustained over time by informed judges who are not the producer — because "great" is a comparative and a claim on posterity, and no individual can make that claim unilaterally.

This definition deliberately excludes two things. It excludes the merely excellent: a first-rate Muscadet, faultless and delicious, is a very good wine and not a great one, and an essay that lets any well-made wine be great cannot answer the question. And it excludes the purely commercial reading in which a globally successful brand is "great". That is a legitimate alternative definition, and I address it separately at the end, because the answer under it is different — and that difference is the most interesting thing in the question.

By great story I mean a compelling non-sensory narrative attached to the wine: origin myth, founder, heroic vintage, historic vindication, dynasty, terroir romance, or scarcity legend.

The question then divides into two, and conflating them is what produces muddle. Does a wine need a story to *be* great — to possess the qualities above? Or does it need one to *become* great — to be recognised, priced and remembered as such? My answer is no to the first and yes to the second.

## Greatness is a property of the liquid, and stories cannot manufacture it

The strongest evidence that greatness does not require narrative is that it survives the removal of narrative. The 1976 Paris tasting, where Californian wines beat classed-growth Bordeaux and white Burgundy judged blind by a French panel, is decisive here precisely because the stories were not in the room. Stag's Leap and Montelena had no history, no dynasty and no myth; Mouton and Haut-Brion had all three. The wines were assessed as liquid and the story-less ones won. Whatever else that tasting proves, it proves that story is not a necessary input to quality.

The reverse test is equally telling. Wines with magnificent stories and inadequate quality do not become great; they become cautionary tales. Estates with four centuries of history and famous names have declined into mediocrity within living memory in every classic region, and the story did not save them — buyers left, prices fell, and the narrative sat there doing nothing. If story were sufficient, or necessary in the constitutive sense, that could not happen. Greatness is made by site, plant material, farming, vintage and judgement in the cellar, and a story is downstream of all of them.

A third test is temporal, and it follows directly from my definition. Greatness has to be demonstrable in the glass fifteen or thirty years on, and the wines that deliver at maturity are not reliably the ones that carried the best stories at release. Every serious cellar contains celebrated bottles that never developed and unheralded ones that did. A mature bottle is wholly indifferent to what was said about it when it was young.

There is also a plain practical argument. There exist, at any moment, wines of genuinely great quality made by growers nobody has written about — in the Jura, in the Canaries, in Georgia, in the Wachau, in the high Andes. Their greatness is a fact about them before anyone narrates it. Recognition lags; quality does not wait for it.

## But no wine becomes great without one

Now the other half, and I think it is the more important one. Greatness, on my definition, requires sustained external recognition — and recognition is a social process that runs on narrative.

Consider how new entrants actually achieved great status. Sassicaia was first sold commercially at the end of the 1960s and won a blind Decanter tasting of Cabernet-based wines in the 1970s; the wine's quality was real, but what converted that into greatness was a transmissible story — the aristocratic experiment, Bordeaux varieties on Tuscan coastal scrub, the defiance of the DOC system. Penfolds Grange was made semi-clandestinely by Max Schubert in the 1950s and initially rejected by his own management before being vindicated: that narrative of persecution and vindication is now inseparable from the wine, and it is the reason Grange occupies the position it does rather than merely being an excellent Shiraz. In both cases the story did not create the quality. It created the attention that allowed the quality to be assessed at all.

The economics reinforce this. Fine wine is an experience good sold years before it can be assessed, at prices far above its production cost, to buyers who mostly will not drink it. In that market, narrative is the mechanism by which trust is transferred. Burgundy's climat system is a story about place, codified; Champagne's houses sell a story about occasion; classification itself — 1855, the Grands Crus, the appellation hierarchy — is institutionalised narrative. Remove the story and the price collapses long before the quality does, which tells you exactly what work it is doing.

The risk in this, and the reason the question is a contemporary issue rather than a philosophical one, is that story is now cheap to manufacture and quality is not. A great deal of contemporary wine marketing consists of narrative applied to unremarkable liquid — the invented heritage, the borrowed grandfather, the sustainability claim with nothing behind it. If consumers learn that the story is unreliable, they discount all stories, including the true ones, and the producers with genuine narratives are the ones who lose most.

## The alternative definition

If "great" is instead read commercially — the globally successful brand — the answer inverts. Under that definition story is not merely helpful but essential, because a brand is a story with a product attached, and its greatness consists precisely in the strength of that association rather than in complexity, ageing capacity or development. The largest Champagne and Prosecco brands and the great New World labels are great in this sense, and their narratives are their principal asset. I note this to show the definition is doing real work, not to hedge: on my chosen definition the answer is different, and that is why the definition had to come first.

## Conclusion

No, great wine does not need a great story to be great. Greatness is a property of the liquid, produced in the vineyard and the cellar, demonstrable blind and independent of anything written on the label — and the Paris tasting settles that. But yes, great wine needs a great story to be *acknowledged* as great, to command the price that funds the farming that sustains the quality, and to be remembered. My position is therefore that story is not a condition of greatness but a condition of its recognition — and that the industry's real problem today is not that great wines have stories, but that so many ordinary wines have borrowed them.
