## What viability means here

The mechanics are quickly stated and I will not dwell on them: a wine is sold as a future in the spring following the harvest, moves château to courtier to négociant to merchant to consumer, and is delivered two years later. The question is not how that works but whether it still pays.

I test viability on three conditions. The model is commercially viable only if it delivers cash and price realisation to the producer, a margin proportionate to the risk borne by each intermediary, and a reason for the consumer to prepay. Fail any one for long enough and the system unwinds. My argument is that Bordeaux currently satisfies the first, is straining the second and has, for most vintages, stopped satisfying the third.

## The financial case for the château

The financial logic for the producer is strong and unchanged. Selling the crop eighteen months to two years before bottling converts an eighteen-month working-capital drag into positive cash flow, and it transfers the cost of financing and storing the stock onto the chain. That is a real balance-sheet benefit, and for estates carrying replanting or building programmes it is the difference between borrowing and not.

The logistical case is equally real. A first growth making perhaps 15,000 to 20,000 cases has no wish to build a global credit-control, allocation and shipping operation. The Place does that for a fee, and reaches perhaps a hundred markets.

The cost is option value. In a great vintage the château sells at a price the market promptly exceeds, and it never recovers that margin. Château Latour left the system after the 2011 vintage precisely to capture it, releasing only when the wine is ready — which requires the balance sheet to hold a decade of stock, and is why almost nobody has followed.

## The financial case down the chain

The margins are thin and the risk is not. The courtier takes around 2% for matching allocations; the négociant typically around 10%; the importing merchant a further 10 to 15%. For that the négociant finances the purchase, carries the price risk for two years, and accepts allocations conditional on also taking the weak vintages — which is the real cost of the system to them.

## Release pricing, and what happens afterwards

The pricing record is the evidence. The 2019 campaign, run in the first summer of the pandemic, worked because the châteaux moved: the 2019s were released some 20 to 30% below the 2018 opening prices and sold through quickly. The 2020 campaign this year showed the reflex return, as the 2020s came out roughly 10 to 25% above the 2019 releases into a small crop.

Set against that is the 2010 and 2011 vintages, much of which still trades at or below its release price a decade on. Liv-ex's recurring finding — that physical back vintages are frequently available below the price of the new release — is the single most damaging fact for the consumer proposition, and it is now visible to every buyer. The market has voted with its feet, Bordeaux's share of secondary-market trade by value falling from almost all of it in 2010 to somewhere around 35 to 40% now.

## Other regions

Burgundy runs a functional en primeur through merchants, and it is more viable than Bordeaux's because the scarcity is absolute: after the 2016 and 2021 frosts, the allocation, not the discount, is the product. The Rhône offers en primeur with far weaker justification, since most wines are still obtainable on physical release. Vintage Port's declaration in the second year is a related device, again driven by cash flow rather than by consumer benefit.

The most interesting comparator is what the Place de Bordeaux now does with Masseto, Opus One, Almaviva and Penfolds: distribute them globally as physical wine on annual release. That tells us the market values Bordeaux's distribution machine considerably more than it values the futures mechanism attached to it.

## Pros and cons, party by party

- **Château**: early cash, risk transfer, an annual marketing event; against that, foregone upside and reputational damage when prices fall.
- **Courtier**: low-risk commission; but a role increasingly hard to justify in a transparent market.
- **Négociant**: volume and allocation access; against that, thin margin, two years of price risk and forced purchase of poor vintages.
- **Merchant/importer**: customer acquisition and cash received long before payment is due; against that, counterparty risk on both sides, and a reputational exposure demonstrated by the 2016 collapse of Premier Cru, which owed customers of the order of £70 million.
- **Consumer**: guaranteed provenance, access to scarce wines and format choice; against that, capital tied up for two years, no ability to taste the finished wine, and, on the evidence above, no reliable discount.

## Conclusion

En primeur remains commercially viable for the producer and, in short vintages, for the consumer. It is not viable as a general system: the layers each take a margin the consumer can now see, for a service the internet has largely replicated. I expect it to survive at the top fifty estates as a cash-flow and marketing ritual, with the Place surviving far more robustly as a distribution channel than as a futures market.
