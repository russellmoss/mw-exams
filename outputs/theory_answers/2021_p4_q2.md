## What viability means here

The mechanics are quickly stated and I will not dwell on them: a wine is sold as a future in the spring following the harvest, moves château to courtier to négociant to merchant to consumer, and is delivered two years later. The question is not how that works but whether it still pays.

I test viability on three conditions. The model is commercially viable only if it delivers cash and price realisation to the producer, a margin proportionate to the risk borne by each intermediary, and a reason for the consumer to prepay. Fail any one for long enough and the system unwinds. My argument is that Bordeaux currently satisfies the first, is straining the second and has, for most vintages, stopped satisfying the third.

## The financial case for the château

The financial logic for the producer is strong and unchanged. Selling the crop eighteen months to two years before bottling converts an eighteen-month working-capital drag into positive cash flow, and it transfers the cost of financing and storing the stock onto the chain. That is a real balance-sheet benefit, and for estates carrying replanting or building programmes it is the difference between borrowing and not.

The logistical case is equally real. A first growth making perhaps 15,000 to 20,000 cases has no wish to build a global credit-control, allocation and shipping operation. The Place does that for a fee, and reaches perhaps a hundred markets.

The cost is option value. In a great vintage the château sells at a price the market promptly exceeds, and it never recovers that margin. Château Latour left the system after the 2011 vintage precisely to capture it, releasing only when the wine is ready — which requires the balance sheet to hold a decade of stock, and is why almost nobody has followed.

## The financial case down the chain

The margins are thin and the risk is not. The courtier takes a small commission, commonly cited at around 2%, for matching allocations, and bears almost no risk in doing so. The négociant's position is the opposite: it finances the purchase, carries the price risk for two years, and accepts allocations conditional on also taking the weak vintages — which is the real cost of the system to them, and the reason the Place tolerates release prices it privately considers too high. The merchant then holds customer money against undelivered wine, so its margin is compensation for counterparty and storage risk as much as for selling.

For the consumer the bargain has narrowed to two things: access to wines that will be allocated out later, and the chance of buying below the eventual market price. The first is real at the very top and largely illusory below it. The second is an investment proposition dressed as a purchase, and it depends entirely on release pricing being disciplined.

## Release pricing, and what happens afterwards

The pricing record is the evidence. The 2019 campaign, run in the first summer of the pandemic, worked because the chateaux moved: the 2019s were released meaningfully below the 2018 opening prices and sold through quickly. The 2020 campaign showed the reflex return, as the 2020s came out meaningfully above the 2019 releases into a small crop.

Set against that is the 2010 and 2011 vintages, much of which still trades at or below its release price a decade on. Liv-ex's recurring finding — that physical back vintages are frequently available below the price of the new release — is the single most damaging fact for the consumer proposition, and it is now visible to every buyer. The market has voted with its feet, Bordeaux's share of secondary-market trade by value having fallen sharply from a large majority in 2010 to a minority now. The Rhône offers en primeur with far weaker justification, since most wines are still obtainable on physical release. Vintage Port's declaration in the second year is a related device, again driven by cash flow rather than by consumer benefit.

The most interesting comparator is what the Place de Bordeaux now does with Masseto, Opus One, Almaviva and Penfolds: distribute them globally as physical wine on annual release. Merchant/importer counterparty risk is real, as demonstrated by the 2016 collapse of Premier Cru, which owed customers a substantial sum reported in the tens of millions of pounds. It is not viable as a general system: the layers each take a margin the consumer can now see, for a service the internet has largely replicated. I expect it to survive at the top fifty estates as a cash-flow and marketing ritual, with the Place surviving far more robustly as a distribution channel than as a futures market.
