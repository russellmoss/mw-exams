## Scope and definition

Blending is the deliberate combination of two or more distinct wine or must components into a single finished wine. I take it broadly, as the examiners' phrasing invites: it covers combining varieties, parcels, vintages, press fractions, vessel and oak treatments, and — in fortified wine — separately prepared sweetening and spirit components. It is both corrective, repairing what an individual lot lacks, and creative, building a wine no single lot could produce. It is also the most powerful quality lever in the cellar, because it is the only one that adds nothing and costs almost nothing.

## Why a winemaker blends

**Balance.** A component may be excellent but incomplete — high acid and low body, or ripe fruit and thin structure. Blending fills the gap: a firm, acid-driven Sémillon lot given weight by a riper barrel-fermented one is a balance problem solved without additions.

**Complexity.** Layering different aromatic and textural registers produces a wine with more dimensions than any of its parts — the logic of the Bordeaux blend and of the Rhône's Grenache, Syrah and Mourvèdre combination.

**Adjusting specific sensory attributes.** Blending is a precision tool for colour density, tannin, alcohol and residual sugar. Adding press wine lifts tannin and colour in a light free-run red; blending a small volume of low-alcohol lot pulls a declared alcohol under a duty threshold.

**Consistency.** A brand must taste the same every year, and blending across parcels, regions and vintages is how vintage variation is absorbed.

**Risk and volume management.** Blending dilutes a marginal lot — slightly elevated volatile acidity, a hint of reduction — into a volume where it disappears, and fills a customer's required volume.

## The approaches, and what each is for

**Varietal combination** is chosen where varieties ripen differently and hedge each other: Cabernet Sauvignon on Médoc gravel with Merlot on cooler clay is a viticultural insurance policy expressed as a blend, which is why the proportions move so sharply between vintages. Some blending is done pre-fermentation: up to 20% Viognier co-fermented with Syrah is a co-fermentation, not an assemblage, and stabilises colour as well as adding aromatic lift.

**Vineyard and parcel selection** is blending by subtraction as much as addition — declassifying weaker parcels into a second label protects the grand vin and creates a second revenue stream.

**Barrel and oak blending** is routinely underrated. Choosing the proportion of new to seasoned oak, French to American or Hungarian, and light to heavy toast is a blending decision made at assemblage, not a purchasing decision made once. American oak brings coconut and dill lactones, French a finer spice; blending them lets a winemaker dial oak intensity without over-oaking any part.

**Vintage blending** is the basis of non-vintage sparkling wine and of every fortified solera.

## The regulatory frame

Law sets the boundaries of every blend. Varietal labelling requires at least 85% of a named variety in the European Union, and 75% federally in the United States; vintage-dating requires 85% in the European Union and 95% for a wine carrying an American Viticultural Area. Appellation rules dictate permitted varieties and often minimum or maximum proportions, so a blend can be technically better and legally impossible.

Two systems make blending mandatory rather than optional. Non-vintage Champagne is defined by assemblage across villages, varieties and vintages, with reserve wine often making up between a tenth and a third of the assemblage, held precisely so house style survives a poor harvest. The criaderas y solera system in Jerez blends fractionally and continuously, drawing from the oldest scale and refreshing it from younger ones, so that no Sherry is a vintage wine and the average age of the solera rises over time. In both cases the regulation exists because the blend *is* the product.

## Blending at commercial scale

At high volume the objectives shift from complexity to consistency, cost and compliance. A multi-million-bottle brand is assembled from dozens of parcels and often from bulk wine bought on the international market, blended to a written specification — alcohol, residual sugar, titratable acidity, pH, colour density, sensory profile — that must be hit within tolerance every month. Broad appellations exist to permit exactly this: a wine labelled South Eastern Australia may draw from three states, allowing a blender to average out a poor season in one region against a good one in another. In-line blending meters components as the tank fills rather than blending in batch, and large tanks are pumped over to homogenise before analysis. Cost engineering is explicit: a small proportion of an expensive, concentrated lot lifts a large volume of an inexpensive one to the required standard.

## How the decision is actually made

The technique matters as much as the intent. Components are analysed first — alcohol, titratable acidity, pH, volatile acidity, residual sugar, free sulphur dioxide, colour density and phenolics — so the blender knows the arithmetic limits before tasting. Components are tasted blind and ranked. Bench trials follow: measured proportions made up in cylinders, tasted blind against each other and against a benchmark, iterating around the two or three best ratios, then retasted after several days, because a blend tastes different once married.

Two practical checks are commonly forgotten. Legal compliance must be recalculated on the final blend, not on the components. And stability must be reassessed on the blend: components that were individually protein- and tartrate-stable can be unstable when combined, so heat and cold stability tests are repeated post-blend and fining adjusted accordingly.

## Conclusion

A winemaker blends whenever the wine required does not exist in any single vessel — which is almost always. At the premium end the circumstance is balance and complexity; at commercial scale it is consistency, cost and compliance; in Champagne and Jerez it is the regulatory definition of the wine itself. The skill lies not in knowing that blending helps but in the disciplined loop of analysis, blind trial and benchmarking that turns a set of lots into a wine better than its best.
