## What makes temperature the "most impactful" variable

Impact here means two things: how much of the outcome a variable controls, and how little the grower can do about it. Temperature scores highest on both. It sets where vines can be grown at all, which varieties will ripen there, when every operation in the year happens, and how much disease pressure and how much crop the grower ends up with. Unlike nutrition, water on an irrigated site, or canopy density, it cannot be dialled in — it can only be selected for at planting and modified at the margins afterwards. Water availability is its equal in arid zones, and sunlight is bundled with it, so I would not claim primacy without qualification. But since the responses to temperature are almost all structural, it is the variable a viticulturist must get right first.

## The thresholds that matter

Vine shoot growth effectively stops below about 10C, which is why growing degree days are summed above that base and why site selection in marginal regions is really a search for accumulated heat. Green shoots are killed at roughly -1 to -2C, so a spring radiation frost after budburst removes the primary crop. Dormant vinifera buds survive to about -15C, with bud death common below -20C and trunk and root death below about -25C — the constraint that defines Ontario, the Finger Lakes, Ningxia and much of central Europe. Photosynthesis is most efficient around 25-30C and declines above that as stomata close; anthocyanin synthesis is inhibited above roughly 30C while malic acid respiration accelerates, which is why hot vintages give pale, low-acid, high-pH reds. Sunburn appears once berry surface temperature passes about 45C, well below air temperature on an exposed western face. Fruit set needs settled weather of roughly 20C: cold, wet flowering gives coulure and millerandage, an outcome Merlot in Bordeaux and Grenache across the south of France show most years.

## Cold-limited zones: protecting the crop and the plant

Where winter kill is the binding constraint the responses are structural and blunt. Growers hill soil over the graft union each autumn, or, in Ningxia and parts of Ontario, bury whole canes. They plant on slopes above the frost-draining valley floor and beside large water bodies — the Finger Lakes and Lake Erie exist as wine regions for this reason alone. They choose hardy varieties and, at the extreme, hybrids. Against spring frost the annual round is: delayed pruning to postpone budburst by a week or more, high trunks to lift buds out of the coldest layer, and then wind machines, sprinklers or bougies on the night — Chablis running water because it can also freeze the shoot in an insulating ice shell. Cover crops are mown or removed before frost season because bare, moist, compacted soil stores and re-radiates far more heat than grass.

## Marginal zones: chasing every degree

In the Mosel, Rheingau, Champagne and southern England the season's work is heat capture. Steep south-facing slopes and low training on single stakes put fruit close to warm slate; stony soils in the Médoc and Châteauneuf re-radiate at night. Leaf removal on the fruit zone after set raises berry temperature and degrades methoxypyrazines — essential for Cabernet Franc in the Loire, actively harmful in a warm year. Crop thinning brings a Pinot Noir crop to ripeness in a cool vintage. Row orientation, vine spacing and shoot number are all set against the heat summation the site can supply.

## Hot zones: shading, cooling and timing

Above the optimum the levers reverse. Growers retain the afternoon-side leaf wall and confine leaf removal to the morning face; they use sprawl or bush training so the canopy shades its own fruit, which is why goblet persists across La Mancha, Sicily and the southern Rhône. Kaolin films and shade cloth reduce berry temperature; overhead sprinklers give evaporative cooling during a spike where water permits. Site selection moves to altitude — Salta, the Uco Valley, the Sierra de Gredos — and variety to late-ripening, heat-tolerant Grenache, Mourvèdre, Assyrtiko and Touriga Nacional. Harvest is taken at night to deliver fruit cool, and picking dates are pulled forward.

## Soil temperature, humidity and a warming baseline

Soil temperature is the neglected half of the question. Dark, stony, well-drained soils warm early and advance budburst — an advantage in Germany, a frost liability in a warming spring. Wet clay stays cold and delays the whole season; mulch cools the root zone and buffers the surface. Temperature also acts through humidity: warm, humid conditions drive downy mildew and botrytis, while mild winters allow Pierce's disease and its vectors to overwinter, so a temperature change is also a spray-programme change.

Warming should be treated as a shift in these same thresholds rather than a separate topic. It advances budburst into the frost window, compresses harvest, decouples sugar from phenolic ripeness, and pushes varieties past their niche. The responses are the ones already listed — later-ripening varieties and clones, higher trunks, drought-tolerant rootstocks, altitude — which is precisely why temperature, and not weather in general, is the variable a grower plants against.
