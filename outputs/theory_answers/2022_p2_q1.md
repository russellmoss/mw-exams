## What the process actually is

Malolactic fermentation (MLF) is not a fermentation in the yeast sense: no sugar is consumed and no ethanol produced. It is a bacterial conversion in which lactic acid bacteria (LAB) decarboxylate L-malic acid, a dicarboxylic acid, into L-lactic acid, a monocarboxylic acid, releasing carbon dioxide. The organism that matters commercially is *Oenococcus oeni*, effectively the only wine LAB that reliably tolerates the combined stress of wine pH, ethanol and sulphur dioxide. *Lactobacillus* — *L. plantarum* is now sold as a co-inoculation strain — and *Pediococcus* also convert malate, but both are less acid- and alcohol-tolerant and carry greater spoilage risk (biogenic amines, ropiness, elevated volatile acidity), so their dominance is usually a fault rather than a plan.

I take the question in its own three parts: the mechanism and the conditions that permit it, the purposes it serves, and the timing decisions available.

## How: mechanism, and the metrics that govern it

The malolactic enzyme decarboxylates malate directly to lactate, with NAD+ and manganese as cofactors. The energy yield is small — for *O. oeni* this is a survival strategy in a nutrient-poor medium, which is why MLF is slow and why it stalls so readily. Because a diprotic acid becomes a monoprotic one, titratable acidity falls, commonly by 1-2 g/L where malic acid is high, and pH rises, typically by 0.1-0.3; lactic acid is also perceived as softer and rounder than malic. Separately, LAB metabolise citric acid to diacetyl, the buttery compound, which yeast and continued lees contact then reduce to near-neutral acetoin and 2,3-butanediol — which is why a barrel-fermented Chardonnay left long on its lees after MLF shows less butter than one racked immediately.

Four metrics decide whether MLF will run.

**pH** is the most limiting. Below roughly 3.2, MLF is slow and unreliable, although *O. oeni* is favoured over spoilage LAB in that range; above roughly 3.5 it proceeds readily but *Pediococcus* and *Lactobacillus* become genuine risks.

**Alcohol** is the limit most often forgotten, and it is why MLF cannot be assumed to happen by itself. Bacterial viability falls as ethanol rises, and commercial *O. oeni* strains are rated to roughly 15-16% abv. A 15.5% Barossa Shiraz is therefore a hostile medium in a way a Mosel base wine is not, and needs an alcohol-tolerant strain or an earlier inoculation.

**Temperature**: the optimum is roughly 18-22C. Below about 15C MLF stalls, which is why an unheated Burgundian cellar may not see it complete until the following spring; above about 25C, off-flavours and competing bacteria increase.

**SO2** inhibits in its molecular form, so the same free SO2 is far more inhibitory at low pH. In practice, keeping molecular SO2 above roughly 0.5 mg/L will hold MLF off, and a modest addition at pressing is enough to block it in a cool-climate white.

Nutrition matters alongside these: LAB are fastidious, and a wine stripped of nitrogen by a hard ferment may need a bacterial nutrient. Residual sugar above a few g/L is a real risk, because heterofermentative LAB will metabolise it to acetic acid rather than simply completing the malate conversion.

## Why it is employed

**Microbial stability**, first. Malic acid is a substrate for any LAB that survives to the bottle. Completing MLF before bottling removes that substrate; leaving malic acid in an unsterile wine invites in-bottle refermentation, with haze, spritz and volatile acidity.

**Deacidification.** In cool climates malic acid can dominate: MLF is the standard route to a drinkable balance in red Burgundy, Bordeaux and Rioja, and in Chablis, without the coarseness of chemical deacidification.

**Sensory modification.** Softer acidity, greater apparent texture from bacterial polysaccharides, diacetyl where wanted, reduced primary fruit and better integration with oak.

It is equally a decision *not* to run it. Mosel Riesling, Sancerre Sauvignon Blanc and Vinho Verde depend on retained malic acid for tension and primary aroma; MLF is blocked there by SO2 at pressing, cool storage, early racking off the lees, lysozyme, and sterile filtration at bottling.

## When it is employed

Almost all red wine goes through MLF, and effectively must, since high-pH reds cannot be reliably protected from it later. The real decisions are timing and vessel.

*Co-inoculation*, with bacteria added within a day or two of the yeast, shortens the total elapsed time, exploits the lower alcohol early in fermentation, and avoids the vulnerable post-ferment window when the wine is dry, unsulphured and unprotected. It suits high-alcohol warm-climate reds. It gives less diacetyl, because the yeast are still present to reduce it.

*Sequential* inoculation after dryness is the traditional route, and gives more control over diacetyl and more predictable aroma outcomes.

*Vessel*: MLF in barrel on gross lees, the classic white Burgundy and premium Chardonnay practice, integrates oak and gives a rounder texture, but is slow and hard to monitor; tank MLF is faster and more uniform.

*Sparkling base wines* face a genuine style choice before tirage: run MLF to soften a very high-acid base, or block it to preserve the acid backbone for long lees ageing. Fortified wines such as Port never complete MLF, because fortification arrests bacteria along with yeast.

## Conclusion

MLF is a controllable microbiological process, not an inevitability. The winemaker decides whether it happens at all, and pH, alcohol, temperature and SO2 are the levers by which that decision is executed.
