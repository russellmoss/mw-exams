## What Brettanomyces is

*Brettanomyces* is a genus of spoilage yeast — particularly *Brettanomyces bruxellensis*, whose sexual form is named *Dekkera bruxellensis*. It is a facultative anaerobe capable of surviving on trace carbon sources in finished wine, tolerant of alcohol and of sulphur dioxide, and slow-growing, which is why it typically appears during extended ageing rather than during fermentation.

Its winemaking significance is the volatile phenols it produces. It possesses cinnamate decarboxylase, which converts the grape-derived hydroxycinnamic acids p-coumaric and ferulic acid into 4-vinylphenol and 4-vinylguaiacol, and then vinylphenol reductase, which reduces these to 4-ethylphenol (4-EP) and 4-ethylguaiacol (4-EG). 4-EP smells of barnyard, horse sweat and sticking plaster; 4-EG is smokier, more clove-like and medicinal. Wine yeast lack the second enzyme, which is why the ethyl phenols are effectively a *Brettanomyces* signature. It also produces isovaleric acid, giving rancid, cheesy notes, and can contribute to mousiness.

Although the problem is associated with red wine — reds carry more hydroxycinnamic acid precursors, higher pH and more barrel time — it is not confined to it. Barrel-fermented, high-pH or oxidatively handled whites are perfectly capable of harbouring it, and it survives in shared cellar equipment regardless of wine colour.

## Complexity or fault

At low concentration the ethyl phenols read as savoury spice, leather and tobacco, and some tasters value that complexity in mature reds; historically it was tolerated, even prized, in parts of Bordeaux and the Rhône. 4-ethylphenol is perceptible in red wine at concentrations of the order of 400 micrograms per litre, and 4-ethylguaiacol at rather less, with sensitivity varying widely between tasters and masking varying with tannin and fruit weight. Once the phenols dominate, they flatten fruit, blur varietal definition and homogenise a portfolio, and the wine is faulty.

My position is that it should be treated as a fault to be controlled, not a stylistic tool, because a *Brettanomyces* population is a living, progressive contamination: a wine that is attractively complex at bottling will be less so in three years, and the winery cannot hold it at a chosen level.

## The four conditions it needs

**Residual sugar.** It does not need much. *Brettanomyces* metabolises glucose and fructose but also trehalose and cellobiose, the latter released from toasted oak, so a wine analysing dry at below 0.5 g/L still has enough substrate.

**pH.** The single most important lever. Above pH 3.6 growth is markedly easier, largely because sulphur dioxide loses its efficacy: only the molecular form is antimicrobial, and the fraction that is molecular collapses as pH rises.

**Temperature.** It grows well at cellar temperatures and accelerates in a warm cellar; a red finishing fermentation warm, then sitting at ambient in an unconditioned barrel hall, is the classic exposure.

**Oxygen.** Underrated and decisive. Although it can survive anaerobically, small repeated doses of oxygen — racking, topping, barrel transmission, sloppy ullage management — markedly stimulate its growth. The post-malolactic, low-sulphur, warm, oxygen-touched barrel period is the danger window.

## Proactive control

**Sanitation.** Most infections are cellar infections, not vineyard infections. That means hot water or caustic cleaning of tanks, pumps, hoses, valves, bottling lines and the crusher-destemmer; attention to gaskets, elbows and sample valves where biofilm persists; and hygiene of the fruit stream, since sour rot and long warm transport raise the incoming load.

**Composition.** Ferment to true dryness and confirm it enzymatically rather than by hydrometer. Complete malolactic conversion promptly, then sulphur immediately — the unprotected window between malolactic conversion and the first sulphur addition is where most damage is done. Manage pH: acidify the must to hold pH below 3.6, which both restrains the yeast and doubles the value of every subsequent sulphur addition. Maintain roughly 0.5 to 0.8 mg/L molecular sulphur dioxide, which requires around 25 mg/L free sulphur dioxide at pH 3.5 but closer to 50 mg/L at pH 3.8. Store cool and keep barrels topped.

**Barrel management.** Barrels are the principal reservoir, because the yeast lives several millimetres into the stave where surface cleaning cannot reach. Options run from hot water above 80 degrees Celsius and steam through sulphur wicks between fills, to ozonated water, which sanitises cold without chemical residue, and high-power ultrasound. Older barrels carrying a history should be retired or downgraded rather than cleaned repeatedly; buying new oak for high-risk lots is cheaper than losing them.

## Detection

Catching it early is what separates a manageable problem from a lost vintage. Routine sensory screening of barrels by an experienced palate remains the fastest warning. Laboratory tools are more reliable: plating on selective media containing cycloheximide, which inhibits *Saccharomyces*; quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) for rapid cell counts including cells that will not culture; and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry to quantify 4-EP and 4-EG directly. A cellar with a known problem should be screening barrel by barrel, since one infected barrel contaminates the blend.

## Reactive treatment

Once established, options narrow. Filtration is the reliable one and is routinely overlooked: crossflow reduces the cell load, and a 0.45 micron membrane at the filler removes viable cells before bottling. Chitosan of fungal origin is permitted for microbial reduction and is useful where sulphur is constrained, though it is more suppressive than eradicative. Dimethyl dicarbonate, permitted in the European Union up to 200 mg/L, sterilises at the filler but leaves no residual protection. Flash pasteurisation is available at volume. Where the phenols are already formed, reverse osmosis coupled with adsorptive resin can strip them, but it is expensive, strips other aroma, and treats the symptom.

## Conclusion

*Brettanomyces* is controlled by denying it its conditions rather than by killing it once present: dry wine, pH below 3.6, molecular sulphur dioxide held at target, cool storage, disciplined oxygen and topping, clean barrels retired when suspect, and early lab screening. Sulphur dioxide, filtration and chemical treatment are the last line, not the strategy.
