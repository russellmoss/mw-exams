## Scope

Preparing a finished wine for bottling is the process of making a wine that is already stylistically complete into one that is safe to seal: stable in the bottle for its intended shelf life, analytically within specification, and legally saleable in its destination markets. The bottling line, its hygiene and the dry goods are a separate discipline, and I set them aside deliberately; my subject is the thinking about the wine. Three questions govern it — is it stable, what do the numbers say, and is it legal — and behind all three sits a fourth, which is time, because several of these operations have lead times of days or weeks.

## Stability

*Microbiological.* The wine must not referment or be spoiled in bottle. That means confirming it is dry and that malolactic fermentation is either complete or securely blocked — below roughly 0.3 g/L of residual sugar and 0.1 g/L of malic acid — and then deciding the filtration specification. A 0.45 micron membrane retains yeast and most spoilage bacteria; a cleaner wine destined for early drinking may go through at 0.65 micron to avoid stripping. Any wine bottled with fermentable sugar must be sterile-filtered and filled aseptically. The plating result and any *Brettanomyces* count drive that decision.

*Protein.* Whites and rosés must pass a heat test, otherwise a haze appears on a warm shelf. Bentonite trials establish the dose, and the addition must be made and settled before the final filtration, not after.

*Tartrate.* Conductivity or a cold test establishes the risk. The decision is between cold stabilisation, which occupies a chilled tank for days, and an in-line addition of carboxymethylcellulose, metatartaric acid or potassium polyaspartate. CMC is not compatible with a protein-unstable white, so the two stability decisions interact and must be taken in the right order.

*Oxidative and metal.* Dissolved oxygen at filling, and total package oxygen, determine how the wine ages; iron and copper are checked for casse risk and as pro-oxidants. Colour stability, and pinking potential in a white, are assessed at the same point.

## Chemical analysis

Analysis is a decision tool, not a record. Free and total SO2, read against pH, set the addition made immediately before filling: the target is molecular SO2 of about 0.5 mg/L in a dry red and 0.8 mg/L where sugar is present, so the required free SO2 roughly doubles for every 0.3 rise in pH. Alcohol, residual sugar, titratable acidity, pH, volatile acidity, malic acid, turbidity and dissolved oxygen complete the standard panel. Each figure is compared with three references: the legal limit, the customer's specification, and the producer's own house style. A supermarket buyer's analytical specification is frequently tighter than the law, and a wine failing it is rejected on delivery.

## Legal conformity

SO2 is where an MW candidate is expected to be exact. The EU permits 150 mg/L total SO2 for a dry red and 200 mg/L for a dry white or rosé, rising by 50 mg/L once residual sugar reaches 5 g/L and to 400 mg/L for the sweetest categories; the United States sets a single total ceiling of 350 mg/L; organic wine in the EU is held some 30 to 50 mg/L below the conventional figures. Volatile acidity has its own ceiling, 1.2 g/L for reds in the EU, and alcohol must be within a labelling tolerance of 0.5 per cent abv.

Beyond that, the winemaker must confirm that every additive used is permitted in the destination market — the permitted lists differ between the EU, the US and Australia, and an additive legal at home can make a shipment unsaleable abroad. Allergenic fining agents from egg, milk or fish must be declared unless residues are below the threshold, and contaminant screens for ochratoxin A and, for some markets, heavy metals and pesticide residues, are part of the release decision.

## Additives beyond sulphur dioxide

SO2 is the one candidates always know; the others are where the marks are. Ascorbic acid is used with SO2 on whites to scavenge oxygen at filling, and is actively harmful without adequate SO2 because its own oxidation product is hydrogen peroxide. Dimethyl dicarbonate at up to 200 mg/L and sorbic acid at up to 200 mg/L are the two microbial insurance options for an off-dry wine — DMDC hydrolyses away and leaves no residue, sorbate persists and risks geranium taint if lactic acid bacteria are present. Gum arabic protects colour and mouthfeel; copper is capped at 1 mg/L and metatartaric acid at 100 mg/L; nitrogen and CO2 are adjusted, since dissolved CO2 of a few hundred milligrams per litre lifts a crisp white and coarsens a soft red.

## Conclusion

The main considerations are therefore stability, analysis and legality, taken together and in the right sequence. The discipline that distinguishes a good winemaker here is planning backwards from the bottling date, because bentonite, cold stabilisation and a rejected analysis all cost time the calendar may not have.
