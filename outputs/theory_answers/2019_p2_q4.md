## What "high-end" means, and what the barrel is for

**High-end wine** I define by four attributes: concentration, complexity, balance and longevity, plus the market position that follows from them. None of the four is exclusive to red wine — Grand Cru white Burgundy, Hunter Semillon, vintage Champagne, Tokaji Aszú and vintage Madeira all qualify — and the answer collapses if it assumes otherwise.

The right way into the question is to ask what a new French barrique actually contributes, then find other routes to the same ends. It supplies four things: **slow, controlled oxygen ingress** through wood and bung; **extractable compounds** — ellagitannin, vanillin, whisky lactone, eugenol, furfural from toasting; a **high surface-to-volume ratio** with lees in a small vessel; and **concentration through evaporation**. Only the second is uniquely oak's. The others can be delivered otherwise, and that is where the alternatives lie.

## Wood, used more intelligently

The obvious moves first, briefly, because they are only part of the answer. Reduce the proportion of new wood and extend the barrel's life over several fills; use French oak of less fashionable provenance, or American, Hungarian, Austrian, Slavonian or Caucasian oak, chosen for grain and tannin rather than reputation; buy in larger formats, where the wood cost per litre falls sharply and the aromatic imprint falls with it — the *botti* of Piedmont and Tuscany, the *foudres* of Alsace and the Rhône, German *Fuder*, and the large-format vessels now common in Chile and South Africa. Staves, inserts, chips and powder deliver the extractives at a fraction of the price — **the EU legalised oak chips and staves in 2006** — though used alone in an inert tank they give flavour without oxygen or texture, which is exactly why they are associated with commercial rather than high-end wine. Combined with a metered oxygen supply, they come much closer.

## Other vessels, and their oxygen transfer rates

The more interesting answer is to change vessel type, and the variable to think with is oxygen transfer rate.

**Concrete**, in tank or egg form, is porous when unlined and effectively inert when epoxy-lined, so the winemaker can choose. The egg's shape sustains a convection current that keeps fine lees in suspension without stirring, giving texture without oak flavour; it is now used for high-end whites and reds from the Rhône, the Loire, Chile, Argentina and South Africa.

**Amphorae and buried clay** — Georgian *qvevri*, Spanish *tinajas* in Valdepeñas and Montilla, Italian terracotta, Portuguese *talhas* in the Alentejo — transfer more oxygen when unlined, less when lined with beeswax or resin. They deliver slow micro-oxidation and, for skin-contact whites, an entirely different textural route to complexity.

**Wooden vats and untoasted large wood** give oxygen without aromatic imprint. **Stainless steel, glass demijohn and sandstone** give none, and are chosen where the fruit is the asset — much high-end Riesling and Assyrtiko is made this way and is unquestionably long-lived, which is itself the proof that oak is not a precondition of longevity.

**Micro-oxygenation** decouples oxygen from the vessel entirely: a metered dose to tank achieves the tannin polymerisation, colour stabilisation and softening the barrel gives, and it is standard for Malbec in Mendoza and Tannat in Uruguay and Madiran. Racking regime, lees contact, cap management and even closure oxygen transmission rate are all further levers on the same variable.

## Lees, tannin and phenolic technique

Barrels are also a texture technology, and lees are the cheaper one. Extended ageing on full lees with *bâtonnage* releases mannoproteins and polysaccharides that build mid-palate weight, bind harsh tannin and protect against oxidation — the mechanism behind Muscadet *sur lie*, Chablis, Hunter Semillon and Rioja Blanco. Ageing reds on fine lees does the same and adds reductive protection.

Tannin and structure can be built without wood: whole-bunch or partial stem inclusion for fine-grained stem tannin, extended post-fermentation maceration to polymerise tannin, *délestage* and gentle infusion instead of hard pumping-over, cold soak, saignée for concentration, and permitted oenological tannins added at crush or during ageing. For whites, skin contact and lees together produce structure that once only barrel could.

Concentration itself has non-oak routes: severe selection at sorting, later picking, saignée, and, where legal, reverse osmosis or cryoextraction.

## Whites, sparkling, and the fortified case

The question is not a red-wine question. **Whites** at the highest level rely on lees, solids, concrete, large old wood and long ageing far more than on new barriques. **Sparkling wine** builds its complexity almost entirely without new oak: long ageing on lees under crown cap, autolysis, reserve wines or a perpetual solera, and slow post-disgorgement development under a chosen closure. **Madeira** is the extreme demonstration — *estufagem* and, at the top, *canteiro* ageing in old, neutral casks in a hot loft, where heat, oxygen and decades, not new wood, create one of the world's most concentrated, complex and long-lived wines. Sherry, Rancio and vin jaune make the same point through flor and oxidative solera ageing in old butts.

## Conclusion

The answer to expensive new French oak is not simply cheaper wood. It is to identify which of the barrel's functions a given wine actually needs, and then to buy that function separately: oxygen from micro-oxygenation, racking or a porous vessel; texture from lees, mannoproteins, concrete or skin contact; tannin from stems, maceration and oenological tannin; aromatic wood character, where genuinely wanted, from a small proportion of new oak or from alternative formats. Assembled deliberately, that produces wines of concentration, complexity, balance and longevity at a fraction of the cost — and, in the white, sparkling and fortified categories, it is already how the best wines are made.
