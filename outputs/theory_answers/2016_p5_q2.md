## Who is a commentator

The question collapses if the term is read narrowly. A wine commentator is anyone whose published or spoken words about a wine are intended to influence a purchase, and that is a very wide field: subscription critics and their scores; newspaper and magazine columnists; specialist publications funded by advertising; sommeliers recommending across a table; retail floor staff and buyers' shelf notes; educators; competition judges awarding medals; bloggers and social-media influencers; crowd-sourced platforms such as Vivino and CellarTracker, where the commentary is an aggregate of strangers; and the producer's own back label, which is commentary too, simply the least disinterested kind.

## And who is the consumer

Equally wide, and the answer differs for each. The complete novice, buying occasionally and mainly worried about making a mistake. The involved regular buyer with preferences but no framework. The enthusiast or collector, spending seriously and able to taste critically. The investor, buying wine he may never open. And the trade professional, who reads commentary as market intelligence rather than as advice.

"How much should a consumer trust" therefore has no single answer. It has an answer per pairing, and the interesting work is in the criteria that generate it.

## Six tests of trustworthiness

**1. Incentive and funding.** Who pays? Robert Parker built The Wine Advocate on a subscription model that took no advertising, and that independence was the source of his authority as much as his palate was. A publication funded by the advertising of the producers it reviews faces a structural conflict, however honourably its staff behave. A sommelier's list may be shaped by supplier support; a retailer's recommendation is constrained to what is in the building; an influencer paid per post is an advertisement with a first-person voice. None of these is disqualifying, but each changes the weight the words can bear.

**2. Disclosure and links to producers.** The test is not whether a commentator has relationships but whether they are declared. A critic who consults for estates, sells wine, brokers it, or accepts hosted travel is not thereby corrupt, but a reader who does not know cannot discount properly.

**3. Method.** Blind or not; tasted in one flight or over days; how many wines in a session; whether samples were solicited or supplied; whether the wine was revisited. A hundred-point distinction drawn from thirty seconds in a room of two hundred bottles is a precision the method cannot support. Competition medals deserve particular scepticism, because entry is paid and self-selected, so the population judged is not the population on the shelf.

**4. Track record and reputation.** The only real evidence. Has this person been right, in the sense that their calls survived contact with the bottle five and ten years later? Reputation built over decades of checkable predictions is the strongest signal available, and it is the one thing a new commentator cannot manufacture.

**5. Relevance and calibration.** A commentator can be honest, competent and useless to a particular reader. Trust here is a question of fit: whether the commentator's taste, price range and drinking window match the reader's. The correct procedure is to calibrate — buy several wines a critic praised, and find out whether you agree. Until then the score is a stranger's opinion expressed in decimals.

**6. Accountability.** Can the reader check anything? Published methodology, corrections, revisited notes and stable scales all raise trust. In 2008 Wine Spectator granted an Award of Excellence to Osteria L'Intrepido, a restaurant that did not exist — a submitted list and a fee were sufficient. That is what an unaccountable process looks like, and the lesson generalises well beyond that one award.

## Applying the tests

For the **novice**, the aggregate crowd is more useful than the individual expert. A Vivino average is a poor guide to quality and a good guide to whether most people enjoyed the wine, which is the novice's actual question. Retail staff are trustworthy on suitability and untrustworthy on comparison, because they can only recommend what they stock.

For the **involved regular buyer**, one well-chosen critic, calibrated against personal experience, beats twenty uncalibrated ones. This is where trust should be highest, because the cost of being wrong is a disappointing Tuesday.

For the **collector and investor**, the stakes invert the logic. Commentary moves price here, so the incentive pressure on the commentator is greatest exactly where the buyer most needs independence; and the conviction of Rudy Kurniawan showed how far provenance can be fabricated while expert palates around the table said nothing. High spending demands lower trust and more verification, not more deference.

For the **trade professional**, commentary should be treated as data about the market, not as a verdict on the wine.

## My answer

Trust the description; do not trust the verdict.

A competent commentator is genuinely reliable on matters of fact and near-fact: whether a wine is faulty, whether it is typical of its origin, whether it is sweet, oaked, tannic, ready. Those judgements are checkable, and consensus among good tasters on them is high. Trust there should be high.

The verdict — the score, the ranking, the buy recommendation — is a different object. It compresses an aesthetic judgement, a stylistic preference and an implicit price-value calculation into one number, and it is the part most exposed to incentive, to method and to the difference between the commentator's palate and yours. Trust there should be provisional and low until earned by a personal track record.

So my answer to "how much" is: considerably less than most consumers do, and in a specific way. A consumer should treat commentary as a filter that reduces two hundred choices to ten, not as an authority that selects one; should require to know who pays; and should invest the small effort of calibrating one or two voices rather than deferring to whichever score is nearest. Jancis Robinson uses a twenty-point scale, Parker a hundred-point one, and the difference is instructive — the finer scale implies a discrimination that no taster can reproducibly deliver, and consumers read the implied precision as certainty.

**The strongest case against my position** is that the alternative to trusting commentators is worse. A shelf holds hundreds of unfamiliar bottles; the consumer cannot taste before buying; and without commentary the default proxy is price, which is a far poorer guide to quality than a critic. Scepticism, pushed too far, produces paralysis or snobbery, and the crowd-sourced alternative simply substitutes an unaccountable many for an accountable few. I accept all of that. It is why my answer is not "do not trust" but "trust conditionally, and know what you are trusting them for". The commentator's proper role is to narrow the field and to describe accurately. The verdict was never theirs to give.
