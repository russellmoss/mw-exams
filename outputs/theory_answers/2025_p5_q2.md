## Terms and scope

Three terms need pinning down before anything can be assessed. **Changing societal attitudes** here means the shift from alcohol as an unremarkable, daily, socially expected product to one treated as a discretionary health risk — a shift driven by health-authority messaging, generational change, and the arrival of alternatives that meet the same social need. **Moderation** is ambiguous and the ambiguity matters commercially: it can mean fewer drinking occasions, less volume per occasion, or lower alcohol per serve, and these three have completely different consequences for a producer's mix. **No/low** I take as the trade does — de-alcoholised or low-alcohol products, which in the EU since the 2021 reform may be labelled as wine, alongside partially de-alcoholised wines and naturally light wines.

The scope point: this is a supply-chain-wide question, not a product question. A Languedoc co-operative member, a Napa cult estate and a multinational brand owner face the same social shift with completely different exposure, and I structure the second half around that.

My position, stated up front: the industry's adaptation so far has been overwhelmingly product-led, and product-led adaptation is the weakest available response, because it treats a change in the meaning of drinking as a change in the specification of the liquid. The correct response is structural — fewer litres produced, more value per litre, and a rebuilt case for the occasion rather than the bottle.

## Part one: how the industry is actually adapting

**Product reformulation.** The visible response is no/low, plus lower-alcohol styles, plus lighter categories. De-alcoholisation by reverse osmosis, spinning cone and vacuum distillation has moved from a fringe technology to standard equipment at scale, and every major brand owner now has a no/low line. Alongside it sits a quieter and more effective shift: earlier picking, cooler sites and cooler regions, and the promotion of naturally moderate styles — Vinho Verde, German Riesling Kabinett, Moscato d'Asti, English and Tasmanian sparkling. Assessment: the second movement is working better than the first, because it sells wine rather than a substitute for it.

**Category and portfolio shifts.** Sparkling has held up far better than still, because it is occasion-led rather than volume-led; Prosecco's growth and the rise of pét-nat and canned sparkling both belong to this. Rosé's success in the US and France is of the same kind. Meanwhile brand owners have been divesting commercial volume brands and buying premium estates. Assessment: this is real adaptation and it is rational, but it is a rearrangement of the same declining pool.

**Supply-side restructuring.** The most consequential adaptation is the least discussed: vine-pull and area reduction in Bordeaux, South Australia, Chile and South Africa, plus co-operative consolidation. Assessment: painful, slow, politically fraught, and the only adaptation that addresses the actual problem, which is that the world plants for a consumption level that no longer exists.

**Marketing and policy posture.** Responsible-drinking messaging, moderation partnerships and no-alcohol serve suggestions have been adopted broadly. Assessment: much of it is defensive and unconvincing, and where the trade has publicly disputed health evidence it has damaged its own credibility for very little delay.

## What no/low can and cannot do

This deserves scrutiny rather than enthusiasm, because it is where most of the industry's optimism is parked.

First, the arithmetic. Even bullish projections for no/low wine growth do not offset the decline in the wine market as a whole; a category growing fast from a very small base cannot fill a hole in a very large one. Second, the red-wine problem: de-alcoholisation works acceptably on aromatic whites, sparkling and sweeter styles, where sugar and CO2 rebuild body, and poorly on dry reds, where removing alcohol leaves tannin hard, aromatics stripped and texture hollow. That excludes the largest single style block and, with it, a whole swathe of consumers. Third, occasion mismatch: no/low wine performs in retail and at home, but sells weakly in restaurants, where wine's margin and its cultural transmission both live. Fourth, price and margin: consumers resist paying wine prices for a product with the excise removed, while de-alcoholisation adds process cost, so the category is structurally squeezed. Fifth, an unmentioned tension — de-alcoholisation is energy-intensive, and a producer with a public carbon-reduction target is adding an energy-hungry unit operation in order to chase a health-driven consumer. Those two goals are in direct conflict and almost nobody says so.

Growth in the no/low category is overwhelmingly concentrated in beer, whose flavour architecture survives alcohol removal far better. My verdict: no/low is a legitimate portfolio hedge and a way of keeping a brand present in a moderating consumer's repertoire. It is not a category answer, and an industry that treats it as one has mistaken a line extension for a strategy.

## Part two: how it should respond

**Fix supply before demand.** The first duty is to stop producing more wine than the world will drink. That means continued area reduction concentrated in the lowest-value fruit, contract discipline, and honesty from co-operatives and regional bodies about which vineyards have no economic future. Every other response is undermined if a surplus keeps commodity wine cheap.

**Compete for the occasion, not the shelf.** Wine's remaining advantage is the table. Moderation reduces frequency, so each occasion carries more weight, and the industry should invest in food pairing, hospitality, by-the-glass programmes and smaller formats — 500ml bottles, half bottles, cans, preservation systems — that let a consumer drink one good glass rather than avoid a whole bottle. This is portfolio and distribution work, not reformulation.

**Segment the response by scale.** A small estate cannot fund a no/low line and should not try; its response is direct relationships, hospitality and price. A regional co-operative's response is supply reduction and diversification into juice, spirit base or contract processing. A multinational's response is portfolio rebalancing, no/low as a hedge, and the political and scientific engagement that only it can fund. Prescribing one response to all three is the standard error.

**Treat developing markets as a separate strategy.** In India, Brazil, Nigeria, Mexico and parts of South-East Asia, wine is a small, urban, aspirational category with no inherited drinking norm to defend. Here the challenge is not moderation but access, price, temperature control in the supply chain, and duty. Growth is genuinely available, and the industry should not export mature-market defensiveness into markets where the category has not yet been built. China is the caution: its decline was economic and cultural, not health-led, and misdiagnosing it as moderation would produce the wrong response entirely.

**Get ahead of the regulation.** Health-authority messaging and policy — labelling, excise, advertising limits, availability — will shape long-term demand far more than any product launch. Accepting labelling, publishing ingredients and nutrition, and moving marketing off health claims are cheap concessions that preserve the credibility needed to argue about the expensive things.

## Conclusion

The industry is adapting mostly by reformulating the product and rearranging its portfolio, and only reluctantly by cutting supply. That is the wrong order. It should respond by shrinking production to match a permanently smaller market, defending the occasion rather than the volume, differentiating its response by scale and by market maturity, treating no/low as a hedge rather than a rescue, and conceding the regulatory ground it cannot hold in order to keep credibility on the ground it can.
