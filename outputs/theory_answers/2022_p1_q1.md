## What makes a disease a threat

A disease's threat is not its notoriety but the product of four things: the annual economic cost it imposes, how fast and how far it spreads, whether it can be cured or only suppressed, and whether it attacks the crop or the vineyard asset. That last distinction organises my answer. The mildews cost growers more money every year than anything else, but they are contained within the season. The incurable diseases — trunk disease, and the vector-borne bacteria and viruses — destroy the planted capital itself and shorten the productive life of the vineyard. Those, I argue, are the greatest threat today.

## Trunk disease: the greatest threat

Esca, Eutypa dieback and Botryosphaeria dieback are the most serious global threat because they are incurable, cumulative and universal. Wood-colonising fungi enter through pruning wounds, spores released after rain, and progressively occlude the vascular system. The vine does not die in a season; it declines over years, losing spurs, then arms, then the trunk.

Why that is worse than an annual disease is economic. A vineyard's business case assumes twenty-five to forty productive years to amortise establishment; trunk disease can put a block into decline by year twelve to fifteen, and replanting a mature premium block costs both the planting and the lost income of the establishment years. It bites hardest exactly where value is highest: old-vine blocks in the Barossa, Rioja and the southern Rhone, and the mature vineyards of Bordeaux, Cognac and Tuscany. There is no curative chemistry since sodium arsenite, withdrawn in the EU in the early 2000s, so growers are left with wound protection, late pruning, sanitation and remedial surgery — all labour-intensive at a time when skilled vineyard labour is the scarcest input in most regions.

## Vector-borne bacteria and viruses: incurable and spreading

**Pierce's disease** (*Xylella fastidiosa*, spread by sharpshooters) kills the vine within a few seasons, has no cure, and its range is set by winter cold — which climate warming is moving poleward. The same bacterium has been established in Puglia since the 2010s on olives, which demonstrates both the regulatory response an outbreak triggers and the cost of eradication zones.

**Flavescence doree**, a phytoplasma vectored by the leafhopper *Scaphoideus titanus*, is a threat less through vine death than through the regulatory regime it forces: it is a notifiable quarantine organism in the EU, which makes insecticide treatment and the uprooting of infected vines compulsory across Burgundy, Beaujolais, the Languedoc and northern Italy. That obligation directly conflicts with organic and biodynamic certification, so it threatens growers' commercial positioning as well as their vines.

**Grapevine red blotch virus** is the most insidious, because it kills nothing. It impairs ripening — sugar accumulation stalls and anthocyanin synthesis is suppressed — so the fruit is downgraded while the vine looks healthy. It moved through North American vineyards inside apparently clean nursery material before it was even described, and the only remedy is to rogue and replant. **Grapevine leafroll**, spread by mealybugs and scale, does the same more slowly and is the defining vineyard-longevity problem of South Africa. Both are threats because the disease is in the planting material and in the global nursery trade, which means the grower can buy a fifteen-year problem on day one.

## The mildews and rots: the greatest cost, not the greatest threat

Downy and powdery mildew, and Botrytis bunch rot, remain the largest recurrent expense in the vineyard and can take an entire crop in a wet season, as northern Europe found in 2021. But they are curable, and the real threat they now pose is a regulatory and resistance one: fungicide resistance is well documented, actives are being withdrawn faster than they are replaced, and copper — the organic grower's only reliable downy mildew tool — is under EU restriction. A wet-climate organic vineyard may soon have no adequate control, which converts a manageable disease into a structural one.

## Why the threat is growing

Four forces compound. Climate change is extending vector ranges and lengthening the infection window: warmer winters raise overwintering survival of leafhoppers and mealybugs, and wetter, warmer springs extend the mildew infection period in regions that previously escaped it. The global trade in propagation material moves latent viruses faster than testing catches them, which is how red blotch crossed a continent unnoticed. The vineyard's narrow clonal genetic base means a pathogen that defeats one defence defeats it everywhere, and there is no equivalent of the annual varietal turnover that arable agriculture uses to stay ahead of disease. And the loss of active ingredients, combined with scarce and costly skilled labour, is removing the tools with which growers previously bought their way out.

## Conclusion

The greatest threats are trunk disease and the vector-borne viral and bacterial diseases, because they are incurable, they are carried in planting material or by mobile vectors that regulation cannot fully contain, and they destroy the vineyard rather than the vintage. The mildews are the greatest cost, but a cost is not a threat while it can still be paid; the danger there is the shrinking chemical toolbox. A grower planning today should treat clean, tested plant material and pruning-wound discipline as the two highest-return investments available.
