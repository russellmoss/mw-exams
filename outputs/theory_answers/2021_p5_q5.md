## Tradition, defined three ways

Tradition is a practice transmitted across generations and valued partly *because* it is inherited — the inheritance is doing work that the practice's own merits would not do alone. That definition matters here, because it means tradition is a claim about provenance, not about quality, and consumers treat it accordingly.

For a consumer, tradition arrives in three quite different forms.

**As practice**: foot-treading, basket presses, chestnut and old oak, the solera, méthode traditionnelle, hand harvesting, appellation rules on yield and ageing. Consumers meet this only indirectly — when it changes the taste in the glass or the price on the shelf.

**As narrative signal**: "family-owned since 1720", monastic origins, heraldic labels, the château, the word "traditional" itself. This is the form most consumers actually encounter, and it is a marketing asset independent of whether any traditional practice survives behind it.

**As ritual**: Champagne at celebrations, Port at Christmas, fino with tapas, Beaujolais Nouveau in November. Here tradition belongs to the consumer rather than the producer, and it is the most durable form of all.

My answer works through those three, because the honest verdict differs for each.

## Five consumers, not one

The question cannot be answered for a single consumer, and the most common failure is to assume the enthusiast is representative. Five segments, with a judgement on each:

The **involved enthusiast** is small in number, large in spend, and highly tradition-sensitive as a source of information — provenance, vintage, estate history are how they navigate. Yet their taste is increasingly counter-traditional: skin contact, low sulphur, pét-nat, forgotten varieties. Tradition is their vocabulary, not their preference.

The **mainstream everyday buyer** accounts for most volume and buys on brand, grape, price, shelf position and a remembered taste. Tradition is close to irrelevant except as background reassurance that the wine is a real thing from a real place.

The **gift and occasion buyer** is where tradition is worth most. Buying a bottle as a signal to someone else makes heritage cues directly useful, because the recipient must be able to read them without tasting. This single segment sustains a large part of the premium market.

The **new-market entrant** — in China, Brazil, India, West Africa — uses European tradition as a status proxy, imported wholesale and often without its content. Simultaneously, local traditions are being constructed at speed.

The **moderation-first younger drinker** treats tradition as a liability where it reads as formality, complexity or exclusion. Not hostility to heritage; hostility to being made to feel ignorant.

Relevance, then, is not high or low. It is highest where the wine is a message to someone else, and lowest where it is a Tuesday.

## Where tradition demonstrably works

Consumers pay for place, and appellation names still command premiums that identical liquid without them does not. They pay for continuity of ownership: Primum Familiae Vini, whose entire proposition is continuity of family ownership, exists because that story converts. They pay for ritual: the traditional-method sparkling category owns celebration in a way no substitute has dislodged, which is a consumer tradition, not a producer one. And cellar-door and wine-tourism revenue is almost entirely a trade in tradition — the visitor is buying the cellar, the family, the age of the vines, and taking a bottle home as evidence.

Note what these have in common. In each case tradition is functioning as a *shortcut*: a low-effort proxy for quality, authenticity or social meaning in a category with punishing information costs. Consumers are not valuing the past. They are using it.

## Where consumers have already abandoned it

The counter-evidence is decisive where tradition costs the consumer anything.

Closures: screwcaps took over the New Zealand and Australian bottling of white wine within roughly a decade, and consumers did not defend the cork. Formats: bag-in-box, cans and kegs grow while the "traditional" 75cl glass bottle attracts criticism on carbon grounds.

Categories: Prosecco outsells Champagne several times over by volume, on a tank-method process, a young brand history and an informal proposition — precisely the opposite of the traditional pitch, and it won. Meanwhile the categories with the most tradition per bottle have suffered most: Sherry shipments have fallen by a very large margin from their 1970s peak, and traditional German Riesling lost the UK market almost entirely. Both are, technically and historically, among the most traditional wines made. Tradition did not save either.

Labelling: consumers navigate by grape variety, a New World convention, rather than by appellation, and every European region that has added a varietal or a plain-English descriptor to its label has done so because consumers asked for it by not buying.

## When innovation becomes tradition, and back

This is where the question becomes interesting, and it also explains the pattern above.

Almost everything the consumer now reads as traditional began as disruptive technology. The Champagne bottle, remuage and dosage were 18th and 19th century innovations — a technological solution to an unstable product, now the definition of "traditional method". Rioja's long American-oak ageing was a 19th century import from Bordeaux, codified into law only in the 20th, and is now the thing modernists were accused of betraying. Barrique ageing in Barolo was heresy in the 1980s and is unremarkable now. Screwcap in Clare Valley will be a tradition within a generation, and Prosecco's tank method is already defended in traditional terms by the people who make it.

It runs the other way too. Practices lose traditional standing when the market retires them: Beaujolais Nouveau went from ritual to punchline, sweet German Riesling from mainstream to museum, and the solera has shrunk with the category that housed it.

The consequence for the consumer is the important part. Tradition is not a fixed set of practices but a status conferred retrospectively on whatever survived long enough to look inevitable. That is exactly why it works as a signal — it feels like a guarantee — and exactly why it is not one.

## What follows for the trade

Sell the benefit and let tradition carry it, rather than the reverse. Use heritage where it does real work: gifting, occasion, provenance reassurance, tourism, and the top of the range. Drop it where it obstructs: closure, format, label complexity, tone of voice, and anything that requires the consumer to already know something. And treat a producer's own conviction that consumers care about tradition with suspicion, because the trade is itself the most tradition-loving segment of the market and consistently projects its own preferences onto people who do not share them.

## Conclusion

Tradition is highly relevant to 21st-century wine consumers, but almost never as tradition. It is relevant as a proxy — for quality, authenticity, provenance and social meaning — and consumers keep it exactly as long as it is cheap to hold and abandon it the moment it costs them convenience, clarity or money. Its power is real at the premium and occasion end of the market and has already gone from the everyday purchase. The direction of travel is that the proxy keeps working while the practices behind it quietly change, which suits the trade rather well and is not something we should mistake for consumer loyalty to the past.
