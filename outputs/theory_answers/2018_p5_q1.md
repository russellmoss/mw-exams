## What a social good is

A social good is something whose benefit accrues to society as a whole rather than only to the person who consumes it: clean air, public health provision, literacy, policing, a functioning justice system. Two features characterise the category. The benefit is largely non-excludable - I gain from an educated population whether or not I paid for the schooling - and the net effect on society is positive once the costs of provision are counted.

That definition sets the two tests I apply. First, does wine confer benefits on people who are not drinking it? Second, do those benefits exceed the costs wine imposes on people who are not drinking it? A thing can be enjoyable, culturally valuable and economically significant and still fail both tests, which is why I am careful to keep this question separate from the far easier question of whether wine is a good thing. Tobacco is culturally embedded, employs millions and raises enormous tax revenue, and nobody would call it a social good.

I also set aside responsible drinking. Whether the industry markets itself well is a question about the industry's conduct, not about whether its product benefits society, and conflating them is the quickest way to answer a different question.

## The case for

**Culture, religion and history.** Wine is the oldest continuously produced agricultural product of Western civilisation, structurally embedded in Judaism and Christianity, and it carries a documented human record - of climate, trade and land tenure - that has value to everyone, drinker or not. This is a genuinely non-excludable benefit.

**Landscape as public heritage.** The strongest version of the argument is physical. UNESCO has inscribed the Alto Douro, the jurisdiction of Saint-Emilion, the climats of Burgundy and the hillsides of Champagne as World Heritage sites, and those landscapes exist only because viticulture makes marginal land economically viable. Nobody has to drink to benefit from the Douro's terraces; they are enjoyed as public goods by people who never buy a bottle.

**Rural economies that nothing else would support.** Vines grow where cereals and livestock cannot: the Mosel's slate slopes, the Douro's schist, La Mancha's plateau, the terraces of the Valtellina. Wine sustains smallholder agriculture, and with it rural population, in places whose alternative is depopulation. That is a societal benefit distinct from the drinking.

**Fiscal contribution.** Excise duty, VAT, corporation and employment taxes on wine fund general expenditure, and wine tourism draws foreign exchange into rural regions.

**Sociability.** Wine is a food-accompanying, table-based drink, and its consumption pattern in traditional markets is embedded in shared meals. There is a serious argument that this is a public benefit - social cohesion has measurable health and civic effects.

**Health.** Historically the strongest claim in the industry's armoury, and I treat it with caution below.

## The case against

**Health harm at population scale.** This is where the argument turns. The World Health Organization attributes of the order of three million deaths a year worldwide to alcohol, and the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies alcohol as a Group 1 carcinogen. The J-curve that underwrote a generation of favourable wine coverage has not survived scrutiny: the 2018 Lancet Global Burden of Disease analysis concluded that no level of consumption minimises overall health harm. An honest candidate must concede that the health case for wine as a social good is now very weak, and that a claim resting on it would need far stronger evidence than the industry currently has.

I would also refuse the move that says wine is different from other alcohol - that wine drinkers are moderate and beer and spirits drinkers are not. Ethanol is ethanol, and wine's share of hazardous consumption in the countries where it is the dominant drink is substantial. Making that argument without evidence is precisely the special pleading that damages the industry's credibility.

**Costs borne by non-drinkers.** Alcohol-attributable harm falls heavily on people who are not drinking: road deaths, domestic violence, foetal alcohol spectrum disorder, workplace injury, lost productivity and the healthcare and policing budgets that absorb them. In most developed economies the estimated societal cost of alcohol exceeds the excise revenue it generates, which reverses the fiscal argument above - the tax is not a contribution but a partial recovery.

**Legislative judgement.** Societies have already ruled on this. France's Loi Evin of 1991 restricts alcohol advertising in a wine-producing country; alcohol is prohibited outright in a number of Muslim-majority countries; minimum unit pricing, advertising bans and health warnings are all instruments applied to things society considers harmful. No state legislates to restrict clean air.

**Religious and cultural objection.** For a large fraction of humanity wine is not a neutral cultural artefact but a prohibited substance. A concept as universal as "benefits society as a whole" cannot survive that.

**Environmental and land-use cost.** Viticulture is chemically intensive by the standards of most agriculture, is a heavy water user in dry regions, and occupies land in a food-insecure world in order to produce a non-nutritive product.

## Applying the test

Weighing these, I make two observations that decide the question for me.

First, most of the benefits I listed on the "for" side are not benefits of *wine*. They are benefits of *viticulture and the wine industry* - landscape, employment, heritage, tourism. They would persist substantially if the crop were pressed into grape juice. The costs, by contrast, attach specifically to the ethanol. The benefits and the harms are therefore not properly comparable, and it is a sleight of hand to set the Douro's terraces against liver disease and call it a balance.

Second, the distributional test fails. A social good benefits society as a whole. Wine's pleasures are excludable, priced, and concentrated among the affluent in a small number of countries; its harms are non-excludable and fall disproportionately on the poor, on the young and on those around the drinker. That is the inverse of the pattern a social good exhibits.

## Conclusion

No. Wine cannot properly be considered a social good, and I think the honest answer for most of humanity - non-drinkers, the abstinent by faith, the families of the dependent, the health economist - would be a firm no.

What I will defend is narrower and, I think, more useful. The wine *industry* generates real public goods as by-products: cultural landscapes that would otherwise be abandoned, rural livelihoods, heritage, and a body of knowledge about land and climate. Those are worth protecting on their own terms. But they are the collateral benefits of an agricultural sector, not evidence that its product benefits society as a whole - and the industry weakens its own position every time it conflates the two. The case wine can actually win is that it is a legitimate, culturally valuable private pleasure whose production has valuable public side effects. That is a good deal less flattering than "social good", and considerably more defensible.
