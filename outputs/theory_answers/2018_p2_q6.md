## What textural profile is, and what that makes relevant

Textural profile is the set of tactile and trigeminal sensations a wine produces in the mouth, as distinct from its aroma and its taste: viscosity and weight, the grain and drying force of astringency, the sharpness acid imparts, the warmth of alcohol, the prickle of dissolved carbon dioxide, and the mouth-coating fullness given by colloids. Defining it that way sets the scope, because it identifies exactly six physical variables the winemaker can move — phenolics, polysaccharides, alcohol, acidity, sugar, and dissolved CO2 — plus the colloidal state that determines how the first two are perceived. Everything below acts on one of those.

Viticulture sets the phenolic and sugar raw material and I take that as given. The question is what the cellar does with it, so I work through vinification and maturation in process order, then judge how far the influence actually extends.

## Extraction: putting phenolics in, and choosing which ones

Astringency is the precipitation of salivary proteins by tannin, and its perceived grain depends on tannin size and origin. Skin tannin is softer and better polymerised than seed tannin, so decisions that limit seed extraction — gentle crushing, early pressing, avoiding aggressive pump-overs late in ferment when alcohol is high and seed tannin most soluble — reduce coarseness directly. Warmer ferments and longer maceration extract more; extended maceration after dryness extracts less new tannin but allows what is present to polymerise into longer, softer chains. Press fraction selection is the bluntest textural instrument in a red cellar.

In whites the same chemistry works at a much lower threshold. Skin contact of a few hours before pressing adds a phenolic grip that gives an otherwise thin white a spine; pushed to days or weeks, as in qvevri and skin-contact styles, it produces genuine tannic structure and a drying finish. Rosé sits between the two, and pale direct-press styles are defined by the deliberate avoidance of that grip.

## Fermentation and lees: adding polysaccharides

Polysaccharides coat the palate and, more usefully, bind tannin so that it precipitates less salivary protein — they make a wine feel rounder and simultaneously less astringent. This is why the lees decision is the strongest single textural lever in white winemaking. Yeast strain matters because strains differ in glycerol and polysaccharide production; ageing on gross lees releases mannoproteins during autolysis; bâtonnage accelerates that release and adds a reductive dimension; and the same effect can be bought directly with mannoprotein or inactivated-yeast preparations where time is not available.

Malolactic fermentation softens texture by converting a dicarboxylic acid to a monocarboxylic one, lowering titratable acidity and raising pH, which reduces perceived sharpness. It is available in every still style and is often the difference between a taut white and a broad one.

## Maturation: oak, oxygen and time

Barrel maturation acts on texture in two ways that are frequently conflated. Ellagitannin from the wood is itself astringent and adds grip, but it also acts as an oxygen intermediary, and it is the slow oxygen ingress that polymerises tannin and softens the wine. Micro-oxygenation in tank isolates that second mechanism and is used precisely to convert green, aggressive tannin into rounder tannin without adding oak flavour. Time in any vessel does some of this on its own.

## Subtracting texture before the bottle

Fining and filtration take texture away, and the decisions are as consequential as the ones that add it. Gelatine, egg white and PVPP remove astringent and bitter phenolics selectively; bentonite strips protein and, over-used, leaves a white noticeably thinner; tight membrane or pad filtration removes the colloids that carried mouthfeel. Conversely, texture can be added at the last moment: oenological tannins are added at 5-20 g/hL to restore grip, gum arabic at 100-250 mg/L to smooth a hard finish, and dissolved CO2 is the cheapest lever of all: 500-700 mg/L reads as freshness in a young white, under 400 mg/L reads as flat. Residual sugar and alcohol level complete the set, sweetness adding weight and alcohol adding both viscosity and warmth, so a partial dealcoholisation thins a wine as surely as a hard filtration does.

## Sparkling wine

Here texture is largely pressure and lees. Pressure sets the intensity of the trigeminal prickle — roughly 5-6 bar in a traditional-method sparkling wine against 1-2.5 bar in a frizzante — and long lees ageing after the second fermentation releases mannoproteins that integrate the bubble into a finer, creamier mousse. Dosage is the final adjustment: a brut nature at under 3 g/L reads leaner and more angular than a demi-sec of the same base wine.

## Fortified wine

Spirit timing is the master decision. Fortifying to about 19-20% abv while some 90-100 g/L of sugar remains, as in vintage Port, delivers weight from sugar and warmth from alcohol at once, and cuts maceration short so tannin is extracted incompletely and firmly. Biological ageing under flor consumes glycerol and leaves a fino thin, saline and cutting; oxidative ageing in a solera concentrates the wine by evaporation and leaves an oloroso viscous and broad. Two textures, from the same base wine, decided entirely in the cellar.

## To what extent

Extensively, but asymmetrically. The winemaker has near-complete control over the perception of texture — CO2, sugar, mannoprotein additions, fining and filtration are all fast, cheap and reversible in blending, and can make almost any wine feel rounder or leaner. What cannot be manufactured is the quality of tannin: fine-grained, sustained astringency comes from ripe skin phenolics, and the cellar can only choose how much of it to extract, how far to polymerise it, and how much to strip. Texture can be adjusted by anyone; it can only be built where the fruit allows.
