## Defining the two threats

Heat and drought co-occur but act through different mechanisms and demand different responses; conflating them is the first error. By **heat** I mean canopy and berry temperature above the vine's physiological optimum. Photosynthesis is most efficient at moderate leaf temperatures and falls away above roughly 30-35C, and in the same range anthocyanin synthesis is inhibited while malic acid respiration accelerates. I separate chronic warming — a rise in seasonal means — from acute heat spikes of two or three days, because the first is a site and plant-material problem and the second is a tactical, in-season one. By **drought** I mean soil water supply below the vine's evapotranspirative demand over a sustained period: a water-balance concept, not a rainfall total, since the same rainfall is ample on deep clay and a severe deficit on shallow sand over granite.

The mitigations only make sense against the physiology. Water deficit closes stomata, which halts carbon assimilation, so sugar accumulation stalls at the moment malic acid is being respired and pH and potassium climb. Berries shrivel; exposed skins burn. Thiols and terpenes degrade, rotundone does not accumulate, and green seed tannin is locked in behind high sugar. Severe stress defoliates the vine, empties reserves and reduces bud fruitfulness for the next season, so the damage is to two crops.

## Establishment decisions, which do most of the work

The cheapest effective mitigation is made before planting. Site: higher altitude, cooler aspects, proximity to water bodies, and soils with real water-holding capacity. Plant material: late-ripening, drought-adapted varieties with thick skins and good acid retention — Grenache, Carignan, Mourvèdre, Touriga Nacional, Assyrtiko, Agiorgitiko, Xinomavro — and later-ripening clones. Rootstock is the single most underused lever: 110R, 140Ru and 1103P confer drought tolerance and deeper rooting where Riparia Gloire and 101-14 do not, and Ramsey adds salinity tolerance under irrigation. Training system: bush vines and higher trunks with a sprawling canopy shade their own fruit, which is why goblet persists across La Mancha, Sicily and the southern Rhône. Row orientation matters more as climate warms: north-south rows expose the western face to the hottest afternoon hours, so a modest rotation, or east-west rows, moves exposure to the cooler morning.

## Canopy and soil in the season

Retain leaves on the afternoon side; do late-season leaf removal only on the morning face, and never strip unacclimated fruit into a forecast heat spike, which is the classic cause of sunburn. Delay winter pruning, or double-prune, to push flowering and ripening into cooler autumn conditions — established in Spain and Australia. Raise soil organic matter with compost and under-vine mulch to increase water-holding and lower soil temperature; use straw or bark mulch where cover crops would compete. Cover crops must be managed both ways: valuable for soil structure and trafficability, but mown, terminated or restricted to alternate rows in a dry season. Kaolin and other particle films reduce berry temperature and sunburn cheaply. Shade cloth and overhead netting work but are capital-intensive.

## Water: availability, cost and law

Where irrigation exists, the gains come from scheduling, not volume: soil moisture probes and a water budget, regulated deficit irrigation to hold a mild stress from set to veraison, then relief before the vine shuts down. Overhead sprinklers give evaporative cooling during a spike where water allocation permits. But three constraints are real. Water may not exist — Chile's Maipo and South Africa's Western Cape have had allocations cut in drought. It may be legally restricted: irrigation remains restricted or banned in appellations such as Champagne and much of Bordeaux without derogation. And it is priced — Australian Murray-Darling water is traded, so in a dry year irrigating a low-value block can cost more than the crop.

## Cost realism across the wine business

Advice that suits a premium estate is often absurd for the bulk sector, which is where most of the world's vineyard area sits. A block in inland irrigated Australia or California's Central Valley returning a few hundred dollars a tonne cannot amortise shade cloth, drip retrofits or hand canopy work that a Napa or Barossa block returning thousands can. For those growers the viable mitigations are the near-free ones: rootstock and variety at replanting, mulch, irrigation scheduling, machine and night harvesting, and accepting an earlier pick. Conversely, for premium fruit the binding constraint is quality, not cost, so shading and cooling justify themselves.

## Conclusion

The effective steps are overwhelmingly structural — site, rootstock, variety, training and soil organic matter — and they must be taken years ahead. In-season measures are palliative: they buy a few degrees and a few days. A vineyard manager should therefore treat each replant as the main mitigation decision, and select in-season tools against the value of the fruit and the legal water regime, not against best practice in a fine wine region.
