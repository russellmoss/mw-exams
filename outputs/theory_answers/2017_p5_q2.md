## What romance means here

Romance in wine is not a single thing, and the answer depends entirely on how widely it is drawn. I take it to have at least four strands. There is mystery — the sense that something in the bottle exceeds explanation. There is place, the belief that a wine is the irreducible expression of a particular patch of ground and a particular year. There is the human strand: the artisan, the family, the grandfather's vines, the unpredictability of harvest and the drama of a vintage saved or lost. And there is glamour — the exotic, the aspirational, the château on the label and the ritual of service. A defensible answer has to test science against all four, because science bears on them very differently, and my conclusion is that it has damaged the first while enlarging the second and third, leaving the fourth almost untouched.

By science I mean the whole modern apparatus: microbiology and selected yeasts, analytical chemistry and the identification of aroma compounds, viticultural physiology, plant genetics, remote sensing and precision farming, and the intervention technologies from reverse osmosis to cross-flow filtration. I include the sensory science that studies the taster as well as the wine, because that is where the sharpest challenge to romance has come from.

## The case that science has taken romance away

The strongest version of this argument concerns mystery. Wine that was once inexplicable is now largely explained. Fermentation was a mystery until Pasteur; the aroma of Sauvignon Blanc is now a named set of thiols and methoxypyrazines; the pepper of cool-climate Syrah is a single sesquiterpene; the reason a wine smells of a mushroom cupboard is a haloanisole with a known threshold. Once the ineffable has a molecular formula and a detection threshold in parts per trillion, a certain enchantment is gone, and it does not come back.

Science has also undermined the romance of the taster. Work in experimental psychology — the best known being the studies in which trained tasters described a white wine dyed red in the vocabulary of red wine, and the analyses showing wide inconsistency in the scores of experienced competition judges on repeated pours of the same wine — has made connoisseurship look considerably less like insight and more like suggestible pattern-matching. The romance of the infallible palate has taken real damage, and MWs have every reason to acknowledge that honestly rather than defend it.

There is also a quieter flattening effect. A generation of travelling consultants and a globally standardised oenological training have carried the same technical solutions to every continent, so wines from very different places have converged on a narrower band of ripeness, oak handling and texture. Difference is the raw material of romance, and technical competence has quietly reduced it.

Then there is the romance of unpredictability. Selected yeast strains, temperature control, must concentration, acidification, reverse osmosis, spinning cone and micro-oxygenation have between them substantially decoupled the wine in the glass from the weather. In the large commercial tier this is complete: a supermarket brand is specified to a target and delivered to that specification, vintage after vintage, from fruit that may be blended across regions. That wine has no story it did not have written for it. If romance depends on jeopardy, science has abolished the jeopardy in exactly the part of the market where most wine is actually sold.

## The case that science has created romance

Against this, three considerable arguments.

First, genetics has produced more romantic narrative in thirty years than folklore produced in three hundred. DNA profiling at Davis showed Cabernet Sauvignon to be a natural crossing of Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc (Bowers and Meredith, 1996-97) - a fact more startling than any of the legends it displaced. Ampelography and archaeology have done the same for origins: the buried qvevri in Georgia, where winemaking has been dated back some eight thousand years, are romance made evidential.

Second, science has vindicated terroir rather than dissolving it. The obvious threat — that soil science would reveal place to be a marketing fiction — has not materialised. Instead, work on water relations, soil physics, canopy microclimate, microbial populations and phenolic ripening has given a mechanistic account of why a site matters. Growers now hold a far finer map of their own land than their grandparents did, and the practical consequence has been more single-site bottlings, not fewer. Science made place legible, and legibility sells.

Third, science has rescued the artisanal. Precise, low-intervention winemaking is only safe because we understand oxygen, sulphur and microbiology well enough to take calculated risks. The most romantically presented wines of the present moment — amphora-aged, ambient-yeast, low-sulphur, old-vine field blends — depend on knowledge, refrigeration and analytical control to reach the market drinkable. Science has also saved the raw material of romance outright: without grafting onto American rootstock after phylloxera there would be no European vineyard to be romantic about, and without modern conservation many old indigenous varieties would simply have gone.

## For whom?

The question turns on who is being asked. For producers, science has replaced one romance with another. The grower has lost the romance of helplessness and gained the romance of craft: the satisfaction of a well-farmed vineyard, understood in detail, is not a lesser thing. For the trade — distributors, retailers, sommeliers — romance is a working tool, and science is a risk-control tool; they use both and feel no contradiction. The retailer wants the family story on the shelf talker and the microbiological stability report in the file. For the critic and the journalist, the demystification has been real, because their authority rested partly on the mystery, and they have adapted by trading heavily in narrative instead.

The consumer divides. At the commodity end there was never much romance to remove: the consumer buying a branded bottle on promotion wants reliability, and science has delivered that. At the fine-wine end romance is undiminished and arguably inflated, since scarcity, provenance and story now command premiums that have grown much faster than any measure of quality. And the middle — the curious, informed drinker who did not exist at this scale a generation ago — is the group for whom science has most obviously added romance, because that drinker has been taught enough to find the mechanism itself interesting.

## Conclusion

No — on balance science has not taken away the romance of wine; it has relocated it. It has stripped the romance of ignorance, which was never worth much and which the trade too often exploited, and it has supplied a richer romance of origin, genetics and craft in its place. But I would not let the industry off entirely. Science has removed romance decisively from the industrial mid-market, where the wine is now a formulated product wearing a fabricated story, and that gap between what the label says and what the process is constitutes a real reputational risk. The threat to romance is not scientific knowledge. It is the dishonest use of romantic language about wines that science has made completely predictable.
