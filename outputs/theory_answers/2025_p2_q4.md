## Framing

Each scenario is diagnosed against its own analytical numbers and its stylistic or regulatory context, since the same figure means different things in different wines. I separate prevention from correction throughout.

## a. 0.9 g/L volatile acidity — Chablis, pH 3.2, 12.5% ABV, intended for lees ageing

**Diagnosis.** The wine is legal but stylistically compromised: 0.9 g/L sits above the commonly cited sensory threshold of about 0.7 g/L, and the associated ethyl acetate — the more damaging component sensorially — will read as sharp and solvent-like. Chablis is sold on freshness, mineral precision and linearity; volatile acidity destroys exactly that, whereas the same figure in a Barossa Shiraz might read as lift. At pH 3.2 the winemaker had excellent SO2 efficacy available, so this is not a chemical failure: the likely causes are a stressed primary fermentation producing acetic acid, or acetic acid bacteria on damaged fruit or in the cellar. The intended lees ageing is the aggravating factor — lees consume free SO2 and bâtonnage introduces oxygen, both of which favour acetic acid bacteria, so the trajectory is upward.

**Prevention.** Clean, undamaged, cool fruit to limit initial microbial load; rigorous sanitation of press, hoses and vessels with oxygen exclusion during handling; yeast health managed through adequate assimilable nitrogen and controlled fermentation temperature so the ferment goes to dryness without stalling; and an SO2 regime of 25-30 mg/L free SO2, which is the appropriate maintenance regime at pH 3.2 and delivers ample molecular SO2 at that pH. Full, topped vessels and prompt racking off gross lees complete it.

**Correction.** Volatile acidity cannot be reversed. Blending with a low-VA Chablis lot is the practical answer and dilutes both acetic acid and ethyl acetate below threshold, though it consumes sound wine. Reverse osmosis with an anion-exchange step on the permeate removes acetic acid selectively but strips aroma and adds cost, so is justifiable only on a valuable lot. If the population is still active, sterile filtration must precede anything else. And the lees-ageing plan should be curtailed: I would rack clean, hold under inert gas at a maintained free SO2, and forgo bâtonnage.

## b. Strong reductive aromas post-MLF — Barossa Shiraz, pH 3.8, 15% ABV, 24-month oak

**Diagnosis.** Reduction is the accumulation of hydrogen sulphide, mercaptans and, over time, disulphides — the opposite of oxidation, and the distinction matters because the two are treated in opposite directions. It arises from yeast sulphur metabolism under nitrogen deficiency and from a low-redox, oxygen-limited environment. Post-malolactic conversion the wine is at its most anaerobic: the bacteria have consumed residual oxygen, the wine is unsulphured, and the conversion has pushed pH up from an already high 3.8. At that pH molecular SO2 is very low for any given free SO2, so microbial stability is genuinely at risk even at 15% alcohol, which suppresses lactic acid bacteria but not *Brettanomyces*. The timing is decisive: over 24 months in oak, free thiols oxidise to disulphides, which are not removable by copper and are far harder to treat, so intervention must happen now.

**Prevention.** Nitrogen management is first — measuring YAN and supplementing early rather than late, when addition can itself trigger sulphide release — followed by low-H2S-producing yeast strains. The Barossa context supplies the structural answer: open-top fermenters with hand plunging or splashing pump-overs are the regional norm and are inherently aerative, dissipating sulphides as they form. Early post-fermentation racking with air contact does the same.

**Correction.** Bench trials first, with copper sulphate and with copper plus ascorbic acid, to establish which fraction is free thiol and which is bound disulphide. Aeration by splash racking is the cheapest and least invasive fix for simple H2S. Copper addition treats free thiols but leaves residual copper that catalyses later oxidation and can itself cause reduction in bottle; copper additions are capped at around 1 mg/L residual in most jurisdictions. Where disulphides have formed, ascorbic acid reduces them back to thiols so copper can act. I would then rack, set SO2, and consider a modest tartaric acidification: dropping pH toward 3.6 improves colour stability and materially improves SO2 efficacy before a two-year élevage.

## c. 180 mg/L total SO2 — Bordeaux blend, pH 3.6, 13.5% ABV, pre-bottling

**Diagnosis.** This is a regulatory problem before it is a sensory one. 180 mg/L exceeds the EU ceiling of 150 mg/L total SO2 for dry red wine, making the wine unsaleable in its home market; the United States permits up to 350 mg/L, so the wine is legal elsewhere but not where it must be sold. How it got there is instructive: at pH 3.6 a large free SO2 addition is needed for adequate molecular SO2, and much of what was added has bound to acetaldehyde, pyruvate and keto acids from a stressed or oxidised ferment. Botrytis-affected fruit would compound this.

**Prevention.** Sound fruit; protective handling to limit acetaldehyde formation; yeast strains producing little pyruvate and keto acid; earlier tartaric acidification so a lower pH does the antimicrobial work SO2 is being asked to do; and, above all, dosing against a molecular SO2 target calculated for the wine's pH rather than adding by habit and measuring total only at the end.

**Correction.** Options are weak, which is why prevention dominates. Blending with a low-SO2 lot of the same blend is the only reliable legal route and is what I would do. Aeration through open racking oxidises some free SO2 to sulphate, and time reduces the free fraction, but the bound majority does not respond. Hydrogen peroxide is used in some jurisdictions but is not an authorised EU practice. Redirecting the wine to a permissive market is commercial rather than technical.

## Conclusion

All three faults are cheaper to prevent than to correct, and in each case the wine's own pH determined how much room the winemaker had. Only the volatile acidity scenario has a genuinely satisfactory correction, and even that costs sound wine.
