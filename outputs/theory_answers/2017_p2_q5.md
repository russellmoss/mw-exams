## The proposition, and my position

Temperature is the only variable a winemaker manipulates at literally every stage, from the harvester in the vineyard to the container crossing the equator, and refrigeration is what made quality winemaking possible in warm climates — so in that historical sense it is indeed the defining technology of *modern* winemaking. But "the key" overstates it. Temperature is an enabling control, not a style in itself; it works alongside oxygen and microbial management, and applying the right number at the wrong stage is worthless. I therefore agree with the statement in its historical claim and qualify it in its analytical one. The way to test it is to walk the process.

## Harvest and fruit storage

A rise of 10C roughly doubles the rate of most chemical and enzymatic reactions, so fruit delivered at 32C is oxidising and fermenting spontaneously before it reaches the crusher. Night machine harvesting, refrigerated transport and short haul times deliver fruit at 12C instead. Where fruit must wait, cool rooms hold it at 4C overnight, which also firms the berries for whole-bunch pressing. Temperature is used as a positive tool here too: cryoextraction chills botrytised fruit to about -7C so that the most watery berries stay frozen in the press, and carbonic maceration requires whole berries held at 30C under carbon dioxide for the intracellular enzymes to work.

## Processing the must

Two operations are commonly confused and are not the same thing. **Must chilling** is the reduction of white juice temperature through a plate or tube-in-tube heat exchanger immediately after pressing, to slow oxidation and microbial activity and to allow cold settling of white juice at 10C for 12 to 24 hours before racking clear juice off the solids. It is protective and clarifying. Cold soak holds destemmed red must at 4-7C for up to about six days, below the threshold at which yeast will start, to extract water-soluble anthocyanins and gentle tannin before alcohol changes the extraction profile. The two share a temperature and share nothing else, and calling white must chilling a cold soak is a category error.

Heat is also a processing tool. Thermovinification and flash détente use it for extraction and to destroy laccase in rotten fruit or to strip smoke taint: flash détente takes must to 85C and then vacuum-cools it in seconds.

## Fermentation

Here the values are stylistic decisions. Aromatic whites at 14C, barrel-fermented whites at 20C, reds at 28C: the cool white ferment retains fermentation esters and volatile thiols that would be stripped by carbon dioxide at higher temperature, the warmer barrel ferment trades some aroma for texture and oak integration, and the red ferment needs the heat to extract anthocyanin and tannin and to allow a healthy, fast conversion. Control matters because fermentation is strongly exothermic: an uncooled large red tank will climb past 35C on its own, and above 35C yeast is stressed, volatile acidity rises and stuck fermentation follows. Sparkling base wines ferment cool, and the second fermentation is conducted at 12C over months precisely because a slow, cold ferment gives finer, better-integrated bubbles.

## Malolactic and post-fermentation

Malolactic bacteria work well at 20C and stall below 15C, so barrel halls are heated to secure it in a cool cellar — and cooling to below 15C with an SO2 addition is exactly how the winemaker *blocks* malolactic in an aromatic white. Extended maceration on skins after fermentation is held warm to continue tannin polymerisation, or cooled to stop it.

## Stabilisation

Cold stabilisation at -4C for one to two weeks precipitates potassium bitartrate, and it is one of the largest energy costs in the winery, which is why contact seeding, electrodialysis, carboxymethylcellulose and potassium polyaspartate have become mainstream alternatives — a genuinely modern shift away from temperature. Heat is used in the laboratory for the opposite test: heat stability trials hold a sample at 80C for two hours to force protein haze and set the bentonite dose.

## Ageing and long-term storage

A cellar held at 13C and stable is the target for maturation: warmer wine oxidises faster, evaporates more from barrel and matures on a shorter arc. Stability matters as much as the number, since cycling drives wine past the cork. But temperature is also used as a deliberate style-maker in oxidative wines: estufagem holds Madeira at up to 50C for at least three months, canteiro lofts do the same more slowly over years, and tawny Port and Rutherglen Muscat are matured warm on purpose.

## Bottling and beyond

Wine is bottled cool and still; flash pasteurisation at around 72C is available as a microbial alternative to sterile filtration for sweet or lower-alcohol wines. After bottling the winemaker's control ends but the risk does not: a container crossing the tropics can exceed 40C, so temperature-controlled shipping and warehousing are now part of the technical specification for any wine intended to arrive as it left.

## Conclusion

Temperature management is not the key to modern winemaking so much as its precondition. Nothing else is applied at every stage; nothing else so directly enabled warm-climate white wine, reliable red fermentation and stable long-distance shipping. But its value lies entirely in knowing what it is for at each step — protective at pressing, extractive in cold soak, stylistic in fermentation, transformative in estufagem. A winemaker who controls temperature and neglects oxygen or microbiology will still make poor wine.
