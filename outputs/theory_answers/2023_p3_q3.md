## What stability means, and the thesis

Stability is the wine's ability to reach the consumer and survive in their hands without throwing a haze or deposit, browning, or restarting a fermentation. Four axes apply to all three wines — protein, tartrate (cold), colour and oxidative, and microbial — and all three producers must satisfy all four. What differs is the tool chosen, and the choice is decided by how the wine is made, how long and in what conditions it must survive, and what the producer is willing to lose. Entry-level Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc buys certainty and pays in aroma. Meursault buys texture and ageing and pays in risk. Sélection de Grains Nobles carries the highest risk and has the fewest usable tools.

## How the three are made, and why that decides everything

The Sauvignon is machine-harvested at scale, sulphured hard on the harvester, cool-settled, inoculated, fermented cool in stainless steel to protect volatile thiols, blocked from malolactic so malic acid stays as freshness, often finished with a few grams of residual sugar, and bottled under screwcap within months. Volumes are enormous — the Marlborough scale of Villa Maria and Brancott — so a single instability becomes a million-bottle recall.

Domaine-bottled Meursault is whole-bunch pressed, fermented with indigenous yeast in 228-litre barrels, taken fully through malolactic, aged on fine lees for a year or more with minimal SO2 during élevage, then bottled under cork with little or no filtration and expected to age a decade. Domaine des Comtes Lafon and Domaine Roulot bottle this way.

Sélection de Grains Nobles Riesling is botrytis-affected fruit picked in successive tries at extreme must weight, pressed slowly from shrivelled berries, fermented very slowly and stopping incomplete, leaving residual sugar often well above 100 g/L at modest alcohol, and usually with malic acid intact because malolactic never happens. The Alsace houses that make it — Trimbach, Hugel and Zind-Humbrecht among them — bottle it to last thirty years.

## Protein stability: bentonite

Heat-unstable pathogenesis-related proteins are common to all three, and the 80 degree heat test is the arbiter in all three cellars. The contrast is in what the fining costs.

For the Sauvignon it is non-negotiable: a bench trial establishes the minimum effective dose and the wine is fined in bulk before bottling, because a case in a warm delivery van is the worst case. The cost is real — bentonite strips thiols with the protein — which is why the dose is trialled to the minimum rather than applied generously.

Meursault often needs little or none, and the production style is the reason: barrel fermentation and extended lees contact release mannoproteins that stabilise protein colloidally, and the wine is sold into cellars rather than warm shelves. The domaine accepts a small residual risk to keep texture.

SGN is the awkward case. Botrytis raises protein and colloid load, but the wine's sugar and viscosity change protein solubility and slow settling, so trials must be run on the finished sweet wine, and the lees volume after fining is a real loss at that value.

## Tartrate stability: the cold test

The Sauvignon must be unconditionally cold stable — chilled retail display makes crystals visible and generates returns. That is done by the contact process, chilled and seeded with potassium bitartrate at around minus 4 degrees, or by electrodialysis at volume, or by carboxymethylcellulose, which is cheap and effective in whites but must be added after protein fining because it interacts with bentonite.

Meursault typically does none of this. A Burgundian cellar cold through winter with the wine in barrel delivers slow natural precipitation, and a deposit in a bottle of that price is understood by the buyer, not returned.

SGN cannot use the easy routes. Chilling a viscous, high-sugar wine to crystallise is slow and inefficient, and metatartaric acid — the cheap inhibitor — hydrolyses within a few years, which is worthless in a wine intended to age for decades. In practice long, cold, slow élevage does the work.

## Colour and oxidative stability

Three strategies for the same enemy. The Sauvignon is protected reductively throughout — SO2 from the harvester, inert gas, minimal oxygen pickup — because oxidation costs both colour and the thiols that define the style.

Meursault does the opposite at the front end: juice is allowed controlled oxidation before fermentation so that oxidisable phenolics polymerise and drop out, leaving a wine less prone to browning later. Premature oxidation in white Burgundy is the well-documented reminder that low SO2 through élevage plus closure variability is where this style's colour risk actually sits.

SGN faces the hardest version of the problem: botrytis brings laccase, which unlike grape polyphenol oxidase remains active at wine pH, is far less inhibited by SO2, and oxidises a much wider range of substrates. Control is fruit selection, high SO2 at the press, rapid settling, and where necessary heat treatment, since laccase is heat-labile; laccase activity is measured rather than assumed.

## The SGN problem: microbiology and filterability

Microbial stability inverts the ranking. Meursault has completed malolactic and has no sugar, so it is intrinsically the most stable and can be bottled unfiltered. The Sauvignon retains malic acid and often a little sugar, so it needs membrane filtration at 0.45 micron and sufficient molecular SO2 to prevent an in-bottle malolactic.

SGN carries both hazards at once: high residual sugar for yeast and retained malic acid for bacteria, at modest alcohol. Refermentation in bottle is the catastrophic failure. It needs high molecular SO2 — helped by low pH — plus sterile filtration. And that is exactly where botrytis takes its revenge: beta-glucans blind membranes, so the wine must be treated with glucanase, settled long and cross-flow filtered gently. Filterability, not the treatments themselves, is the practical constraint.

## Conclusion

Same four stabilities, opposite priorities. The Sauvignon eliminates every risk by intervention because failure is a recall; Meursault removes almost nothing, relying on its own production style and on a forgiving consumer, with oxidation its true exposure; SGN must achieve the strictest microbial stability of the three with the most obstructive matrix in the cellar.
