## The objective, and why bottling is the deadline

Quality control at bottling exists to ensure the wine reaches the consumer palatable, typical of its style and label, stable, free of faults and contaminants, and compliant with every applicable regulation. Bottling is the last point at which any of that can be corrected: once the wine is sealed, an error becomes a recall, a write-off of unsaleable stock, a disappointed consumer and lasting damage to the brand. Laboratory analysis is therefore not record-keeping; it is the evidence on which a small number of irreversible decisions are taken. I cover the wine, the dry goods and packaging operation, the compliance position, and staff safety.

## Microbial stability: the filtration decision

The manager needs to know whether the wine can referment or be spoiled in bottle, and therefore how tightly to filter and how much preservative to add.

Residual sugar is the first question, because a wine with fermentable sugar and viable yeast will referment. Malic acid tells whether malolactic fermentation is complete or was blocked: below about 0.1 g/L confirms it is finished, with the AWRI target being under the enzymatic detection limit of 0.05 g/L; a wine deliberately holding malic must be bottled sterile. Membrane filtration or plating gives the live population and identifies *Brettanomyces*, with 4-ethylphenol quantifying any damage already done. pH sets how effective the SO2 will be.

The decision that follows is the filtration specification: a 0.45 micron membrane, which retains yeast and most spoilage bacteria, or 0.65 micron where the wine is clean and phenolic stripping is a concern, and whether the filler must be sterilised and run aseptically. It is also the DMDC decision — a dose at the filler where the population is low but not zero — and the sorbate decision for an off-dry wine.

## Oxidative stability: the additive decisions

Free and total sulphur dioxide, read against pH, determines the addition made minutes before filling. The target is molecular SO2 of about 0.6 mg/L for a dry red - the AWRI's standard recommendation - rising to the 0.8 mg/L that inhibits lactic acid bacteria where risk is higher, as in a wine holding residual sugar. Total SO2 is then checked against the legal ceiling, which the free addition must not breach: EU total SO2 ceilings of 150 mg/L for dry red and 200 mg/L for dry white and rosé, rising to 400 mg/L for the sweetest categories.

Dissolved oxygen and total package oxygen are measured on filled bottles off the line, with dissolved oxygen held below about 1 mg/L for an age-worthy white and total package oxygen specified separately; a high reading stops the line and sends the operator to the filler bowl inerting and the vacuum-jetting step. Protein instability is assessed by a heat test at 80°C - two hours on the current AWRI method, six on the older industry practice - with a turbidity rise above about 2 NTU calling for a bentonite trial and addition. Tartrate stability is assessed by conductivity or a cold test, and the decision is between cold stabilisation, which needs days and a chiller, and a CMC or potassium polyaspartate addition made in-line — a decision that must be taken before the last filter, not after. Turbidity itself should be under 1 NTU for a bright white, and iron and calcium are read for casse risk. Each of these is a decision with a lead time, which is why the analysis has to be booked well before the bottling date.

## Compliance with specification and with the law

Alcohol must be within the 0.5% abv label tolerance, or the label is wrong. Volatile acidity is checked against the EU volatile acidity ceiling, of the order of 1.2 g/L expressed as acetic acid, and against the buyer's tighter internal specification. Allergen residues from egg, milk or fish fining are verified so the label declaration is accurate, as is the sulphite declaration once total SO2 exceeds the threshold. Contaminant screens cover ochratoxin A against the EU limit of 2 micrograms per kilogram, and heavy metals and pesticide residues where the destination market demands them. Retail customers routinely impose an analytical specification of their own, and a wine outside it is rejected on delivery.

## Dry goods and the packaging process

The wine is only half of it. Bottles are checked dimensionally and for internal cleanliness; closures for oxygen transmission rate, extraction force and, for cork, releasable TCA on a soak test; capsules and labels against artwork and barcode. On the line the checks are fill height, torque or insertion depth, headspace oxygen, seal integrity and label placement, sampled at a set frequency, with retained samples and batch traceability so any later problem is isolated to a pallet rather than a vintage. HACCP is the framework: the filler, the closure and the final filter are the critical control points, each with a limit, a monitoring method and a corrective action. That sits inside the wider quality assurance system, and for most commercial producers inside ISO or BRC certification, because wine is a foodstuff.

## Staff safety on the line

Bottling is the most hazardous operation in a winery. Glass breaks at the filler and capper, bringing cuts and eye injury; line noise routinely requires hearing protection; palletising and case handling cause lifting injuries; sulphur dioxide, caustic and peracetic acid used in CIP are respiratory and burn hazards; and confined-space entry kills people every year through CO2 and nitrogen asphyxiation. The QC manager's remit covers guarding, protective equipment, gas monitoring and the discipline of stopping the line, and a breach here costs more than any wine fault.
