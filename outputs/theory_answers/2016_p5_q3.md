## Definitions, and the difference that matters

**Tradition** is a practice transmitted across generations and valued partly *because* it is transmitted: its authority comes from having been done before. In wine it is codified more formally than in almost any other industry — appellation law is tradition with statutory force, fixing varieties, yields, training systems, minimum ageing and, in places, the shape of the bottle.

**Innovation** is the introduction into a context of a practice new to that context, adopted because it changes an outcome. Its authority comes from results, and it is context-relative: qvevri fermentation is tradition in Georgia and innovation in the Barossa.

The difference is therefore not old versus new but two different sources of legitimacy: tradition legitimises by continuity, innovation by demonstrated advantage. That matters for this question because it means they are not naturally in opposition. Most traditions are simply innovations that survived long enough to be forgotten as innovations.

**Important** needs defining too, because the answer changes with it. Important to what — the quality of what is in the glass, the survival of producers, the value the wine commands, the cultural inheritance, or the distinctiveness that makes wine different from other drinks? I take all five, and I argue the question along the chain from the land to the end user, because both forces operate at every link and an answer that examines only the cellar will mislead.

## In the vineyard

The case for tradition here is strong and often underrated. Site selection in the classic regions is the accumulated result of centuries of trial that nobody can now afford to repeat: the Mosel's steepest south-facing slate, the Côte d'Or's mid-slope, the *cru* boundaries of Barolo. Massal selection preserves genetic diversity that clonal nurseries narrowed. Bush vines, dry farming and high-density planting are traditional answers to heat, drought and poor soil that precision viticulture is now rediscovering with instruments.

The case for innovation is equally strong and, at one moment, was decisive: grafting European vines onto American rootstock after phylloxera saved European viticulture, and every "traditional" vineyard in France today rests on a nineteenth-century technological rescue. That is the single most important fact in this debate — the tradition survives only because of an innovation. The same pattern is running again: rootstock selection for drought, canopy management and later pruning for delayed ripening, precision irrigation where it is permitted, and disease-resistant varieties that cut fungicide passes.

## In the winery

Tradition's case: large old oak, concrete, ambient fermentation, basket pressing, extended lees ageing, solera systems, foot-treading in the Douro — practices retained not from sentiment but because they produce results that stainless steel and selected yeast do not. The recent movement of good producers *back* to these methods is the strongest available evidence that tradition encodes real knowledge.

Innovation's case: temperature-controlled fermentation, which from the 1960s made dry white wine possible in warm regions and effectively created the modern industries of Australia, Chile, South Africa and southern Spain, is a larger contribution to the quality of what most people drink than anything tradition has offered. Add inert-gas handling, refrigeration, hygiene, sterile filtration and cross-flow, and the plain fact is that faulty wine has become rare. Innovation raised the floor; tradition still largely defines the ceiling.

This link also shows the continuum most clearly. Amphora fermentation is a Georgian tradition adopted as an innovation in Friuli and Australia. Ripasso, once a domestic economy, is now a technique. Carbonic maceration travelled from Beaujolais as an innovation everywhere else.

## In packaging and at the point of sale

The screwcap is the clean case. It was an innovation against a five-hundred-year tradition; New Zealand producers organised a collective move to screwcap in the early 2000s, and it is now so entrenched there that it is the local tradition, while cork remains traditional and commercially necessary elsewhere. One generation converted an innovation into a tradition.

At the consumer end, tradition still does most of the selling. The consumer pays for Burgundy's *climats*, for Champagne's method, for Rioja's ageing categories — that is, for tradition made legible. Meanwhile the route to market has been reinvented: direct shipping, e-commerce, subscription, canned and kegged wine. Note which is which: innovation in how wine reaches the drinker, tradition in why the drinker wants it.

## What tradition is actually for

Before ranking, the strongest form of the case for tradition. Wine is not sold as a beverage; it is sold as a place, a history and a set of constraints voluntarily accepted. Strip those away and you have a well-made alcoholic drink competing on price against other well-made alcoholic drinks — which is the fate of every category that innovated away its identity. Tradition is what allows a hectare in Chambolle-Musigny to be worth what it is worth. It is also a genuine repository of knowledge: appellation rules are, at their best, the empirically selected answers to that place's problems, and their conservatism has repeatedly protected regions from fashions that would have cost them their distinctiveness.

## The ranking, since the question asks for one

If forced to choose — and the question does force it — I judge **innovation more important now, and tradition more important to value**.

Innovation is more important now because the binding constraints have changed. Warming, water scarcity, disease pressure, labour cost and falling consumption are problems tradition has no answer to, since tradition is by definition an answer to the conditions that produced it. Regions are already conceding this: Bordeaux approved six new varieties for its regional appellations in 2021, among them Touriga Nacional, Marselan and Alvarinho, and Champagne has admitted the fungus-resistant Voltis to trial. When the most tradition-bound appellations in the world amend their varietal lists, the argument is effectively settled on the ground.

Tradition is more important to value because it is the entire reason wine commands a premium over other agricultural products. Innovation raises the floor; tradition sets the price.

But the ranking is less interesting than the relation between them, and here is where I would rest the answer: in wine, innovation's usual purpose is to preserve a tradition, not to replace it. Grafting preserved the French vineyard. New varieties are being admitted so that Bordeaux can still taste like Bordeaux at two degrees warmer. Resistant vines are being trialled so that Champagne can keep farming Champagne. The two are not competing forces but a single mechanism with a long feedback loop: innovation earns its place by surviving into tradition, and tradition earns its keep by making the innovation worth undertaking. A region that has only tradition becomes a museum and dies of climate; a region that has only innovation becomes a factory and dies of price. The producers who will matter in twenty years are those who can tell which of their traditions carry knowledge and which merely carry habit.
