## Which bacteria matter, and one clarification

Two families of bacteria are relevant to wine. Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) — principally *Oenococcus oeni*, with *Lactobacillus*, *Pediococcus* and *Leuconostoc* species — are facultative and tolerate wine's low pH and alcohol. Acetic acid bacteria (AAB), chiefly *Acetobacter* and *Gluconobacter*, are strict aerobes. One organism must be excluded at the outset: *Brettanomyces* is a yeast, not a bacterium, and its volatile phenols come from a hydroxycinnamic acid pathway, not a bacterial one. I examine the beneficial and spoilage roles by pathway, then the management levers, then how the balance shifts by style.

## The beneficial pathway — *Oenococcus oeni* and malolactic conversion

Malolactic conversion is not a fermentation in the energetic sense that primary fermentation is. The malolactic enzyme decarboxylates L-malic acid, a dicarboxylic acid, directly to L-lactic acid, a monocarboxylic acid, releasing CO2, with NAD+ and manganese as cofactors. Three consequences follow. Titratable acidity falls, typically by around half the malic concentration, and pH rises by something like 0.1-0.3 units; the palate softens because lactic acid is perceptibly less aggressive than malic. Microbiological stability improves, because malic acid is the substrate any later LAB population would have used — removing it in the cellar prevents a refermentation in bottle with its haze, spritz and off-odours. And *O. oeni* metabolises citric acid in parallel, producing diacetyl, whose buttery character is dose-dependent and can be tuned by lees contact (yeast reduce diacetyl to odourless diol) or by early SO2 addition to lock it in.

## Spoilage pathways

*Lactobacillus* and *Pediococcus* are the LAB to fear. Both decarboxylate amino acids — histidine to histamine, tyrosine to tyramine — producing biogenic amines that are a health and regulatory concern in several markets and carry no sensory warning. *Pediococcus* secretes exopolysaccharides that produce ropiness, an oily viscous texture. Certain *Lactobacillus* strains, with *Brettanomyces*, produce tetrahydropyridines responsible for mousiness, which is pH-dependent in expression and only appears retronasally, so it escapes routine nosing. Some strains metabolise glycerol to acrolein, which binds phenolics and gives bitterness. These organisms become dominant when *O. oeni* is not — at high pH, high temperature, or after a sluggish or delayed malolactic conversion.

Acetic acid bacteria act only in the presence of oxygen. They oxidise ethanol to acetaldehyde and then to acetic acid, which is the dominant component of volatile acidity, and a portion is esterified to ethyl acetate, whose nail-varnish character has a lower sensory threshold than acetic acid itself and is the real fault most of the time. *Gluconobacter* prefers sugar-rich juice and is the vector on damaged fruit; *Acetobacter* takes over in finished wine at any air interface. They thrive with oxygen exposure and at pH above 3.5.

## Managing bacteria to encourage them

Where malolactic conversion is wanted, the parameters are narrow. *O. oeni* performs reliably in a pH range of about 3.2-3.5, a temperature of 18-22°C, alcohol below about 14%, and low total SO2 — a bound fraction it can partly tolerate but a free fraction it cannot. Practical levers are inoculation timing and nutrition. Co-inoculation with the yeast at or shortly after the start of primary fermentation exploits a lower alcohol and nutrient-rich environment and shortens the vulnerable window between the end of primary fermentation and the setting of SO2; the trade is less control over diacetyl and more competition with the yeast. Sequential inoculation after primary fermentation gives cleaner style control but leaves the wine unsulphured and exposed for longer. Where pH is low or alcohol high, *Lactobacillus plantarum* is a useful alternative starter, homofermentative and better suited to co-inoculation because it does not metabolise sugar to acetic acid. Warming the cellar, retaining gross lees for nutrients, and barrel inoculation all help.

## Managing bacteria to suppress them

Suppression rests on the same variables inverted. pH below 3.2 substantially inhibits LAB, while above 3.5 both LAB and AAB become far harder to control — and, crucially, molecular SO2, the only antimicrobial form, falls as pH rises, so high-pH wines need much larger free SO2 additions for the same effect. The working target is molecular SO2 below roughly 0.5 mg/L to permit malolactic conversion, and at or above that figure to prevent it. Lysozyme hydrolyses the cell wall of Gram-positive bacteria and is therefore effective against LAB but useless against AAB; it also strips protein and can cause haze in whites. Dimethyl dicarbonate offers a sterilant dose at bottling that dissipates to methanol and CO2, and is best used with clean, low-load wine. Cool storage, sterile filtration at 0.45 micron pre-bottling, and rigorous hygiene of pumps, hoses, barrels and drains complete the picture. Against AAB specifically the answer is oxygen exclusion — sound fruit, topped barrels, inert gas blanketing of tanks and headspaces, minimal transfers — because no SO2 regime survives repeated air contact.

## Style, and the trade-offs

Practice varies with style. Most reds — Syrah in the Northern Rhône or Barossa — go through full malolactic conversion for stability and texture. Chablis may use partial conversion to retain tension. Aromatic whites such as Riesling suppress it entirely by early SO2 and sterile filtration, because lactic softening would erase the style. Port tolerates volatile acidity that would be a fault elsewhere, where it forms part of the oxidative character. The strategic trade is predictability against expression: inoculated *O. oeni* is fast, safe and uniform, while an indigenous conversion in barrel may add complexity and site expression at the cost of a longer window in which *Pediococcus* or *Brettanomyces* can establish.

## Conclusion

Bacteria are a tool and a threat governed by the same three variables: pH, oxygen and molecular SO2. Get those right for the style intended and the desirable pathway is favoured; get them wrong, particularly at pH above 3.5, and the spoilage pathways are the ones the wine will take.
