## Consolidation and fragmentation are the same phenomenon

Both are happening at once, and for one reason: returns to scale are strongly positive in some activities of this industry and strongly negative in others. Scale wins where fixed costs are large and the product is undifferentiated — glass, closures, distribution, retail buying, high-volume brands. It loses where value comes from scarcity, place, story or proximity to the drinker, and where technology has removed the fixed costs that used to force producers to be big. So the question is not whether the industry is consolidating, but which activities have had their cost structure changed. I take four such areas, explain the mechanism in each, and name businesses winning as a result. I draw the examples from four continents deliberately: a fragmentation argument that only works in one market is an argument about that market.

## 1. Production — the capital barrier has collapsed

Making wine used to require owning a winery. Custom-crush and contract facilities, mobile bottling and mobile filtration, leased tank space and freelance winemaking now let a producer buy the whole production chain by the hour. The consequence is measurable: the number of bonded wineries in the United States has grown several-fold since the turn of the century, from roughly 2,000 to well over 10,000 on trade estimates, while the share of volume held by the largest few companies has also risen. Both tails have grown and the middle has hollowed out.

Who wins: virtual and micro-négociant businesses that carry no plant — Cameron Hughes in California built a business buying finished lots and selling them under lot numbers, with no vineyard and no winery. In Australia and New Zealand, contract crush has let a generation of small labels reach market without capital.

## 2. Route to market — the distribution moat has leaks

Distribution consolidated ferociously, and that is exactly what created the opening. In the United States the 2005 Supreme Court decision in Granholm v. Heald struck down discriminatory bans on interstate direct shipment, and direct-to-consumer sales have grown into a major channel for wineries too small for a distributor to bother with. Elsewhere, third-party logistics, bonded-warehouse fulfilment and low-cost e-commerce platforms mean a producer can reach a consumer without ever entering a distributor's portfolio.

Who wins: Naked Wines, which funds independent winemakers from subscriber deposits, was bought by Majestic in 2015 and eventually gave the merged group its name — a business that exists only because it needed neither distributor nor shelf. In Champagne, grower Champagnes bottled by récoltants-manipulants such as Egly-Ouriet and Larmandier-Bernier have taken value away from the houses they once sold fruit to, because sommeliers and specialist merchants will now buy 20,000 bottles a year direct.

## 3. Consumer taste — the discovery cost has fallen to zero

Fragmenting demand is the deepest cause. When information was expensive, the rational consumer bought the famous name; the brand was a substitute for knowledge. Search, social media and crowd-sourced reviewing have made discovery close to free, so obscurity is no longer a commercial death sentence, and a generation whose identity is expressed through the specificity of what it drinks now treats the famous name as a negative signal. Small volume becomes an asset rather than a handicap.

Who wins: Etna's producer count has grown substantially, from a handful of estates in the 1990s to well over a hundred today, and its producers sell at prices the same fruit could not have commanded as anonymous Sicilian bulk. Georgia's qvevri producers, Jura, the Canaries and England have all been recruited from obscurity by the same mechanism. Chapel Down raised significant capital through an equity crowdfunding platform, financing a new region's expansion from the enthusiasm of its own consumers rather than from a bank. Commentary and gatekeeping

The influence of a small number of critics rested on the economics of print: a handful of publications could reach everybody and nobody else could reach anybody. That fixed cost has gone, and with it the concentration. Vivino, CellarTracker and thousands of individual accounts have distributed the gatekeeping function to the point where no single voice moves a market as one once did.

Who wins: the aggregators themselves, and the producers who no longer need a critic's endorsement to be found.

## Conclusion

The industry is barbelling, not consolidating. It is fragmenting wherever technology has stripped out a fixed cost (production plant, distribution access, publishing, market research) and wherever value derives from being identifiable rather than reliable. It is consolidating wherever the opposite holds. The businesses succeeding at the fragmented end share one trait: they own a direct relationship with either the drinker or the specialist buyer, and therefore do not need the scale infrastructure that is consolidating. The businesses in real trouble are the mid-sized ones that need that infrastructure but are too small to command terms from it.
