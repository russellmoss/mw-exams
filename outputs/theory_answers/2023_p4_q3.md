## My brand, and the parameters I am setting

There is no correct answer without stated parameters, so I fix them first. My house sells around two million bottles a year at an average retail of roughly 50 euros, with 65 percent exported and the United States, Japan, the United Kingdom and Germany carrying most of that. The range is a non-vintage brut backbone at about 80 percent of volume, a vintage and a rosé, and a prestige cuvée of a few tens of thousands of bottles.

Positioning: an established house whose credibility rests on consistency and on the quality of its reserve wines, sold as a wine rather than as a celebration accessory. Proposition: *the Champagne that belongs on the table, not only in the ice bucket*. Priorities in order: defend price, shift mix upward, and recruit a younger consumer without discounting to do it.

Five million euros against two million bottles is 2.50 euros a bottle of brand investment. That figure disciplines everything below: it funds a serious, focused plan in four markets and it does not fund global television.

## What the industry picture obliges me to do

Each fact here matters only for the decision it forces.

The appellation's planted area is effectively capped, at roughly 34,000 hectares, and yield is set collectively each year. I therefore cannot grow by volume. Growth must come from price and mix, which makes premiumisation the plan's spine rather than a slogan.

Grapes are the largest single line in a Champagne house's cost base, and grape prices have risen. Margin protection therefore depends on holding price and lifting mix, not on selling more brut NV.

The region ships of the order of 300 million bottles a year, and the shift over the last decade has been value over volume, with growers and small houses capturing the authenticity narrative. My brand is exposed there: consistency reads as industrial to a sommelier. So the plan must supply provenance content — vineyard sourcing, reserve-wine library, the chef de cave — rather than lifestyle imagery.

Finally, the competitive set is no longer other Champagne. It is premium spirits, English sparkling and prestige Crémant at the low end of my price band, and it recruits younger drinkers better than we do. Hence a specific recruitment objective rather than a general awareness one, aimed at the 25 to 40 age group in gastronomy rather than in nightlife, because the drinking occasion I am defending is the meal.

I am also not funding volume defence. If a market wants my brut NV at a promotional price, that is a commercial decision funded elsewhere; my budget exists to make the promotion unnecessary.

## The three-year shape

**Year 1 — evidence and foundations.** Brand tracking baseline in the four core markets, a repositioning of the visual and verbal identity around the wine-not-occasion proposition, rebuilt owned assets, and the start of the sommelier programme.

**Year 2 — activation.** Full weight behind the United States and Japan, the two markets where prestige pricing is achievable and on-trade advocacy converts. Trade education scales; hospitality at the maison runs at capacity.

**Year 3 — mix.** Launch of a late-disgorged expression of the vintage at roughly twice the vintage price, using the reserve-wine story built in years one and two, plus a first serious move into direct-to-consumer for allocated cuvées.

## Where the five million goes

Annual allocation, each year, with phasing inside the lines:

- **On-trade and sommelier advocacy — 1.1m (22%).** Ambassadors in the US and Japan, by-the-glass and pairing programmes, competitions, trade tastings. My highest-return line because a sommelier recommendation carries price.
- **Partnership — 1.0m (20%).** One flagship property only, in gastronomy or classical music, held for the full three years. Multiple small sponsorships buy nothing.
- **Brand experience and hospitality — 0.9m (18%).** Hosting trade, press and top private clients at the house; the cellars are the most persuasive asset I own.
- **Digital, content and CRM — 0.85m (17%).** Chef de cave and vineyard content, search, targeted social, CRM build for year three's DTC move.
- **PR and press — 0.55m (11%).** Agency retainers in four markets, sample programme, critic and awards submissions.
- **Point of sale and visibility — 0.4m (8%).** Glassware, ice buckets, on-premise materials.
- **Research and measurement — 0.2m (4%).** Tracking, pricing studies, post-campaign evaluation.

No price promotion appears here; that sits in the commercial budget by the question's own terms, and my brand plan explicitly resists funding it.

## Measurement

Average realised price per bottle and mix by cuvée are the primary KPIs, because they are what premiumisation actually means. Behind them: prompted awareness and consideration among 25–40s in the core four, number and quality of on-premise placements, share of gastronomic lists, and CRM database size by year three.

## Conclusion

With a capped appellation I cannot buy volume, so the three years buy price. I spend the majority of five million euros on the people who recommend Champagne and on the place that proves it, concentrate on four markets, hold one partnership rather than ten, and end the period with a higher-priced cuvée and a direct relationship with the customers most likely to buy it.
