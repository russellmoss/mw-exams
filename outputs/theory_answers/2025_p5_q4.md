## What I mean by ethical, and by production

An ethical concern in this context is not simply something undesirable. I take it to mean a harm that is imposed by the way wine is made on a party who has little or no power to refuse it, and which the price of the wine does not compensate. That definition does three useful things: it separates ethics from mere sustainability accounting, it puts the powerlessness of the harmed party at the centre, and it gives me a basis for ranking rather than listing.

By production of wine I mean the vineyard, the cellar and the inbound supply chain that serves them — labour, land, water, agrochemicals, energy, packaging materials — up to the point the wine leaves the winery. I exclude the health consequences of drinking and the ethics of alcohol marketing. Both are serious, but they are ethics of consumption and of communication, and folding them in would let a producer's actual conduct disappear behind a debate about the product.

## How I rank them

Four criteria: the severity of harm to an identifiable person; the number of people affected; the urgency, meaning whether the harm is happening now or is a risk to the future; and whether the harmed party can exit or object. On those tests I rank labour first, agrochemical exposure second, water third, and systemic inequality fourth — though inequality is arguably the cause of the other three rather than a peer of them.

## First: labour

Wine is one of the most labour-intensive crops in commercial agriculture, and the work is seasonal, physically punishing and largely done by people with the least bargaining power in the economies concerned: migrant, undocumented or contracted-out. The harms are direct and current — underpayment, unlawful hours, unsafe or squalid housing, heat exposure, and in the worst cases trafficking. Recent prosecutions in Champagne over the housing and treatment of migrant harvest workers were significant precisely because they occurred in the most prestigious and best-capitalised appellation in the world, which disposes of the argument that this is a problem of poor regions.

It is not confined to Europe. South Africa's wine industry still carries the legacy of the dop system, in which farm labour was part-paid in wine, and its consequences for tenure and dependency have outlasted the practice. California's harvest workforce faces heat and wildfire smoke exposure with limited employment protection. Contract labour is the common mechanism everywhere: it places a layer between the estate and the worker, so the estate can hold a certification and still not know who picked its fruit.

This ranks first because the harm is to people, now, who cannot leave, and because it is the one concern where a producer's own decisions are decisive. There are counter-examples worth naming: New Zealand's Recognised Seasonal Employer scheme created a regulated route for Pacific Island workers, showing that the alternative to exploitation is administrative effort rather than higher prices alone.

## Second: agrochemical exposure

I treat agrochemicals as an ethical rather than an environmental question first, because the earliest and most severe harm falls on people — applicators, their families, and the neighbours of vineyards. Vines receive more fungicide than most crops because they are grown as monocultures in humid climates, and the exposure is chronic. A case in Bordeaux in which children at a school beside a vineyard were affected during spraying is the kind of event that turned this from a technical matter into a public one, and vineyard-boundary buffer rules in several countries followed.

It ranks second rather than first because protective equipment, timing rules and spray technology genuinely reduce the harm, whereas labour abuse requires a change of intent. But it carries a dilemma that deserves stating: copper is permitted in organic viticulture but capped in the EU because it accumulates permanently in soil. The organic grower avoids synthetic residues at the cost of a persistent heavy metal, and there is no clean answer available.

## Third: water

Water ranks third because the harm is severe and often irreversible, but it is concentrated in specific regions rather than universal. Where vineyards draw from over-allocated basins the wine is being made at the expense of the communities and ecosystems sharing the resource — California's groundwater legislation and the Murray-Darling basin are the clearest examples of the public catching up with the problem, and irrigation-dependent regions in Chile, Argentina and parts of Spain face the same arithmetic. The ethical sharpness comes from the fact that an export vineyard is exporting water in liquid form from a water-stressed country, and the people who lose access are not the people who drink the wine.

## Fourth: systemic inequality

This is the least discussed and, I would argue, the most structurally important. Three strands. Land ownership: in South Africa, Chile and much of the New World, vineyard land is held in patterns set by colonisation, and ownership rather than employment is where the value accrues. Supply-chain power: grape growers routinely sell below the cost of production because a co-operative, a négociant or a retailer sets the price, and no discussion of a grower's environmental practice is honest without noting who takes the margin. Access to sustainability itself: certification costs money and administrative capacity, so the smallholder in a developing region cannot afford the credential that would earn the premium, and the ethical badge concentrates among those who least needed it.

## Dilemmas, technology and unintended consequences

Nothing above has a costless solution, and the strongest signal of a serious answer is saying so.

Mechanisation removes workers from chemical exposure, heat and back injury, and removes their jobs in regions where vineyard work is the only employment; it is available in the Languedoc or inland Australia and impossible on the terraces of the Douro or the Mosel, so the regions with the least capacity to mechanise are the ones most dependent on manual labour. Precision spraying and sensor-based application reduce chemical volumes and require capital that excludes small growers. Lightweight glass cuts emissions and raises breakage. De-alcoholisation, promoted as a responsible product, is energy-intensive.

The point is that most ethical improvements in wine transfer the cost to someone, and the ethical question is usually who.

## Certification, transparency and greenwashing

Certification is the main mechanism the industry offers, and it is doing less work than it appears. Schemes have proliferated to the point of consumer meaninglessness, most audit practices rather than outcomes, and many are self-declared. Critically, the dominant schemes are environmental: a wine can be certified organic and have no labour standard attached at all. The exceptions prove the gap — WIETA in South Africa and Fairtrade certification exist precisely because the environmental schemes did not cover people. The greatest ethical risk here is substitution: a label that lets a producer, a retailer and a consumer all feel the matter has been dealt with.

## Conclusion

The major ethical concerns in wine production are, in order, the treatment of the people who do the work, the chemical exposure of those same people and their neighbours, the appropriation of water in stressed regions, and the ownership and power structures that determine who can afford to behave well. I would demote carbon emissions and packaging — real, but wine is a small contributor to a diffuse harm, and the industry's attention to it is partly because it is the easiest to measure and the least confrontational to fix. If one concern deserves the industry's next decade, it is labour: it is the most severe, the most immediate, the most within producers' control, and the one that certification currently does least to reach.
