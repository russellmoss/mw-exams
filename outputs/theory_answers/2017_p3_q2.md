## Scope

Two questions are being asked: is the wine in good condition, and is it legal to sell in the market it is going to? I answer them as a schedule of named analyses, each with the threshold that triggers a decision, split into pre-bottling condition and stability, pre-bottling legal limits, labelling conformity, and post-bottling verification. Analyses without thresholds are records, not quality control.

## Pre-bottling: condition and stability

**Composition.** Alcohol by densitometry or near-infrared, titratable acidity, pH, residual sugar as glucose plus fructose by enzymatic assay, and malic acid. Sugar and malic set the microbial risk: glucose plus fructose below the 0.1 g/L enzymatic detection limit and malic acid not detected (below 0.05 g/L) mean the wine cannot referment or complete a bottle malolactic, and can be filtered less tightly.

**Sulphur dioxide.** Free and total by aspiration or ripper, read against pH, because it is molecular SO2 that protects. A target of 0.5 to 0.6 mg/L molecular SO2 in a dry red requires roughly 25 to 30 mg/L free at pH 3.5, and much more at higher pH.

**Dissolved oxygen** in tank and at the filler, and total package oxygen on filled bottles, held below about 1 mg/L for an age-worthy white.

**Protein stability** by heat test at 80 degrees C for two hours, cooled for three hours at 20 degrees C, with a turbidity rise above about 2 NTU counting as a fail. **Tartrate stability** by conductivity or mini-contact test. **Turbidity** itself, below 1 NTU for a bright white. **Metals**: iron above roughly 7 mg/L risks ferric casse, as does copper, where the EU limit is 1 mg/L and anything above about 0.5 mg/L is a pro-oxidant; calcium is measured for calcium tartrate risk, which cold stabilisation does not fix.

**Microbiology.** Plating or membrane filtration for total viable yeast and bacteria, with PCR or selective media for *Brettanomyces*, and 4-ethylphenol to quantify damage already done. **Carbon dioxide** by carbonation meter, since a still wine above roughly 1 g/L spritzes and a Vinho Verde style depends on it. **Sensory assessment** by panel, which catches what no assay was set to find.

## Pre-bottling: the legal limits

Total SO2 against the EU ceilings of 150 mg/L for dry red and 200 mg/L for dry white and rose, rising through the sweeter categories to 400 mg/L for the sweetest categories; organic wine sits materially below the conventional figures. Preservatives and processing aids against their own limits: sorbic acid at 200 mg/L, ascorbic acid at 250 mg/L, dimethyl dicarbonate at 200 mg/L and lysozyme at 500 mg/L. Volatile acidity against the EU ceiling of roughly 1.2 g/L as acetic acid for red and 1.08 g/L for white. Methanol, capped at 250 mg/L in white and rose and 400 mg/L in red. Contaminants: ochratoxin A against the EU ceiling of 2 micrograms per kilogram, plus heavy metals and pesticide residue screens where the destination demands them. Allergen residues from egg, milk, casein and isinglass fining, which must be declared on the label unless analysis demonstrates residues below 0.25 mg/L.

## Labelling conformity

The analyses feed the label, so they must be done before artwork is signed off. Actual alcohol determines the declared strength; in the EU the tolerance is 0.5% abv (0.8% for sparkling, liqueur and overripe-grape wines), whereas the United States allows 1.5% for wines at or below 14% abv and 1.0% above it - a difference that matters for a single global label. Residual sugar determines the permitted sweetness term: in the EU, dry at or below 4 g/L, medium dry at or below 12 g/L, medium at or below 45 g/L, sweet above that, with the acidity derogation that lets a high-acid Riesling declare halbtrocken at or below 18 g/L. Sparkling wine has its own scale: brut nature below 3 g/L, extra brut at or below 6 g/L, brut below 12 g/L, and so on to doux. Total SO2 fixes the sulphite declaration, required once total SO2 exceeds 10 mg/L, and the fining residue analysis fixes the egg and milk declarations. Analytical certificates are also the basis of the export documentation many markets require before customs clearance.

## Post-bottling analyses

Post-bottling verification checks that the process, not the tank, was sound. Total package oxygen on bottles taken across the run, since pick-up happens at the filler. Sterility by incubating retained bottles at 25 to 30 degrees C for five to seven days and plating for growth, which detects a filler contamination the tank sample never would. Free and total SO2 again, to confirm the in-line dose landed. Turbidity and visual inspection against a light box. Net content by weight or level, against the average-quantity rules. Closure performance: extraction or unscrewing torque, and headspace oxygen ingress over time. Then a retained-sample programme, tasting and re-analysing bottles at intervals through the stated shelf life, which is the only way to know whether the SO2 regime and closure specification were right. Full batch traceability links every result to a pallet, so any later failure is contained rather than a whole-vintage recall.
