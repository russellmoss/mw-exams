## What proper nutrition means, and what I am leaving out

Proper vine nutrition is not maximum nutrition. It is supplying each element in the right proportion at the stage the vine needs it, so that the vine achieves moderate vigour, sets and ripens a balanced crop, and enters winter with adequate reserves. Excess is as much a failure as deficiency: over-supplied nitrogen produces vigour, shading, poor fruit set, botrytis and dilute fruit, while excess potassium raises must pH and costs the winemaker acidity.

The temptation with this question is to survey every element in turn, and I am deliberately not doing that. I take three steps in order — understand the soil as a delivery system, diagnose before intervening, and then intervene with the cheapest durable lever available — because that sequence is what separates growers who correct nutrition from growers who merely fertilise.

## The soil is the delivery system

Almost every nutritional problem is a problem of availability rather than of absolute quantity, and availability is set by three interacting processes.

Physically, the soil determines how much root there is. Depth, structure, drainage and the absence of compaction control root volume, oxygen supply and water movement, and a vine on a compacted headland will show deficiency symptoms in a soil that analyses as fully adequate. Water is the carrier: dry soil is nutritionally unavailable soil regardless of what it contains, which is why irrigation scheduling is a nutrition decision as much as a water one.

Chemically, pH governs the availability of nearly everything. High-pH calcareous soils immobilise iron and induce chlorosis; acidic soils release aluminium and manganese to toxic levels and lock up phosphorus and molybdenum. Cation exchange capacity determines how much a soil can hold against leaching — a sandy soil cannot store an annual application, so the same total dose must be split.

Biologically, mycorrhizal associations extend the effective root system and are the principal route for phosphorus uptake; organic matter mineralisation supplies most of a vineyard's nitrogen; and soil fauna maintain the structure the physical side depends on. The point that matters is that these three interact, so a chemical correction applied to a compacted or biologically dead soil will not deliver.

## Diagnose before intervening

The step most often skipped is measurement. A full soil analysis before planting and at intervals thereafter establishes pH, organic matter, texture and base saturation. Petiole or leaf-blade sampling at flowering and again at veraison is the more useful annual tool, because it measures what the vine has actually taken up rather than what the soil holds — which is precisely the distinction that availability makes important. Visual symptom diagnosis is fast but unreliable, since deficiencies mimic each other and mimic virus. Must analysis at harvest, particularly yeast assimilable nitrogen, closes the loop retrospectively and tells the grower whether the vineyard programme worked.

Modern technology mostly serves targeting. Aerial and satellite infrared imagery maps vigour variation across a block so that sampling is directed at the anomalous zones rather than averaged into meaninglessness; soil conductivity mapping does the same for texture and depth; neutron and capacitance probes track soil moisture through the profile, which matters because uptake follows water. The gain is not new information about the vine so much as the ability to manage a block in zones instead of as a unit.

## The interventions

Structural corrections come first because they are made once and last for decades. Lime raises pH on acid soils, gypsum addresses sodicity and structure, deep ripping and drainage restore root volume. Where soil pH is high and calcareous, the decisive step is rootstock: rootstocks selected for lime tolerance, such as 41B and Fercal, prevent lime-induced chlorosis in a way no annual foliar programme can match, and that choice is made once at planting.

Annual supply then follows. Mineral fertiliser applied to the soil is precise, fast and easy to calculate against yield removal. Fertigation is more precise still where irrigation exists, allowing small doses timed to demand at flowering and post-set, and minimising leaching losses. Compost, manure and legume cover crops build organic matter, feed the biology and release nitrogen slowly, but they are imprecise and slow to act. Foliar application is the tool for rapid correction of trace elements — zinc, boron, magnesium — where soil chemistry prevents root uptake.

## Choosing between routes

Different approaches work, and the choice is agronomic rather than ideological. A dry-farmed, low-vigour hillside with no irrigation cannot fertigate and must build fertility through organic matter and mulch; a high-yielding irrigated block can dose precisely and should. A vigorous, nitrogen-rich site needs the opposite intervention entirely — under-vine or inter-row vegetation to compete for water and nitrogen and bring the canopy back into balance. To claim that only composting or only mineral fertiliser is correct is to mistake a site-specific decision for a principle.

## Maintaining it

Maintenance is a cycle rather than an event: sample annually at the same phenological stage, keep records long enough to see trends rather than noise, match inputs to the nutrients removed in fruit and prunings, and re-test after any structural change.

Ranked by return, the highest-value steps are the durable ones — drainage, pH correction and rootstock choice — followed by a genuine diagnostic programme. The annual fertiliser decision, which is where most attention goes, matters least of the three.
