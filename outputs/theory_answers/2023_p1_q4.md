## What is actually in short supply

Nitrogen deficiency is a shortfall of *plant-available* nitrogen — nitrate and ammonium in the root zone, taken up when the vine needs it — not a shortage of total soil nitrogen. The great majority of soil nitrogen is locked in organic matter and becomes available only as microbes mineralise it. That distinction drives everything below: most deficiency is a failure of release, placement or uptake rather than of stock.

## Causes

**Low organic matter.** Since mineralisation is the main supply, soils with little organic matter are structurally deficient: sandy, long-cultivated or eroded soils, and hot climates where organic matter oxidises rapidly. Australia's Riverland and California's Central Valley illustrate the type; so do the eroded slate soils of the Mosel, where topsoil has to be carried back up the slope.

**Mineralisation conditions.** Microbial release requires warmth, moisture and aeration together. A cold, wet spring in Champagne or the Rheingau suppresses mineralisation exactly when the vine's demand peaks at flowering, producing a transient deficiency on soils that test well. The mirror case is a Mediterranean summer: dry soil stops both mineralisation and mass-flow transport, so a Priorat or Douro block can be nitrogen-starved in the presence of adequate reserves. That is a water problem presenting as a nutrition problem, and treating it with fertiliser wastes money.

**Soil pH.** Strongly acidic soils suppress nitrifying bacteria and slow mineralisation, and are common under long-term ammonium fertiliser use and high-rainfall leaching. Highly calcareous, alkaline soils promote volatilisation of surface-applied ammonium and interact with induced deficiencies of other elements.

**Leaching.** Nitrate is anionic, mobile and poorly retained; free-draining soils under heavy rain or excessive irrigation lose it below the root zone. Marlborough's stony Wairau soils and the Médoc's gravels are the standard cases, as is any drip block over-irrigated on sand.

**Competition and immobilisation.** Permanent grass sward is a strong competitor for nitrogen — which is precisely why it is planted in Bordeaux and Coonawarra to devigorate — and where it was installed years earlier and never reassessed, deliberate devigoration slides into deficiency. Weeds, adjacent trees, and high carbon-to-nitrogen mulches such as straw or woodchip immobilise nitrogen as they decompose.

**Vine factors.** Rootstock alters uptake capacity: low-vigour Riparia Gloire, 101-14 and 420A extract less than 1103P, 140Ru or Ramsey on the same soil. High crop load partitions nitrogen to fruit at the expense of reserves, so an over-cropped vine becomes deficient the following season. Varieties with high demand, notably Sauvignon Blanc, show it soonest.

## Effects

**Vine growth.** Reduced shoot growth, short internodes, thin canes, and small pale leaves — chlorosis appearing on older leaves first, since nitrogen is mobile and remobilised to the growing tips — with early leaf fall. Leaf area falls, so photosynthetic capacity falls, and the vine cannot fill both the crop and its reserves. Low reserves in the permanent wood mean weak budburst and reduced fruitfulness next spring, so the deficiency compounds.

**Quantity.** Poorer set, fewer and smaller bunches, smaller berries, and over successive seasons a declining yield trend.

**Quality — and it is not simply negative.** Mild deficiency is a quality tool: less vigour, an open canopy, better light in the fruit zone, smaller berries with a higher skin-to-pulp ratio, better colour and phenolic ripeness, lower pyrazine and lower botrytis risk. Severe deficiency reverses all of it. Limited leaf area delays and truncates ripening, so sugar stalls, seed tannin stays green, herbaceous character persists in reds, and aroma precursors — thiol precursors in Sauvignon Blanc above all — are reduced.

**Through to fermentation.** Low vineyard nitrogen produces low yeast assimilable nitrogen in the juice. Below a commonly cited threshold, a yeast assimilable nitrogen figure of the order of 140 mg per litre, fermentations turn sluggish or stick; stressed yeast produce hydrogen sulphide and mercaptans, volatile acidity rises, and desirable ester formation falls. The vineyard decision therefore reaches the finished wine.

## Remedies

Diagnose before acting: petiole or blade analysis at flowering and veraison, soil analysis including pH and organic matter, and juice YAN at harvest, read together to separate true deficiency from an uptake failure caused by dry soil.

Then correct the specific cause. Build the stock with compost and manure and reduce cultivation. Change the competition: legume cover crops — vetch, clover, faba bean — mown and dropped or incorporated, or a cleared under-vine strip. Correct pH by liming so that mineralisation can proceed. Time fertiliser to demand in split applications post-flowering and post-harvest rather than one large pre-budburst dose, and fertigate to place nitrogen in the wetted zone on sandy soils where leaching is the cause. Where dry soil is blocking uptake, irrigate. Foliar urea before harvest is the targeted tool for lifting juice YAN without adding vigour. At replanting, choose a more extractive rootstock; in the meantime, moderate crop load. Winery addition of DAP is a legitimate backstop but corrects the symptom only, and is constrained under organic rules.

## Conclusion

Nitrogen deficiency is rarely an absence of nitrogen. It is a failure of mineralisation, of retention, or of uptake, and each has a different and non-interchangeable remedy. The grower's task is to establish which, because the wrong correction is both expensive and ineffective.
