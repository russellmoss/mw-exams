## What I mean by artificial intelligence

I use "artificial intelligence" to mean systems that perform tasks we would normally expect to require human intelligence: learning patterns from data rather than following written rules, interpreting complex or ambiguous inputs, integrating several sources into a decision, and adapting to conditions they were not explicitly programmed for.

That excludes a great deal of technology the industry loosely calls smart. A tank temperature probe is a sensor; a mechanical harvester with a driver is automation; a spreadsheet with a formula is neither. Sensing measures, automation executes, AI decides. Exactly where the line falls is genuinely contested and I do not propose to adjudicate it; the commercially useful test is simply whether the system has taken over a judgement a person used to make.

## In the vineyard

This is where adoption is furthest ahead, because the vineyard generates the data and carries the cost.

**Interpretation of remote sensing.** Multispectral and thermal imagery from satellites and drones is now routinely interpreted by trained models to map vigour and estimate vine water status, driving variable-rate irrigation and selective picking. The value is greatest where water is scarce and metered — California's Central Valley, Mendoza, inland Australia, increasingly the Douro.

**Disease prediction.** Models combining local weather, canopy imagery and historical incidence forecast downy and powdery mildew and botrytis pressure, moving spraying from a calendar to a risk basis. Camera-equipped robots patrolling rows to detect disease and vine mortality are the field version of the same idea.

**Yield estimation.** Machine vision counting inflorescences and bunches replaces manual sampling and extrapolation. For a cooperative or a large brand, an accurate early yield number is worth more than most cellar improvements.

**Autonomous machinery.** Naio Technologies' autonomous weeding machines working in French vineyards, and driver-optional electric tractors of the type Monarch has trialled with California growers, are already commercial. Robotic pruning remains the hard problem, because pruning requires reading an individual vine's architecture and making a judgement — an AI task rather than an automation one — and it is also the largest remaining skilled labour cost in the vineyard.

## In the winery

**Fermentation management.** Systems that read density, temperature and nitrogen streams and predict stuck fermentations or volatile acidity risk are moving from reporting to deciding — setting pumpover frequency and cooling regimes within limits the winemaker sets.

**Sorting.** Optical sorters classifying berries and MOG by image are the most mature machine-vision application in wine and are now unremarkable in large wineries.

**Analysis.** Chemometric models interpret spectra to predict phenolic ripeness or flag taint. Smoke-taint prediction has become the pressing case in Australia and the US West Coast, where the commercial question — pick, declassify or abandon — has to be answered within days.

**Blending.** Optimising many lots against a target specification and a cost ceiling is a combinatorial problem at which machines genuinely outperform people, and it is the first place the judgement itself, not the information, gets handed over.

## Through logistics and the market

Demand forecasting, dynamic pricing, allocation and route optimisation are standard in any consumer-goods business and are arriving in wine through the large retailers first. Container monitoring with anomaly detection catches temperature excursions in transit. In the fine-wine market, price modelling and image-based authentication of labels, capsules and glass offer a real answer to counterfeiting, which manual expertise has never scaled to.

On the consumer side the application is already dominant. Vivino, whose label-recognition app has assembled probably the largest consumer wine dataset in existence, recommends by inferred taste rather than by score. Virtual sommeliers and chatbots handle first-line advice for retailers. Firms such as Tastry in California model a wine's measured chemistry against consumer preference panels and sell the output to producers and buyers — which means AI is already influencing what gets blended and what gets listed.

## Impacts

**Financial.** AI needs data density and capital, so it is scale-biased. A 500-hectare operation amortises an autonomous machine; a four-hectare Mosel grower cannot. The likely consequence is further consolidation, mitigated — importantly — by contractors and cooperatives becoming the delivery mechanism for small growers, as they already are for harvesting. The second financial issue is ownership: the agronomic data generated on a grower's land is captured by the platform or equipment vendor, and whoever holds the multi-year dataset holds the pricing power. Broadacre agriculture is fighting that battle now and wine will inherit it.

**Social and labour.** Labour is the honest motivation behind most vineyard robotics. Border closures in 2020 and 2021 left the UK, New Zealand and Australia short of pickers and cellar hands; rural populations across southern Europe are ageing; California competes for the same workers as construction. Unlike earlier mechanisation, AI-driven machines can in principle work where a machine harvester cannot — selective picking, steep sites, bush vines — so this is substitution, not merely assistance. The cost is that vineyard employment is the economic base of many rural communities, and the regions whose reputation rests on landscape and human craft are precisely those that lose most if the people leave. There is an unresolved tension between what the industry sells and what it is automating.

**Ethical.** Three concern me. Homogenisation: if many producers optimise blends against similar preference models trained on similar panels, stylistic diversity narrows, and this risk arrives with commercial success rather than with failure. Disclosure: there is no convention requiring a producer marketing a wine as handcrafted to say that its blend was machine-selected. Discovery bias: recommendation engines reward what already sells, which disadvantages the small and unknown producers the digital channel is assumed to help.

**Environmental.** This is the least-discussed motivation and possibly the strongest. Precision irrigation cuts water use where water is the binding constraint on viticulture's future. Risk-based spraying reduces passes, and fewer passes mean less diesel, less compaction, less copper and less synthetic fungicide. Electric driver-optional machines take diesel out of the row. Supply-chain optimisation reduces empty running and refrigeration load. Suitability modelling supports variety and site decisions decades ahead, and early frost and heat warnings allow action rather than post-mortem. Against this, training and running models consumes energy and the machines themselves have an embodied footprint — but in water and agrochemical terms the net effect is clearly positive.

## The coming decades

Within this decade, sensing and decision support become normal on large estates, and fermentation modelling and optical sorting standard in large wineries. Next, autonomous machinery reaches economic parity through contractors; robotic pruning is the milestone to watch, because it is the point at which the vineyard's skilled labour bill collapses. Later, preference modelling begins to influence what is planted, not only what is blended.

There are limits worth stating. Appellation law constrains adoption directly — irrigation is restricted or banned in much of Europe, so the flagship application is unavailable there. Fine wine's value rests on human authorship and scarcity, which no producer will willingly dilute. And steep-slope viticulture will stay manual longer than the technologists predict.

## Conclusion

AI's greatest impact will be felt in the vineyard and in demand-side matching, not in the cellar, and its most valuable contribution will be environmental. But it is a consolidating technology: it rewards scale, capital and whoever owns the data. The industry's task over the coming decades is not to adopt it, which will happen regardless, but to decide who owns the vineyard's data, what happens to the rural employment it displaces, and how much stylistic diversity it is willing to trade for predicted consumer approval.
