## Scope: carbon neutrality is not sustainability

Carbon neutral means net zero carbon dioxide equivalent across a defined boundary, achieved by reducing emissions as far as practicable and neutralising the remainder. It is a narrower objective than sustainability: water use, biodiversity, soil health and social conditions are all legitimate goals and none of them is what I have been asked to deliver. Some of them trade against carbon — a lightweight bottle is better for carbon and worse for perceived quality; organic conversion may raise tractor passes and therefore diesel. I will say where those tensions arise, but I will not write a sustainability plan.

I also define the boundary at the outset, using the GHG Protocol scopes. Scope 1 is what we burn and emit directly: tractor and truck diesel, boilers, refrigerant leakage, fermentation CO2. Scope 2 is purchased electricity. Scope 3 is everything upstream and downstream: glass, closures, cartons, bought-in grapes and bulk wine, freight, employee travel, and end-of-life packaging. A carbon-neutral claim that covers only Scopes 1 and 2 is not credible in wine, because most of our footprint is Scope 3.

## Step 1: measure before acting

Nothing can be planned until the baseline exists. I would commission a full inventory to the GHG Protocol using the international wine industry greenhouse gas accounting tool developed jointly by the Californian, Australian, New Zealand and South African industry bodies, so the result is comparable with peers rather than bespoke. I would have it verified by a third party — membership of International Wineries for Climate Action requires independently verified inventories, and that external discipline is what stops the exercise becoming marketing.

The output must be expressed per unit as well as in total: kilograms of CO2e per 75cl bottle shipped, and per case. Total emissions fall when you sell less wine, which is not an achievement; the intensity metric is what management should be held to, with the absolute figure reported alongside it.

## Step 2: where the emissions actually are, and therefore what to attack first

At one million cases — twelve million bottles a year — the ranking is fairly predictable and it should drive everything. Across published winery inventories, packaging dominates, and within packaging glass is typically the single largest line, of the order of a third to a half of the total, because glass furnaces run at high temperature on gas. Distribution is generally second, electricity for refrigeration third, and the vineyard, despite being where most of the visible activity happens, is usually a modest share.

That ordering is the analysis the question wants. If glass and freight are more than half the footprint, then a programme that starts with cover crops and solar panels is well-intentioned and arithmetically incapable of reaching the target.

## Step 3: the plan, in production chronology

**Vineyard.** Reduce tractor passes by combining operations and moving to lighter machinery; trial electric or hybrid tractors as they become commercially viable. Cut synthetic nitrogen to the measured requirement — nitrous oxide is a greenhouse gas of the order of 265 times more potent than carbon dioxide over a century, so fertiliser discipline outperforms most other vineyard measures. Cover cropping, compost and reduced tillage sequester carbon in soil, and I would claim that conservatively: the gains are real but slow, hard to measure and reversible.

**Winery.** Refrigeration is the dominant electrical load. Insulate tanks and the barrel hall, recover heat, fit variable-speed drives, and cool at night. Replace chilled cold stabilisation with electrodialysis or with CMC or potassium polyaspartate additions, which removes a very large refrigeration load outright. Fix refrigerant leakage and move to ammonia or CO2 systems, since F-gas leaks are a Scope 1 line most wineries never count. Install on-site solar to cover the daytime load and contract a certified renewable supply for the remainder.

**Packaging — the biggest single lever.** Lightweight the standard bottle. Moving a standard bottle from around 550g to around 400g across twelve million bottles removes of the order of 1,800 tonnes of glass a year, with a proportionate cut in furnace emissions and in freight weight. Require higher recycled cullet content from suppliers and engage them on their own furnace decarbonisation. Move a defined share of volume out of glass altogether: bag-in-box and cans for everyday tiers, kegs for on-trade accounts.

**Distribution.** Ship in bulk and bottle in market wherever the brand allows: a flexitank moves around 24,000 litres in a container against roughly 9,000 litres shipped in bottle. Sea freight always, never air. Optimise pallet configurations and consolidate loads.

## Step 4: sequencing and governance

I would rank every intervention on tonnes of CO2e abated per pound of capital, build a phased plan against it, and re-measure annually with third-party verification. Supplier contracts get carbon clauses, since Scope 3 is delivered by other people's factories.

## Step 5: offsetting the residual

Offsetting comes last, deliberately. Some emissions — freight, glass melting, harvest diesel — cannot be eliminated this decade, and the residual must be neutralised to claim neutrality. I would buy only verified removals rather than avoidance credits, under Gold Standard or Verra, with demonstrable additionality and permanence, and prefer insetting: sequestration in our own vineyards and land, which is auditable and on our balance sheet. The offset line should shrink every year. An offset budget that stays flat is evidence the reduction programme has failed.
