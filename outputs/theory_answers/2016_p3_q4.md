## a. Pinking in a white wine made for drinking early

*Mechanism.* Pinking is a salmon or onion-skin blush that appears in white wines made from certain varieties — Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, Riesling, Pinot Gris, Sémillon and Grenache Blanc among them. It is important to get the causation the right way round: pinking is a consequence of protective, reductive handling, not of oxidative handling. Colourless phenolic precursors related to the anthocyanidins survive a strictly anaerobic vinification, and then colour up when the finished wine meets a small amount of oxygen — at bottling, or on the shelf. A wine handled oxidatively at the juice stage has already lost those precursors to polymerisation and settling, and does not pink.

*Prevention.* The primary lever is therefore juice-stage oxygen. Deliberate must oxidation or full hyperoxidation on a susceptible variety precipitates the precursors before fermentation and is the most reliable prevention available. Beyond that: maintain adequate free SO2 after fermentation to keep the precursor reduced, avoid ascorbic acid, which is a documented aggravating factor, minimise oxygen pick-up at bottling, and screen the wine before bottling with a pinking susceptibility test, in which a small hydrogen peroxide addition is followed by an absorbance reading at 520 nm.

*Correction.* PVPP is the specific tool — it binds the low-molecular-weight phenolics responsible — at doses of the order of 10 to 50 g/hL, established by bench trial. Casein and activated charcoal also work, charcoal at real cost to aroma. Blending with an unaffected lot is the cheap answer where volumes allow.

*Verdict.* Prevention is cheap and correction is effective, so this is the least serious of the three problems. Because the wine is made for early drinking, the small aromatic loss from PVPP is a fair price and there is no ageing requirement to protect. My judgement is that the essential discipline is the pre-bottling test: an untested susceptible white is the one that pinks in the trade.

## b. Phenolic bitterness in a red wine

*Mechanism.* Bitterness is a taste, distinct from the tactile astringency with which candidates often conflate it, and it comes principally from low-molecular-weight phenolics — flavan-3-ol monomers such as catechin and their small oligomers. The sources are seeds, stems, unripe skins and hydrolysable tannins from new or poorly seasoned oak. High alcohol and low residual sugar sharpen the perception.

*Prevention.* Everything here is a vinification decision. Harvest at phenolic rather than merely sugar ripeness; destem where stems are green; avoid extracting seed tannin late in fermentation when alcohol is high, and use délestage to remove seeds from the vat; keep fermentation temperature and cap work proportionate to the fruit; press before dryness and keep hard press fractions separate; and choose oak by seasoning and toast rather than by newness alone.

*Correction.* Fining is the main route, and selectivity matters: PVPP targets exactly the small bitter phenolics; gelatin binds larger, astringent tannins and strips colour with them; egg albumen is the gentlest polish for a fine red; casein, and pea or potato protein for vegan specifications, sit between. Every one of these requires a bench trial, because over-fining removes colour and mid-palate and cannot be reversed. Micro-oxygenation and extended barrel and lees ageing help by polymerising phenolics into larger, less bitter forms. Gum arabic and a few grams of residual sugar mask rather than fix. Blending is usually the most efficient remedy of all.

*Verdict.* Correction is genuinely limited here — every subtractive treatment takes something else with it — so prevention carries far more weight than in part a. If the wine is already bitter I would blend first, fine with PVPP second, and accept a repositioned wine rather than fine it into hollowness.

## c. High volatile acidity in a full-bodied red

*Mechanism.* VA is chiefly acetic acid, produced by acetic acid bacteria acting on ethanol in the presence of oxygen, and by lactic acid bacteria on residual sugar; the associated ethyl acetate, which becomes obtrusive from roughly 150 mg/L, is what makes the fault smell of solvent. The sensory threshold in a full-bodied red is around 0.7 to 0.9 g/L, and the legal stop is the EU ceiling of 1.2 g/L expressed as acetic acid. Full-bodied reds are the vulnerable case: high pH, high alcohol, long maceration and years in barrel.

*Prevention.* Sound, unsplit fruit; rapid processing; adequate yeast assimilable nitrogen so fermentation finishes cleanly; never a dry, exposed cap; molecular SO2 at 0.5 to 0.8 mg/L maintained after malolactic fermentation, which is the moment of maximum vulnerability; disciplined barrel topping and barrel hygiene; cool cellars; and routine VA analysis so a rise is caught while the wine is still blendable.

*Correction.* Genuinely poor. The bacteria must first be removed by filtration and SO2, or the VA simply returns. Blending down against a low-VA lot is the practical answer and the arithmetic is simple. Reverse osmosis coupled with an anion-exchange resin does remove acetic acid, but it is costly and its permissibility varies by jurisdiction.

*Verdict.* This is the most serious of the three, because it is progressive and barely reversible. Prevention is effectively the only real answer, and a modest VA that lifts a big red is a judgement, not a licence.
