## Scope and the shape of the decision

Pre-bottling treatment means everything done to the wine in bulk after maturation and before it is packaged: fining, protein and tartrate stabilisation, filtration, preservative adjustment, gas management and the choice of package itself, since the package sets the oxygen budget the wine must survive. In each of the four cases below the same question is asked — what instability must be removed, what is the minimum intervention that removes it, and what constraint (certification, style, cost) forbids the obvious route. Packaging is treated as part of the answer throughout, not just glass.

## a) A vegan wine

The governing constraint is that no product of animal origin may be used, including as a processing aid that is filtered out and does not appear in the finished wine. That rules out isinglass, gelatine, casein and potassium caseinate, egg albumen, and — a point often missed — lysozyme, which is egg-derived, and chitosan sourced from crustacean shell rather than from *Aspergillus niger*.

The practical decisions are therefore substitutions. Protein stability is unaffected, since bentonite is a clay: dose to the heat-test minimum, often 20-60 g/hL, determined by bench trial rather than habit. For clarification and tannin softening in reds, pea or potato protein at roughly 10-30 g/hL replaces gelatine, though it is a gentler agent and may need a longer settling time or a crossflow pass behind it. For browning and phenolic pinking in whites, PVPP - trialled anywhere from 10 to 80 g/hL, within the OIV ceiling of 80 g/hL - is synthetic and compliant. Where fining is only about clarity, filtration can substitute entirely.

Beyond the wine, the QA burden is documentary: written declarations from suppliers for every aid, filter media and fining agent, plus checks on adhesives, capsules and any beeswax closure treatment, since certification bodies audit the whole package.

## b) An orange wine

Here the constraints are stylistic rather than legal, and the answer is largely about what not to do. Extended skin contact leaves a white wine with red-wine phenolic structure, so protein instability is usually already resolved by tannin-protein binding — run a heat test and expect to skip bentonite, which would strip texture. The wine is oxidatively robust for the same reason, so oxygen tolerance during transfer is high.

Two real risks replace those. First, skin contact extracts potassium and pushes pH up, so a molecular SO2 of 0.5-0.8 mg/L that needs only 25-40 mg/L free at pH 3.5 needs 50-80 mg/L at pH 3.8 - an uncomfortable figure for a category whose buyers expect low sulphur. Second, these wines commonly go through spontaneous fermentation and long unsulphured elevage, so *Brettanomyces*, acetic acid bacteria and residual sugar with viable yeast are live threats.

The decision is where on the intervention scale to sit. If the wine is to be sold unfined and unfiltered, as most are, that must be earned by clean racking, low viable counts confirmed by plating, cold storage and controlled shipping, not assumed. If filtration is used, a coarse 1.0 micrometre pass preserves texture where a 0.45 micrometre membrane would flatten it. Tartrate stabilisation is usually declined outright, the category tolerating crystals and sediment. Packaging follows: dark glass and a closure with modest oxygen transmission.

## c) An organic wine

Organic wine is regulated, and the regulations differ by market — the first consideration is which market's rules apply. In the EU, the reference is a positive list of permitted oenological practices with lower SO2 ceilings: EU organic caps total SO2 at 100 mg/L for dry red and 150 mg/L for dry white and rose, roughly 50 below conventional. Several stabilisation routes are excluded, notably electrodialysis and cation exchange for tartrate stability, dealcoholisation, physical SO2 removal, filtration below about 0.2 micrometres and heat treatment above 75 degrees C. Cold stabilisation by traditional chilling, gum arabic or metatartaric acid remains available.

The US is not equivalent. The US National Organic Program allows no added sulphites in wine labelled organic, with total below 10 mg/L; the workable export category is "made with organic grapes", which permits up to 100 mg/L. A single bottling cannot easily satisfy both, so the label claim must be fixed before the treatment plan is written.

Practically, the lower SO2 ceiling forces cleaner microbiology: complete malolactic fermentation, malic below 0.2 g/L, tight filtration within the permitted pore size, and organic-certified fining agents.

## d) A mass-market, inexpensive wine

The temptation is to say such a wine should be heavily fined and heavily treated. That is wrong, and it is expensive. Balanced winemaking with modest, trial-determined additions produces a better entry-level wine than blanket dosing, and every gram of bentonite costs money, occupies tank time and generates lees loss.

What genuinely differs is the risk profile. The wine will sit warm on a supermarket shelf for a year with no owner, so it must be absolutely stable: heat-stable to the bench minimum, cold-stable via CMC at 5-10 g/hL or metatartaric acid at around 10 g/hL for fast-turn stock rather than energy-hungry chilling, sterile-filtered through a 0.45 micrometre membrane where there is residual sugar, and carrying free SO2 at bottling nearer 35-40 mg/L than the fine-wine norm.

Package choice is a pre-bottling decision, not an afterthought: bag-in-box, PET and can all admit more oxygen than glass, so each demands a higher dissolved-oxygen discipline at fill and a shorter declared shelf life. Consistency across very large blends, and throughput on the line, complete the picture.
