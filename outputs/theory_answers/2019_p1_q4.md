## The two organisms, and why the distinction drives strategy

*Plasmopara viticola* (downy mildew) and *Erysiphe necator* (powdery mildew) are the two that matter, and they behave differently enough that a single programme fails against both.

**Downy** is an oomycete, not a true fungus. It overwinters as oospores in leaf litter on the vineyard floor, needs free water for infection, and spreads by rain splash and warm humid nights. It attacks green tissue systemically — oil spots on leaves, white sporulation on the underside, brown shrivelled bunches — and its damage is worst in wet, warm springs: the Atlantic and continental west of Europe, and any humid summer-rainfall region.

**Powdery** overwinters as mycelium inside dormant buds and as cleistothecia in bark, needs no free water, and thrives in warm, dry, shaded conditions — which is why Mediterranean and Californian growers fight it in years when downy is absent. It is superficial rather than systemic, but it scars berries, splits skins, opens the door to *Botrytis* and sour rot, and at low visual incidence still puts an unmistakable taint into the wine.

*Botrytis* and *Phomopsis* interact with both and share several of the same controls — canopy openness, hygiene, timely sprays — but they are secondary here and I will treat them only where they bear on the mildew programme.

## Plant material and site

The most durable reduction in risk is genetic. Vitis vinifera has essentially no resistance because it never co-evolved with either pathogen; the American species do. The modern PIWI crosses — Regent, Solaris, Cabernet Blanc, Souvignier Gris, and the INRAE ResDur series such as Voltis and Artaban — carry stacked resistance genes from those species and can cut spray numbers dramatically. Their limits are commercial and regulatory: appellation rules exclude them in most classic regions, market acceptance is slow, and single-gene resistance breaks down, which is why stacked resistances and continued low-rate spraying are recommended even in PIWI blocks.

Within vinifera, susceptibility varies and can be planned for: thin-skinned, tight-bunched varieties such as Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Carignan are more exposed than loose-clustered ones, and loose-cluster clones reduce risk directly. Rootstock and nutrition matter indirectly, since a devigorated vine makes a more open canopy.

## Canopy, floor and hygiene

Canopy management is the cheapest continuous control. Shoot thinning to an appropriate density, shoot positioning, trimming and leaf removal in the fruit zone shortly after fruit set do three things at once: they dry the microclimate, they expose powdery mildew to direct ultraviolet light which it cannot tolerate, and they let spray reach the target — poor coverage is behind a large share of programme failures. Higher trunks and open training lift fruit away from the soil-borne downy inoculum.

Hygiene: shred and incorporate prunings, remove mummified bunches and infected leaf litter which carry the overwintering forms, control weeds and suckers that provide a green bridge, and clean machinery moving between blocks.

Vigour control supports all of this — restrained nitrogen, a competitive cover crop, deficit irrigation where legal — because a dense, lush canopy is the single best predictor of mildew pressure.

## Forecasting and the critical timing of treatments

Sprays are only as good as their timing. Weather-station networks with in-canopy leaf wetness and humidity sensors, feeding models such as the classic downy mildew risk rules and modern decision-support platforms, tell the manager when an infection event has occurred and when the incubation window closes. That converts a calendar programme into a targeted one, typically saving passes in a dry year and, more importantly, catching the one infection event a calendar programme misses.

The critical window for both is from shortly before flowering to bunch closure, when inflorescences and young berries are most susceptible; powdery in particular must be controlled pre-flowering, because once berries reach a few millimetres they gain ontogenic resistance and late sprays are wasted.

## Modes of action, and resistance management

This is where good programmes are distinguished. **Contact (protectant)** materials — copper, sulphur, folpet, mancozeb — sit on the surface, are washed off by rain and must be reapplied after growth flushes. **Local systemic (translaminar)** materials move through the leaf and give short kick-back. **Fully systemic** materials — phosphonates, and the sterol-inhibitor and QoI classes — move in the plant and offer curative action, but they are single-site and therefore resistance-prone; powdery mildew has already developed field resistance to several. So the programme must rotate FRAC groups, avoid consecutive single-site applications, keep multi-site contacts as the backbone, and never rely on curative sprays as a routine.

## Organic and biodynamic strategies, and the copper problem

Organic and biodynamic growers control powdery adequately with wettable sulphur, and biodynamic practice adds horn silica, plant teas of horsetail and nettle, and whey or bicarbonate products. Downy is the hard case: **there is no easy organic or biodynamic substitute for copper**, which is why it remains approved despite accumulating in soil and being toxic to earthworms — the EU has cut the permitted copper rate to 4 kg per hectare per year, averaged over seven years, which makes precision timing and forecasting not optional but essential. Potassium bicarbonate, plant extracts, chitosan and biocontrol agents such as *Bacillus* and *Aureobasidium* strains supplement copper; none replaces it.

## Conclusion

Risk is reduced by layering: resistant or loose-clustered plant material, an open devigorated canopy, clean floor and equipment, model-driven timing, and a rotated programme mixing multi-site contacts with sparing systemic use. A manager relying on any one layer — most commonly the spray tank — will eventually meet the season, or the resistant strain, that defeats it.
