## Who counts as an expert

The word has to be opened up before the question can be answered, because most people hearing it think of a critic, and critics are a small and shrinking part of the answer.

I distinguish three families:

**Production experts** - viticulturists, winemakers, oenologists, consulting winemakers, laboratory and quality-control staff, coopers, nurserymen. They determine whether the wine in the bottle is sound and whether it is any good.

**Intermediation experts** - buyers, importers, retailers, sommeliers, brokers, auctioneers, authenticators, logistics and storage specialists. They choose what exists in a market, at what price, in what condition.

**Communication experts** - critics, journalists, educators, Masters of Wine, Master Sommeliers, wine schools, and now influencers and platform curators. They translate and recommend.

These correspond to four different kinds of expertise, and the distinction matters: **technical** expertise (making a sound wine), **selection** expertise (choosing well on someone's behalf), **provenance** expertise (verifying authenticity and condition), and **translation** expertise (explaining and teaching). A consumer may need one and not another, and the answer to the question is different for each.

## Which consumers

"Consumers" is equally undifferentiated, and I segment on engagement first, then cross it with the demographic axes:

1. **The non-engaged majority.** Buy wine occasionally, choose on price, colour, brand and offer, and cannot name a region. This is most of the market everywhere.
2. **The exploring middle.** Interested, buying above entry price, willing to be guided, but with no vocabulary.
3. **The enthusiast.** Reads, tastes, has preferences, often disagrees with critics.
4. **The collector and investor.** Buying for cellar and resale.

Across these run geography (a consumer in a mature wine market has a food culture to lean on; one in an emerging market does not), generation (older consumers inherited retailer and merchant authority, younger ones inherited peer and platform authority), socio-economic position (which determines the cost of a mistake), and education (which determines confidence with a written list).

## Matching need to segment

**The non-engaged majority need experts absolutely, and never knowingly consult one.** Their expert is the supermarket buyer who selected the range and set the specification. This is the single most consequential act of expertise in the wine trade, and no consumer thinks of it as expertise at all. In monopoly markets - Sweden, Norway, Finland, Ontario - the state buying team performs it, with published tender criteria, and the consumer's whole choice architecture is the product of an expert panel.

**The exploring middle need translation and reassurance**, which is where the retail adviser, the sommelier, the educator and the shelf-barker do their work - and also where peer ratings substitute most effectively.

**The enthusiast needs experts least**, having become a partial expert. What they want is a critic whose palate they can calibrate against, not one to obey.

**The collector needs provenance and authentication above all** - the auctioneer, the authenticator, the storage specialist - and this need has grown rather than shrunk. Rudy Kurniawan was convicted of wine fraud in a United States federal court in 2013, and the counterfeiting problem he exposed can only be addressed by expertise, never by crowd opinion.

Geographically, the need is highest where wine has no cultural footing. A new consumer in China, India or Nigeria has no inherited framework, no family reference and no food pairing tradition to fall back on, and needs guidance far more than a French consumer choosing the local co-operative's wine. This is why expertise demand is rising globally even as it falls in mature Western markets - a dynamic that a single-market answer misses entirely.

## The experts consumers never meet

The most important part of the answer, and the part most easily overlooked because it is invisible.

Every consumer relies utterly on production expertise. That a bottle of £6 wine is sound, stable, free of volatile acidity and reliably the same as the last one is an achievement of technical expertise - controlled fermentation, temperature control, microbiology, filtration and quality assurance - and it is very recent. Wine at the bottom of the market was routinely faulty within living memory. Michel Rolland has consulted for wineries in a dozen or more countries, and Australian and New Zealand flying winemakers worked vintages in southern France and eastern Europe from the 1980s, transferring technique into regions that lacked it. The global rise in the quality *floor* is the largest single improvement in the consumer's experience of wine in half a century, and it was delivered entirely by experts the consumer will never hear of.

The same is true of viticultural expertise in a warming climate: rootstock and clone selection, canopy management and picking decisions determine whether a region can still make balanced wine. Consumers do not need to know this. They need it to be true.

## What has changed

The relationship is dynamic, and three shifts define the present.

**Authority has moved from the individual to the aggregate.** Vivino and CellarTracker hold many millions of consumer ratings, and for a consumer choosing a £10 bottle, a large average score from people like them is more useful than one authoritative palate. Robert Parker gave up reviewing Bordeaux in 2015, having sold control of The Wine Advocate three years earlier, and no successor has inherited that concentration of influence - the era of the single decisive critic is over.

**Search has replaced advice for factual questions.** Anything a consumer once asked a merchant - what is it, what does it cost elsewhere, what do people think - is now answered in seconds.

**But the crowd has failure modes that keep experts necessary.** Aggregate ratings are popularity measures, so they regress towards the familiar and the sweet, penalise challenging and age-worthy styles, and can be gamed. They cannot authenticate a bottle, assess a wine's future, judge a wine unreleased, or select a range from ten thousand samples. Where the question is "will I like this tonight", the crowd wins. Where it is "is this real, is this good, and will it be better in ten years", it cannot help at all.

## My answer

Yes - wine consumers need wine experts, and need them more than ever, but not the ones they think of and not for the reasons usually given.

The need for *translation* expertise, the visible kind, is genuinely falling in mature markets, and the industry should accept that with better grace than it has. Peer aggregation does most of the recommending now, and it does it well enough for most purchases. The critic's role is contracting to the fine-wine tier and to calibration for enthusiasts.

The need for *technical*, *selection* and *provenance* expertise is rising, and it is invisible. Every consumer depends on a winemaker they cannot name, a buyer they will never meet, and a supply chain whose integrity they cannot check. The proliferation of choice, the growth of counterfeiting, the value of fine wine and the pressure of climate change all increase that dependence.

So the honest formulation is this: consumers need experts *behind* the bottle far more than *in front* of it. The visible expert is optional and increasingly declined. The invisible expert is the reason there is anything worth drinking - and, in a world where anyone can post a rating, the reason it is worth trusting.
