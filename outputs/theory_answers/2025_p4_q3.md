## Two problems, or one?

Overproduction is production persistently above what the market will absorb at a price covering the cost of growing; falling consumption is the decline in volume drunk per capita in mature markets. They meet in the same inventory, but treating them as one problem with one fix is the first error. The surplus is not general. It is concentrated in inexpensive, generic wine — bulk red above all — while several segments are structurally short of fruit: sparkling base, premium white, and the no- and low-alcohol category. A blunt global response therefore mis-prices the problem at both ends.

The second error is to read the recent OIV data as evidence that supply has already corrected. World production in 2023 and 2024 ran at the lowest levels in some sixty years, and consumption has fallen to around where it stood in the mid-1990s, yet surpluses persisted through both years. That is diagnostic: the production shortfall was weather-driven and temporary, while the surplus is structural and will reappear in the first normal-sized vintage. Any plan built on the recent short crops is built on the wrong data.

## Supply: reduce capacity permanently, and target it

Crisis distillation and private storage aid preserve capacity and defer the reckoning. They are occasionally necessary and never a solution. Permanent removal is. The EU-supported vine-pull in Bordeaux and Australia's earlier round of pulling are the working models, and the lesson from both is that voluntary schemes remove the wrong hectares: those who accept are the oldest, the most indebted and the least able to wait, not necessarily those farming the least suitable land. Better design pays for exit weighted by site — remove the irrigated flatland planted for a market that no longer exists, and keep the old dryland vineyard capable of making wine somebody wants.

Yield ceilings, appellation reclassification and mothballing are softer levers with a supporting role, and diverting fruit to juice concentrate, spirit base or de-alcoholisation can absorb a marginal crop without depressing the wine price. None of these substitutes for capacity reduction; they buy the time to do it in an orderly way, which matters because disorderly adjustment destroys more growers than fast adjustment does.

## Demand: buy occasions, not share

Falling consumption is not uniform, and the industry damages itself by treating it as a single trend. The volume decline is concentrated in everyday drinking; premium segments and no/low have grown. The generational shift is the substantive point: younger cohorts drink less alcohol overall, and when they do drink they spread across beer, spirits, ready-to-drink and cocktails. Wine has lost occasions rather than merely losing share, and occasions are recovered through format, price and ease — single serve, cans, bag-in-box, keg for the on-trade, by-the-glass programmes with credible economics — not through education campaigns aimed at consumers who are not looking for a category to study.

Wine's self-inflicted weakness is choice architecture: thousands of near-identical options, weak brands, and labels that presuppose prior knowledge, at a moment when every adjacent category has done the opposite. Segmentation is the missing discipline — knowing which wines are commodities to be made efficiently at scale, which are brands, and which are places sold at low volume and high price, and refusing to run all three on the same cost base.

## Health, without ideology

Public health guidance in several jurisdictions has moved against moderate consumption and is unlikely to move back on the current evidence. The industry's interest is served by planning for a durable constraint rather than litigating the science, an argument it will lose in public. Equally, it is poor analysis to attribute the whole volume decline to health messaging: price inflation, generational change, category competition and falling on-trade footfall all pull the same way, and separating them matters because they call for different responses. No/low is one of the few genuinely incremental occasions available, and treating it as a threat rather than an addition has cost the industry time.

## Emerging markets

Worth pursuing, and not the answer. China's contraction after a rapid rise showed the danger of building a strategy on one market; India, Brazil and parts of Africa are growing from small bases and cannot absorb Europe's surplus at a price covering European costs. Their value is at the premium end, where the prize is value rather than volume, and where the wines in surplus are not the wines in demand.

## How best to address it

The best response is deliberately asymmetric, because the two problems are interdependent but not symmetrical. On supply: permanent, site-targeted capacity reduction, collectively funded and completed quickly. On demand: stop defending volume in the generic segment, which is money spent slowing an exit, and spend instead where consumption is actually growing — formats, occasions and price points that suit how people now drink. Both point to the same destination, a smaller and better-segmented industry selling less wine at a higher average price. The only real question is whether it arrives there by design or by insolvency.
