## Definitions and position

By *post-maturation stabilisation* I mean the treatments applied after élevage and before bottling to stop the wine changing physically, chemically or microbiologically in bottle: tartrate stabilisation, protein and colloidal stabilisation against haze, colour stabilisation, and microbial stabilisation. A *low-end wine* I take to be a high-volume, entry-level-priced wine made over a short timescale — in any style, still, sparkling, sweet or fortified — typically bottled within months of harvest and drunk within a year.

I disagree with the proposition. An instability is itself a quality defect: a tartrate crystal in the glass, a protein haze, a browning white, a refermenting bottle. Stabilisation is therefore relevant at every price point. What legitimately differs between an entry-level wine and a fine one is not *whether* stability is pursued but *how* it is achieved — fast, additive and interventionist at one end, slow and process-based at the other. Below I test that against tartrate, haze and colour, then name the one real exception.

## What gives the proposition its force

The case for restricting stabilisation is that the active treatments cost quality, and the wine with the most to lose is the wine with the most in it.

Bentonite is not selective: it takes aroma compounds with the protein, strips colour in rosés and light reds, and generates lees losses of several percent. Contact-process cold stabilisation means chilling, agitating with seed potassium bitartrate and racking a wine cold — aroma loss and oxygen pickup in a single operation. Cation exchange drops pH and strips potassium indiscriminately. PVPP removes browning precursors and colour with them. Sheet and earth filtration cost colour and mid-palate weight, and sterile filtration costs aromatic detail. I concede this much of the argument: the more actively you stabilise, the more you flatten.

## Tartrate: one goal, two methods

The commercial route buys speed and certainty. Contact-process cold stabilisation over a day or two; electrodialysis where the volume justifies the plant; or an inhibitor dosed at bottling - metatartaric acid, cheap but hydrolysing, giving protection for a year to eighteen months at cool cellar temperature but only around three months at true room temperature (20°C), which is perfect for a wine drunk that fast and useless for one that is cellared; or carboxymethylcellulose in whites and sparkling. CMC is avoided in reds because it destabilises the colour colloids, so potassium polyaspartate is the red-wine analogue.

The fine-wine route reaches the same place through time: long, cool cellar ageing across a winter, extended lees contact liberating mannoproteins that inhibit crystal growth naturally, and then a mini-contact or conductivity test to confirm before bottling. That is stabilisation — it is simply passive. And the premium wine often needs it more, not less: it will spend years in cellars of unpredictable temperature, and the buyer who paid most is the least tolerant of crystals.

## Haze and colloidal stability

Protein haze is a white-wine problem, since red tannin precipitates protein naturally. The entry-level answer is a heat-test-dosed bentonite addition shortly before bottling. The premium answer is to move the intervention earlier and make it gentler: bentonite in juice or during fermentation, when protein binds at less aromatic cost; flotation; and long lees ageing, whose mannoproteins reduce the bentonite requirement. Some producers deliberately under-fine and accept a trace of instability for a wine sold into a reliable cold chain. That is a risk assessment, not an absence of stabilisation.

## Colour stability — the axis most often ignored

Colour is where the "method differs" argument is clearest, and it is oxidative as much as physical.

In whites, browning comes from oxidation of phenolics. The industrial controls are generous SO2, ascorbic acid used with SO2, and PVPP; where laccase from botrytised fruit is present, flash pasteurisation, because laccase is heat-labile and is not reliably inhibited by SO2. The premium control is protective handling from press to bottle, low dissolved oxygen and copper and iron management, so that less correction is needed at the end.

In reds, colour stability means polymerising anthocyanins with tannin so the pigment is no longer pH- and SO2-labile. A premium red does this during maturation — barrel ageing, gentle oxygen ingress, co-pigmentation from ripe phenolics. A commercial red compresses the same chemistry: micro-oxygenation, oenological tannin additions, sometimes gum arabic to hold colloidal colour. Both are colour stabilisation. One is presented as maturation, the other as treatment.

## The exception worth naming

Natural wines are the honest exception: bottled without fining, filtration or meaningful SO2, sold with sediment, bottle variation and a cold-chain expectation built into the proposition. But the failure modes are real — refermentation, *Brettanomyces*, mousiness — and the category's better producers manage that risk by other means: low pH, immaculate fruit and cold storage. Even there the choice is method, not exemption.

## Conclusion

The statement inverts the truth. Stability is a quality attribute rather than a compromise of it, and any wine that will be transported, stored and opened by someone else needs it. The proposition is right only in the narrow sense that *aggressive, additive* stabilisation belongs where speed and certainty are worth more than nuance. The decision rule should be a per-wine risk assessment — style, pH, residual sugar, heat and cold test results, market temperature, expected time to consumption — not the price bracket.
