## What the label is being asked to do, and for whom

A wine label has three jobs and they conflict. It must comply — carrying the legally mandated declarations. It must sell — winning a two-second glance on a shelf of two hundred competitors. And it must inform — telling the buyer what is in the bottle and whether it suits the occasion. "The most important information" therefore has no single answer: it depends entirely on who is holding the bottle. I take the question as asking whether the label serves the buyer's decision, and I answer it segment by segment, weighing what labels genuinely do well against what they conspicuously do not.

## Whose information? A segmented view

- **The engaged enthusiast and the trade** — a small minority of buyers. They want producer, vineyard or cru, vintage, alcohol, appellation, importer, sometimes dosage or residual sugar. They read the label as a technical document.
- **The involved but uncertain majority** — buy on grape variety, brand, country and price. They want style: is it dry, is it heavy, will it work with what I am cooking.
- **The occasion and gift buyer** — risk-averse, wants signals of appropriateness and quality at a price point: medals, brand familiarity, weight of glass, prestige cues.
- **The price-driven buyer** — needs price, volume and a familiar category name; almost nothing else on the label is read.
- **The health- and lifestyle-aware buyer**, increasingly the younger consumer — wants alcohol, calories, ingredients, additives, organic or vegan status.

## What labels do well

The mandatory content is accurate, enforced and consistent, which is not a small thing. EU and UK rules mandate net volume, actual alcoholic strength within a tolerance of 0.5 per cent, country of origin, bottler or importer, a lot number and a declaration of sulphites above 10mg per litre. Nothing else on the shelf in a supermarket carries an origin guarantee comparable to a protected designation.

Geographic indications are also, for those who hold the code, information of extraordinary density: "Chablis Premier Cru Montée de Tonnerre 2020" specifies grape, style, soil, climate, yield ceiling and ageing potential in five words. New World varietal labelling was arguably the single most successful communication reform in modern wine, and it worked because it answered the question the majority of buyers actually ask. Back labels, food-matching icons, sweetness scales such as the International Riesling Foundation's sweetness scale, and — belatedly — the EU's move to ingredient and nutrition declarations delivered off-label through a QR code all represent real progress.

## What labels do badly

The mandatory content is the information regulators wanted, not the information buyers want. "Contains sulphites" tells the consumer nothing actionable. Alcohol is stated but its meaning is not: a 250ml glass of a 14 per cent wine is three and a half UK units, and no label helps anyone work that out.

More seriously, the European system requires prior knowledge to decode. Chablis does not say Chardonnay, Sancerre does not say Sauvignon Blanc, and no classic appellation states whether the wine is dry — the one thing an uncertain buyer most wants to know. Sweetness is unstated on the great majority of still wines, and Prosecco labelled "Extra Dry" is sweeter than one labelled "Brut", which is not merely unhelpful but actively misleading. Quality-implying terms — Reserve, Vieilles Vignes, Grand Vin, Superior — are unregulated in most of the world. Château imagery attaches to brands with no château. Back-label prose is largely marketing ("hints of forest floor"), written for the enthusiast and unread by everyone else. And there is no drinking window, no serving temperature, no indication of whether the wine will improve.

## The verdict

No. Today's labels do a good job of complying and an excellent job of selling, and a poor job of communicating.

The evidence for that is behavioural: the whole apparatus the trade has built around the bottle — shelf talkers, staff recommendation, medal stickers, critic scores, aisle signage segmenting shelves by style rather than by origin, and phone apps that photograph the label to tell the consumer what it means — exists because the label itself does not answer the buyer's question. An information system that requires a second information system to interpret it has failed.

Segment the verdict and it sharpens. For the enthusiast and the trade, labels work well, and they are the people who write the rules. For the involved-but-uncertain majority, labels work only in New World varietal form. For the health-aware younger consumer — the fastest-changing segment and the one the category most needs to recruit — the label has until very recently told them almost nothing they asked for.

I would reject the counter-argument that decoding is part of wine's pleasure and that simplification cheapens it. Complexity that rewards the initiated is a barrier to recruitment dressed up as a virtue, and a category losing consumers to drinks that explain themselves cannot afford it.
