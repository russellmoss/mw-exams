## Scope and the test applied

I compare **Bordeaux** and the **Barossa Valley**, both of which sell wine from a few euros to several hundred, and I apply one test throughout: a vineyard's revenue per hectare is yield multiplied by price per tonne, so a low-priced wine must reach its revenue through volume and a high-priced wine can reach it through price. Every decision below is therefore judged not on whether it improves the wine but on whether the improvement is recoverable at that wine's price. Six issues follow.

## 1. Site and planting density

At the top, site is bought rather than managed. In the Médoc the deep gravel croupes with free drainage and a high water table beneath are worth many multiples of the Entre-Deux-Mers clays, and the classed growths plant tight — planting densities of 8,000 to 10,000 vines per hectare in the Médoc — to force root competition and hold yield per vine low while keeping yield per hectare workable. Generic Bordeaux plants at a fraction of that density on flatter, more fertile ground, because wide rows admit larger machinery and cut the per-hectare cost of every subsequent pass.

The Barossa shows the same split geographically: Eden Valley's elevated, cooler, poorer sites and the Barossa floor's old dry-grown blocks carry the premium wines, while irrigated high-vigour plantings on the valley's deeper alluvial soils carry the volume. The contrast is the same one: the premium site is chosen for constraint, the commercial site for capacity.

## 2. Yield and crop load

This is the central decision. The generic Bordeaux yield ceiling sits in the region of 55 to 60 hectolitres per hectare and growers aim at it, because at commodity grape prices the only route to a viable return is the full crop. A classed growth in the Médoc will commonly work at 35 to 45, achieved by conservative bud load at winter pruning, shoot thinning and, in a generous year, a green harvest. Cutting a third of the crop is only rational when the bottle price rises by more than a third — which at first-growth prices it does, and at generic prices it never does.

Barossa contract growers are frequently paid by the tonne against a quality grade, which makes the same arithmetic explicit: a grower on a premium contract restricts crop because the price band pays for it, and a grower on a commercial contract carries maximum crop because it does not.

## 3. Water

Irrigation is prohibited in Bordeaux's appellations other than by derogation, so water management there is soil management — cover crops sown or ploughed out to regulate vine water status, which is a premium tool because it costs tractor passes. In the Barossa the contrast is sharper than anywhere. Premium fruit comes from dry-grown or minimally irrigated old blocks where deficit stress concentrates the crop; commercial fruit is irrigated to sustain yield, and water is a purchased input whose cost is set against the tonnage it buys. Irrigating a premium block to yield would destroy more value than the water costs.

## 4. Canopy management and labour

Leaf removal, shoot positioning, lateral trimming and bunch thinning are labour, and labour is the largest controllable cost in viticulture. A premium estate does them by hand, selectively, fruit zone by fruit zone and often side by side to manage sun exposure. A commercial block does them once, mechanically, or not at all — accepting more shading and a less even ripeness because the wine's style does not require the precision and the price will not fund it.

## 5. Plant material and vine age

Premium blocks favour massal selection or low-yielding clones on devigorating rootstock, and old vines are retained despite falling productivity: the Barossa Old Vine Charter, which recognises vines from 35 years upward, exists precisely to convert that low yield into a price premium. Commercial blocks favour certified high-yielding clones on vigorous rootstock and replant on economic rather than sentimental grounds, because a block that has fallen below its target tonnage no longer pays its costs.

## 6. Harvesting

The clearest divergence of all. Commercial fruit in both regions is machine-harvested, at night for temperature, in a single pass, with the date set by sugar and by winery logistics — one pass over many hectares, at a small fraction of the cost per hectare of hand picking. Premium fruit is picked by hand into small boxes, parcel by parcel, sometimes over several tries as blocks reach phenolic rather than sugar ripeness, and then sorted at the winery. Hand picking costs several times what machine harvesting costs per hectare and requires a large, skilled, briefly-employed crew, which is now the binding constraint in both regions.

## Conclusion

The methods are not different in kind; they are the same decisions taken with opposite answers, because the two price points face opposite economics. High-priced viticulture spends labour and forgoes yield to buy precision, and can do so only because the bottle price recovers it. Low-priced viticulture protects yield and mechanises everything, because at commodity prices cost per tonne is the only variable that decides whether the block survives.
