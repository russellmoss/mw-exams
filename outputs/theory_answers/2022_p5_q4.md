## Definitions

**Sustainable**, for this answer, means the capacity of a wine business — and of the industry it belongs to — to continue operating indefinitely on three axes. *Environmental*: soil, water, biodiversity, agrochemical load and greenhouse-gas emissions. *Economic*: profitability, access to capital, succession, the ability to reinvest. *Social*: fair pay and conditions, safe work, and the survival of the rural communities that supply labour. The boundary runs from the nursery to the consumer's recycling bin, not from the vineyard gate to the bottling line. An estate that farms beautifully and loses money every year is not sustainable; nor is one whose environmental footprint is dominated by a package it never examines.

**Key** can mean the most crucial factor, or the one that unlocks the others. I test both.

**Biodynamic practices** are, in outline: certified-organic farming as a base; composting and the field and compost preparations, principally horn manure and horn silica; a whole-farm approach treating the estate as a single organism, with land set aside for biodiversity; and timing of operations by a lunar and planetary calendar. Certification is by Demeter or Biodyvin.

**My answer is no** — biodynamics is not the key on either reading. It is a strong contributor on one axis, silent on most of the others, and its best practices are separable from the belief system that carries them.

## What biodynamics actually delivers

The environmental case is real and I do not want to understate it. Eliminating herbicides, synthetic fertilisers and synthetic pesticides is the largest single vineyard-level gain available, and biodynamic estates achieve it by construction. Composting and permanent cover crops raise soil organic matter, which improves water-holding capacity, resists erosion and sequesters carbon. Demeter certification requires whole-estate conversion, a three-year transition and the setting aside of a share of the farm for biodiversity, so habitat returns at farm scale rather than in token corners.

There is a second, less tangible effect that I think matters more than the preparations. Biodynamics compels attention: a grower making compost, walking rows on a calendar and observing individual vines makes better decisions than one following a spray diary. Château Pontet-Canet has farmed biodynamically in Pauillac since the mid-2000s, gaining formal organic and biodynamic certification in 2010, and the quality trajectory is not disputed -- but the honest reading is that meticulous, well-capitalised farming explains it at least as well as the horn silica.

That distinction is where the evidence sits. Controlled comparisons have generally struggled to isolate any effect attributable to the preparations as distinct from the organic farming underneath them. For an evidence-based answer this has to be said plainly: the anthroposophical apparatus is unproven. It does not make the farming worse, and if the calendar is what gets a grower into the vineyard at dawn, it has an instrumental value. But it cannot be the basis of an industry-wide claim.

## Where biodynamics is neutral or negative

**Copper.** Organic and biodynamic growers depend on copper against downy mildew. EU rules have progressively restricted copper to an average of around 4 kg per hectare per year precisely because it accumulates permanently in soil and is toxic to earthworms and soil microbiota. This is the strongest evidence-based critique of organic and biodynamic viticulture and it goes to the heart of the soil-health claim.

**Fuel and compaction.** Under-vine mechanical weeding and rain-washed contact sprays mean more tractor passes than a conventional programme, particularly in wet vintages: more diesel, more emissions, more soil compaction.

**Yield arithmetic.** The unit that matters commercially and environmentally is the bottle, not the hectare. Lower yields spread the same fixed footprint over fewer bottles, so a biodynamic estate can carry a higher carbon intensity per bottle than a conventional neighbour despite using fewer inputs. This is uncomfortable and it is arithmetic.

**Cost, labour and price point.** Biodynamics is labour-intensive and expensive, and it therefore concentrates where the price point absorbs it. That is a financial and a social objection at once: a model available only above a certain bottle price cannot be the industry's answer, and the industry's volume — and most of its employment — sits below it.

Scale is not an absolute barrier: Emiliana in Chile farms its roughly 1,000-hectare estate organically, with a substantial biodynamic-certified component, showing the model can operate well beyond boutique scale. But it does so in dry summers with low disease pressure. The same programme in the Loire, Alsace or England in a wet year is a different proposition entirely, and any claim that biodynamics is the key must survive the wet vintage, not the easy one.

## The comparison the question demands

**Conventional** farming has genuine sustainability advantages that are usually skipped. Systemic fungicides give effective control with fewer passes, so less fuel; they prevent the crop losses that make a bad year financially unsustainable; higher yields dilute fixed footprint per bottle; and herbicide use, whatever its effect on soil biology, avoids tillage — which on a slope protects soil carbon and prevents erosion. That is a real trade-off, not a debating point.

**Organic** delivers most of biodynamics' environmental gains, at lower cost, against a legally defined and internationally recognised standard. On a cost-adjusted basis it is the better-value certification for most producers.

**Other approaches entirely** may matter more than any of them. Integrated pest management with weather-driven spray decisions, pheromone confusion for moth control and precision application cuts agrochemical load without ideological commitment. Disease-resistant varieties are the single largest available lever, removing most fungicide passes rather than substituting one product for another, and their progressive authorisation in German and Swiss quality categories is the development to watch. Biodynamics has no equivalent.

## What sustainability requires that biodynamics never touches

This is the decisive part of my argument. Glass is typically the single largest component of a bottled wine's carbon footprint, so lightweighting, bulk shipment and alternative formats outweigh almost any vineyard decision — and packaging sits entirely outside biodynamics' compass. So do winery energy and refrigeration, and renewable generation. So does water: wineries typically consume of the order of three to six litres of water for every litre of wine (with efficient operations closer to three and the industry average nearer six), and wastewater treatment is a serious cost and a serious pollution risk. So do waste streams — pomace, lees, cardboard. So does logistics: modal shift and filling in market. So do the social questions — seasonal worker pay and housing, safety, equity, succession. And so does profitability, without which none of it continues.

Whole-business schemes address exactly these gaps. Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand, which covers the overwhelming majority of that country's vineyard area, together with Certified California Sustainable Winegrowing, South Africa's IPW and Sustainable Winegrowing Australia, are weaker than biodynamics inside the vineyard and far stronger everywhere else. They are also auditable at national scale, which biodynamics is not.

## Conclusion

On the first reading of "key" — the most crucial factor — the answer is clearly no. Packaging, energy, logistics and profitability dominate a wine business's sustainability, and biodynamics is silent on all four.

On the second reading — the thing that unlocks the rest — it is more arguable, because biodynamic converts often go on to tackle energy and packaging. But that reflects the kind of producer who converts, not a property of the practices themselves.

The defensible position is selective adoption. Composting, permanent cover crops, no herbicide, real biodiversity and the discipline of daily observation are the biodynamic practices that survive scrutiny, and they can be taken up inside a certified whole-business sustainability programme by any producer at any price point. Full Demeter certification is a legitimate choice for estates whose climate and pricing support it, and a poor prescription for the industry as a whole.
