## Scoping the question

Grapevine trunk diseases are **fungal** colonisations of the permanent wood — trunk, cordon and spur — that enter almost exclusively through pruning wounds, occlude the vascular system and cause progressive dieback, yield loss and vine death. They are not viral, and they are not phylloxera or flavescence dorée, which attack the root and the phloem respectively and fall outside the question. They are now the single largest cause of unplanned vine replacement worldwide.

## Part 1: the most important trunk diseases

**Esca.** A complex rather than a single pathogen, involving *Phaeomoniella chlamydospora*, *Phaeoacremonium* species and, in the mature form, the white-rot basidiomycete *Fomitiporia mediterranea*. It presents as tiger-stripe leaf chlorosis and, in the apoplectic form, sudden collapse of a whole vine in high summer. It is the dominant trunk disease of Mediterranean Europe — Italy, Spain and southern France — and became far more damaging after the loss of arsenite.

**Eutypa dieback.** *Eutypa lata*. Stunted, cupped, chlorotic spring shoots and a wedge of dead wood in section. Spores are released by winter rainfall, so it is worst in regions with wet, cool winters: coastal California, cooler and wetter southern Australian regions, and Cognac, where Ugni Blanc is notoriously susceptible. Cabernet Sauvignon and Sauvignon Blanc are also documented as susceptible.

**Botryosphaeria dieback.** The Botryosphaeriaceae, including *Diplodia* and *Neofusicoccum* species. Cordon and spur dieback with internal necrosis, and the one that dominates in warm and arid regions — inland Australia, South Africa, the Californian interior, Spain — where vines are already under heat and water stress.

**Phomopsis dieback.** *Phomopsis viticola* and related *Diaporthe* species. Basal shoot lesions and cane bleaching, favoured by wet, cool springs; it matters most in northern Europe, Oregon and New Zealand.

**Young-vine diseases.** Petri disease, caused by the same *Phaeomoniella* and *Phaeoacremonium* fungi as Esca, and black foot, caused by *Ilyonectria* and related soil fungi, kill or stunt vines in their first few years and are largely nursery-borne. A vine of five to seven years is a young vine, not a middle-aged one, and the fact that decline appears this early is diagnostic of a nursery rather than a vineyard infection.

## Part 2: control and management

There is no curative chemistry: sodium arsenite, the one genuinely effective curative treatment, was withdrawn in the European Union in the early 2000s, and nothing has replaced it. Management is therefore a matter of preventing infection, protecting wounds, and removing infected wood.

**Prevent infection at the wound.** Prune as late in dormancy as the region permits, so wounds are exposed for less time and heal faster as sap rises; prune in dry weather, since spore release is rain-driven; and prune in two passes, mechanically pre-pruning then finishing by hand, which also reduces cost.

**Reduce wound size and respect the vine's architecture.** Large cuts into old wood are the highest-risk entry points. The Simonit and Sirch approach to respecting sap flow — small cuts, on the same side, leaving a desiccation cone away from the sap path — is now widely adopted precisely because it is a training decision that costs nothing but skill.

**Protect the wound.** Physical sealants and paints, wound-protectant fungicides, and Trichoderma-based wound biocontrol products applied immediately after cutting. Timing is everything: a wound is most susceptible in the first days.

**Sanitation and remediation.** Remove and burn infected prunings and dead vines rather than mulching them back into the row, which recycles inoculum. On individual vines, remedial surgery works: trunk renewal by cutting back below the necrosis and re-training a watershoot or sucker, and curettage to excise decayed wood, both of which can return a vine to production far more cheaply than replanting.

**Clean planting material.** For Petri and black foot the leverage is at the nursery: hot-water treatment of dormant cuttings, typically around 50 degrees Celsius for about thirty minutes, hygiene in hydration tanks and grafting, and avoiding planting stress in the young vineyard.

## Control compared by climate

The right programme differs by where the vineyard is. In **winter-rainfall Mediterranean regions** — southern France, Spain, coastal California — spore release is tied to winter rain, so pruning *timing* is the primary lever: pruning late, and never immediately before rain, delivers more than any product. In **warm arid irrigated regions** — the Barossa, South Africa's interior, the Californian Central Valley — Botryosphaeria dominates and infection periods are less predictable, so routine wound protection and, critically, the relief of water and heat stress that predisposes vines to dieback matter more than calendar timing. In **cold continental regions** the pruning window is short and the wood frozen, so late and double pruning are hard to schedule; the emphasis shifts to sanitation, clean nursery stock and remedial surgery.

Varietal susceptibility should shape the programme too: a susceptible variety on a high-value site justifies hand wound-painting that would be uneconomic elsewhere.

## Conclusion

The four diseases that matter globally are Esca, Eutypa, Botryosphaeria and Phomopsis dieback, with Petri disease and black foot the young-vine equivalents. Since none can be cured, management is a discipline rather than a treatment: prune late and small, protect every wound, sanitise ruthlessly, buy clean plants, and operate surgically on individual vines — with the balance between those levers set by the region's rainfall pattern and heat load.
