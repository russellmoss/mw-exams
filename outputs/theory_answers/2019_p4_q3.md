## Three different claims, routinely conflated

The commercial mistake underlying most industry responses is to treat these as one trend. They are three claims with different costs and different proof requirements.

**Vegan** is a claim about processing aids: no gelatine, isinglass, casein, egg albumen or crustacean chitosan. It is cheap, fast and almost invisible to the drinker, and it is verified by supplier declarations, not by analysis. **Organic** is a legally defined, third-party-certified standard covering inputs in the vineyard and permitted treatments in the winery, with real cost and real risk. **Sustainable** is the broadest and the least defined: environmental, social and economic, mostly self-declared or covered by national schemes of varying rigour.

The consumer conflates all three and buys them as a single reassurance signal. That gap between what the claims cost the producer and what they mean to the buyer is where the commercial opportunity and the reputational risk both sit.

## Political

Policy is now a demand driver in its own right. The EU's Farm to Fork strategy targets a quarter of farmland under organic production by 2030, with conversion subsidy behind it, which means European organic supply will grow whether or not consumer demand keeps pace. Producers should expect the organic premium in Europe to be squeezed by supply, not sustained by policy.

## Economic

Organic conversion takes three years of organic practice before certification, during which costs rise and the wine sells at the conventional price. Yields are typically lower and more variable, and a wet season under copper restrictions can cost a meaningful part of the crop. The commercial questions are therefore who funds conversion and whether the premium survives.

The evidence is that it does not survive at the bottom. Large volumes of certified organic bulk from La Mancha and the Languedoc have made organic entry-level wine ordinary, and retailers now expect organic at close to conventional prices in own label. At £10 and above the premium is real, because there the claim sits alongside a story the consumer is already paying for. My recommendation follows directly: certify where the price point can carry the cost; below that, treat organic as a listing qualification rather than a margin opportunity, and price accordingly.

Vegan is different economically because it is nearly free. Switching to bentonite and pea protein costs a fining trial and some paperwork. Any producer selling into the UK or the Nordics should simply do it.

## Social: the claims mean different things in different markets

The Nordics and Germany index highest on organic, and in the Nordic monopoly systems sustainability requirements function as a route to listing rather than as a premium — you comply to be considered. The UK skews to vegan and to retailer-level sustainability policy, with third-party vegan marks doing the consumer-facing work. The US behaves differently again: certification matters less than a marketing vocabulary of "clean", low-sugar and low-intervention, and US regulation reserves the term organic wine for wine with no added sulphites, which is why most such wine is labelled made with organic grapes — a labelling constraint that has held back the category there. Mainland China and much of Asia show low salience for all three claims, where price, origin and brand still dominate. Australia and New Zealand sit closer to the sustainability end, driven by producer schemes and export requirements.

The commercial consequence is that a single global pack and a single global claim is the wrong answer. The lever with the largest environmental effect is often the cheapest: packaging and distribution, rather than farming, are typically the largest single contributors to the carbon footprint of a bottle of wine. Lightweight glass, bulk shipping with bottling at destination, bag-in-box, cans and flat recycled formats all deliver more measurable reduction than a change of herbicide, and they save money rather than costing it. Retailers with their own emissions targets are already pushing glass weight down. This is where the industry can act fastest.

## Legal

Organic is enforceable; vegan and sustainable are not defined in wine law and depend on third-party marks and national schemes. Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand covers a very large majority of that country's vineyard area, and Chile, South Africa, California and Australia all run credible national programmes. The risk is logo inflation: too many marks, of uneven rigour, and a consumer who cannot tell them apart. Unaudited "sustainable" claims are a greenwashing scandal waiting to happen, and the reputational cost of one would exceed the premium the whole category earns from the claim.

## What the industry should do

Three things, in order. **Adopt vegan fining across the board** — the cost is negligible and it unlocks listings. **Certify organic selectively**, where the price point pays for it, and resist the assumption that certification is a marketing asset at entry level. **Consolidate behind a small number of audited sustainability schemes** rather than inventing more logos, and lead the communication with packaging and carbon, where the numbers are defensible and the savings are real.

The honest counter-argument is that certification adds cost and rigidity for a premium that is thin and shrinking. That is true at the bottom of the market and false at the top; the response should be differentiated accordingly rather than adopted as a category-wide article of faith.
