## The test I am applying

Before listing anything I need a rule for deciding, because a label is a finite surface and every element added displaces another. My rule is this: a wine label should carry what a purchaser cannot verify for themselves at the point of sale and would materially want to know, plus what the law requires to make the supply chain accountable. Everything else is marketing, and marketing belongs on the label only to the extent that it does not crowd out the first two.

Two consequences follow immediately. The label is not one object but three — the front label, the back label, and now an electronic label reached by code — and the right question is not what should be on "a label" but what belongs at each of those three levels of attention. And the label is not written for one reader: a regulator, an importer, a supermarket buyer, a sommelier and a shopper with forty seconds in a fixture all read the same bottle for different purposes.

## Front, back, and the field of vision

The front label is the branding surface and it is read at a distance of a metre in poor light. Its job is identification and differentiation: who made it, what it is, where it is from. EU law already requires the sales denomination, actual alcoholic strength, nominal volume, provenance, the name of the bottler or importer, allergen declaration for sulphites, and a lot code, with several of those in a single field of vision — a requirement that exists precisely so a consumer cannot be made to rotate the bottle to find the strength.

The back label is the reading surface, consulted at arm's length by someone who has already picked the bottle up. It is where information belongs that requires more than a glance, and where regulatory clutter should be pushed so it does not degrade the front.

The e-label is the third tier, and it changes what the argument is about. Under EU rules, wines produced from 8 December 2023 must carry an energy value and a full ingredient list, which may sit behind a QR code. That relieves the surface-area problem almost entirely: anything a diligent minority wants, and a majority ignores, can go there.

## Six competing needs

**Legal.** Non-negotiable and different in every market. This is the largest practical constraint on label design and the reason a global brand carries market-specific back labels: the United States has required a government health warning on every container since the late 1980s, and the EU has not.

**Consumer protection.** The most underrated category and, in my view, the most important. Sulphite and other allergen declarations protect a small group from real harm. The bottler or importer name and the lot code exist so a contaminated or fraudulent batch can be withdrawn — the least glamorous elements on a label and the ones I would defend hardest. Authenticity elements sit here too: for wines that trade on a secondary market, a serialised bubble tag or NFC seal does more for the buyer than any tasting note.

**Branding.** Legitimate, and worth defending against the assumption that information is always virtuous. A distinctive front label is how a small producer competes for attention against a listed brand with a marketing budget, and how a regional trade association such as Rioja or Chianti Classico converts a collective investment into a recognisable seal.

**Informative.** Strength, volume, vintage, variety where meaningful, and — the element most often missing — a plain indication of style. A Mosel bottle whose sweetness the consumer must infer from a Prädikat term is failing the purchaser; the German trade's own dry/off-dry taste profiles exist because it recognised the problem. I would make a simple sweetness or style descriptor mandatory.

**Ethical.** Vegan and organic status, carbon and recycling information, fair-trade certification. Retail evidence is that these improve listing success as much as sell-through, because buyers use them to satisfy their own corporate commitments — a case where the label serves the trade at least as much as the drinker.

**Health advisory.** Units or grams of alcohol, calories, moderation messaging. Ireland legislated in 2023 for mandatory health warnings and calorie and gram-of-alcohol declarations on the label itself, and that is the direction of travel across developed markets.

## Who gains and who pays

Every element above transfers value from one part of the chain to another, and an answer that lists elements without saying who pays is incomplete.

Producers bear the whole cost. Regulatory change means redesign, artwork approval in each market, and the writing-off of printed label stock — a serious expense for an estate with thirty SKUs and a trivial one for a multinational, so labelling regulation is quietly regressive and consolidates the market. Importers and bottlers gain traceability protection but also acquire liability for what the label says. Retailers and monopolies gain: standardised ethical and nutritional data lets them compare and rank suppliers, and the supermarket buyer is often the real audience for a sustainability logo. Sommeliers gain almost nothing from the back label and everything from the front, because their reference is the producer name. Consumers are not one group: a category buyer wants price, colour and sweetness; an engaged buyer wants site, vintage and winemaking.

The unresolved risk is plain packaging. If wine does not put credible health information on the label voluntarily, it invites tobacco-style regulation, in which the branding surface is confiscated altogether. That argues for the industry conceding the health tier now, on its own terms, rather than defending an absolutist position it will lose.

One thing I would fix that almost nobody discusses: the alcohol tolerance. The EU tolerance on declared alcoholic strength is generally 0.5% by volume, widening to 0.8% for some categories, while the United States permits 1.5% below 14% and 1% at or above it. A figure that can be a full point and a half wrong is not consumer information, and it is indefensible on a label that is about to carry a per-serving alcohol declaration derived from it.

## My prescription

**Front label:** producer or brand, geographical indication and its status, vintage, actual alcoholic strength, nominal volume. Nothing else. This is the identification tier and it should be protected from encroachment.

**Back label:** bottler or importer with address, lot code, allergen declaration, a plain style and sweetness indicator, grams of alcohol and energy per serving with a moderation statement, and the QR code.

**E-label:** full ingredient and processing-aid list, nutritional detail, certifications, carbon and recycling data, provenance and authentication.

## Conclusion

A wine label should be a hierarchy, not a list. It should carry, on the bottle itself, only what protects the purchaser, identifies the wine honestly and enables recall — and it should push everything else behind the code. I would add exactly three things to today's mandatory set: a plain style and sweetness indicator, a per-serving alcohol and calorie declaration, and a tightened tolerance on declared strength. And I would take one thing away — the assumption that adding more to the front label serves the consumer, when in practice it serves the regulator and costs the smallest producers the most.
