## Scope and the two tests

A drinking guideline is non-statutory advice from a health authority on how much alcohol may be consumed at what level of risk. It is not law: the drink-drive limit, the purchase age and minimum unit pricing compel, whereas a guideline only persuades. That distinction runs through the whole answer.

"Make sense" admits two tests, and I apply both. **Internally**: is the guidance scientifically coherent, self-consistent and comprehensible? **Instrumentally**: does it achieve the purpose set for it? A guideline can pass one and fail the other, and in my judgement most of them do exactly that.

## What the guidelines actually say

The divergence between countries is the single most important fact in this question. The United Kingdom's Chief Medical Officers revised their guidance in 2016 to 14 units a week for men and women alike, spread over three or more days, and abandoned any claim of a safe level; a UK unit is 8g of ethanol, so that is roughly 112g a week. The United States Dietary Guidelines have advised no more than two drinks a day for men and one for women, on a standard drink of 14g — for a man, about double the British allowance. Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council recommended in 2020 no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than four on any one day, on a 10g standard drink. Canada's guidance moved in 2023 to a continuum of risk on which two drinks a week or fewer is described as low risk — a reduction of roughly an order of magnitude from its own previous advice. France advises a maximum of two glasses on any day and no more than ten a week, with alcohol-free days. Above all of them, the World Health Organization stated in 2023 that no level of consumption is safe.

The definition of the unit being counted also varies internationally, from 8g in the United Kingdom to around 20g in some European countries, so even the measuring stick is not common.

## What a guideline is for

Guidelines in public health generally do four things. They give a population a simple heuristic where individual risk calculation is impossible. They give clinicians and employers a threshold to work against. They anchor downstream policy — labelling, licensing, advertising codes, taxation. And they signal an official position, since a government that declines to state one has effectively said that any amount is acceptable.

Judged against those purposes rather than against biological precision, they are doing something reasonable and cheap.

## The case that they make sense

The underlying evidence supports the direction of travel. Dose-response relationships for alcohol with liver disease, several cancers and hypertension are well established, and the earlier "J-curve" showing cardio-protection at low doses has been substantially undermined by the recognition of abstainer bias — former heavy drinkers who quit through illness were counted as non-drinkers. Guidelines that moved downwards were responding to evidence, which is what one wants from them.

A heuristic is also necessary. Personalised risk is unknowable to the drinker; a number that can be remembered is worth more in practice than an accurate model nobody can use. And guidelines do real institutional work: they underpin the alcohol content and health messaging that now appears on labels, they give a doctor a language for a difficult conversation, and they legitimise not drinking, which matters socially.

## The case that they do not

**They cannot all be right.** The same global evidence base yields limits that differ several-fold between comparable countries. Either most of them are wrong, or the number is not really a scientific output at all but a policy judgement dressed as one. I think it is the second, and presenting a policy judgement as a biological threshold is where the credibility is lost.

**They are internally contradictory.** If there is no safe level, what is 14 units? A threshold implies safety below it and danger above it, and both authorities and campaigners now deny the first while continuing to publish the second.

**They are not understood or usable.** Very few drinkers know their national limit, and fewer can convert real drinking into it: a 250ml glass of 14 per cent wine is about three and a half UK units, so a quarter of the week's allowance is gone in one restaurant pour. A guidance system whose arithmetic defeats its audience has failed at the only thing it exists to do.

**They target the wrong distribution.** Alcohol-related harm is heavily concentrated in a minority of very heavy drinkers, and they are precisely the group least responsive to advisory numbers. The moderate majority who might comply were carrying modest risk anyway. So the instrument is aimed away from the harm.

**Nothing follows from breaching them.** Being non-statutory is presented as a virtue but means there is no mechanism at all beyond persuasion.

**The efficacy evidence is thin.** There is little good evidence that publishing guidelines changes consumption. The interventions with a measurable effect are price, availability and marketing restriction — Scotland introduced minimum unit pricing in 2018, and that class of measure has an evidence base that guidance alone does not.

## My verdict

Yes in principle, no as presently constructed.

Governments should state a position on alcohol; the alternative is silence that reads as endorsement, and the anchoring function for labelling and clinical practice is genuinely useful. To that extent guidelines make sense and should exist.

But most of them do not make sense as written. They express a policy judgement with a false air of biological precision; they contradict their own "no safe level" framing; they are not comprehensible to the people they address; they are aimed at the population least implicated in the harm; and there is little evidence that they work. Three changes would fix most of that: communicate absolute risk honestly — the United Kingdom's 2016 review anchored its number to a comparably small lifetime risk of death, and saying so plainly is more persuasive than any threshold — harmonise the standard drink internationally so the advice is at least commensurable, and stop pretending that advice can substitute for the pricing and availability measures that actually move consumption.

**The strongest argument against my position** is that I am demanding a precision no public health message can carry. Guidelines are not scientific statements; they are behavioural nudges, and nudges are allowed to be crude if they shift a population mean slightly in a good direction. On that reading, incoherence between countries is unimportant and my objection is a category error. I take the point seriously, and it is why my verdict is not that guidelines should be abolished. But a nudge that nobody can follow, in a direction nobody understands, backed by numbers that contradict each other across borders, is not a crude instrument — it is an ineffective one, and it spends public credibility that stricter and more honest measures will need.
