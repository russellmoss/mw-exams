## Scope and position

Three words in this question decide the answer: how, why, and should. I take "influence consumption" to mean any deliberate state action that changes how much wine is drunk, by whom, in what circumstances and at what price — not merely restriction, since much government activity promotes consumption. And I take the epidemiological position as settled: alcohol is a group 1 carcinogen with a dose-related contribution to liver disease, cardiovascular harm, dependency and mental illness, and no established risk-free intake. Arguing with that evidence is not a defence of wine; it is the fastest way to lose the argument.

My position is that governments not only may but must influence wine consumption, because they carry the external costs; that the legitimate instruments are strength-based taxation, price floors on the cheapest alcohol, controls on availability and age, and honest labelling; and that the instruments they should be far more cautious with are blanket advertising bans, moral campaigning and protectionist trade measures. Wine deserves no pharmacological exemption but does have a distinct cultural and land-use profile, which should shape how policy is applied, not whether.

## Why governments intervene

**Public health and safety.** The primary motive and the strongest. Alcohol-attributable cancer, cirrhosis, dependency and mental ill-health fall on public healthcare budgets, as do road deaths, violence and lost productivity. A state that funds treatment has an unavoidable interest in the volume consumed.

**Protection of minors and vulnerable groups.** Distinct from general health policy and much less contested. Early initiation predicts later harm; dependency concentrates in identifiable groups; foetal alcohol harm is preventable.

**Consumer protection.** Standardised labelling of strength, allergens, origin and, increasingly, ingredients and nutrition allows an informed choice and suppresses fraud and adulteration.

**Supporting the domestic industry.** Governments in producing countries have a direct stake in wine's contribution to employment, exports, rural economies and land use, and act through promotion funding, export support and appellation law.

**Culture and tourism.** In France, Italy, Spain, Portugal and Georgia, wine is heritage and landscape as well as a product; enological tourism sustains regions with few alternatives.

**Regulating commerce.** Excise, VAT and licensing raise substantial revenue and give the state visibility of a supply chain that is otherwise easy to run informally.

These motives conflict — often within the same ministry, and sometimes within the same budget.

## How they intervene, and how well each tool works

**Taxation.** The most powerful lever, because consumption is price-elastic at the low end. Rates vary enormously: Nordic states and Ireland tax wine heavily; France and Italy tax still wine at or near nothing; Australia taxes by wholesale value rather than by volume or strength. The United Kingdom moved in 2023 to a duty system taxing all alcohol by strength, which is in my view the correct model, because it prices the harmful agent rather than the category and gives producers a commercial reason to lower abv. Value-based taxes such as Australia's do the opposite: they tax quality and leave cheap, strong wine lightly taxed. I would move every jurisdiction to strength-based duty.

**Minimum pricing.** Scotland introduced minimum unit pricing in 2018 at 50p per unit and raised it to 65p in 2024. It is well targeted: it bites almost entirely on cheap high-strength alcohol bought by the heaviest drinkers, and barely touches wine at typical retail prices. Evaluations have reported reduced consumption and alcohol-attributable deaths. I regard it as the single best-evidenced intervention available and would adopt it widely, with the caveat that the margin accrues to retailers rather than to the exchequer, which is a design flaw that taxation avoids.

**Availability and licensing.** Retail monopolies in Sweden, Norway, Finland and parts of Canada, licensing hours, outlet density limits and the American three-tier system all restrict where and when wine can be bought. The evidence that availability drives consumption is strong, but the costs are real: monopolies narrow range, and the three-tier system principally protects incumbent distributors rather than health. I support licensing and outlet controls; I do not support state monopoly of retail, which achieves its health aim at a disproportionate cost to choice and to small producers.

**Age limits and behavioural law.** Purchase-age minimums — 18 across most of Europe, a federal minimum purchase age of 21 in the United States — and drink-driving limits are the most defensible interventions of all, and drink-driving limits are the clearest case of a policy that saves lives at almost no cost to the enjoyment of wine. Every government should have both, rigorously enforced.

**Labelling and information.** Ireland has legislated for cancer and liver-disease warnings on alcohol labels, with application from 2026, and the EU now requires ingredient and nutrition information. I support mandatory, factual disclosure without reservation: a consumer cannot make an informed choice from a label that hides what most other food and drink must declare. Graphic warnings, by contrast, have a weaker evidence base and risk being ignored.

**Advertising restrictions.** France's Loi Évin, in force since the early 1990s, permits alcohol advertising only in defined media and restricts content to factual matter — origin, strength, production method — with no lifestyle or aspirational imagery. Is it sensible? In its core it is: separating alcohol marketing from aspiration, and from young audiences, is proportionate, and I would recommend that principle to other countries. Its execution is not: the law's breadth has at times chilled ordinary wine journalism and regional tourism promotion, which serves no health purpose. Other governments should adopt its content rules and reject its reach.

**Trade and industrial policy.** Tariffs, quotas, export subsidies and promotion funds also shape consumption. China imposed punitive duties on Australian wine in 2020 and removed them in 2024, reshaping two national industries. Here I am most sceptical: measures of this kind are almost never about consumption and almost always about leverage, and their costs fall on growers who have no part in the dispute.

## The conflict at the centre of the question

A government that both funds cancer treatment and funds wine export promotion is discharging two duties that cannot be fully reconciled. I do not think the conflict should be resolved by abolishing one side. Wine's cultural and economic contribution is genuine — landscape, rural employment, tourism, export earnings — and it is legitimate for a state to sustain it. But the health duty is owed to individuals and the industrial duty to a sector, and where they collide the individual should win. The practical resolution is that governments should stop promoting consumption volume and instead support the industry's value, quality and viability, which is where policy money actually earns its return.

## Conclusion

Governments should influence wine consumption, and the honest reason is that they bear the costs of it. They should do so with strength-based duty, a floor price on the cheapest alcohol, firm age and drink-driving law, controlled availability short of monopoly, and mandatory factual labelling — a package that concentrates on harmful drinking rather than on wine as a category. They should be far more restrained about advertising bans beyond content rules, about moralising campaigns, and about trade measures dressed as consumption policy. Wine is not a special case in what it does to the body. It is a partial special case in what it does to a landscape and a rural economy, and that difference should shape the design of intervention, never its existence.
