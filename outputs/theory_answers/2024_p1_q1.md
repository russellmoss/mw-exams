## What a must adjustment is, and which ones the vineyard can reach

A must adjustment is any correction of juice or must composition made between fruit reception and the end of fermentation: enrichment by chaptalisation or concentrated grape must; acidification with tartaric acid; deacidification with calcium or potassium carbonate; additions of assimilable nitrogen and thiamine; tannin, enzyme and fining additions; sulphur dioxide beyond routine protection; and water addition where it is legal. Each answers a compositional deficiency — potential alcohol, titratable acidity and pH, yeast assimilable nitrogen (YAN), phenolic ripeness, or fruit health. The vineyard cannot design out every one, and in a difficult vintage it should not try. But it sets the composition that arrives at the press, so I take the adjustments in turn and identify the vineyard levers behind each, moving from decisions taken once (site, variety, clone, rootstock, planting density, row orientation), through the structural (training system, canopy architecture, soil and nutrition management, cover cropping), to the seasonal (crop load, irrigation, spray programme, picking date and fruit handling).

## Potential alcohol: enrichment and de-alcoholisation

Site and variety choice do most of the work. Matching a variety's ripening period to the site's heat accumulation decides whether enrichment or alcohol removal becomes an annual habit; a late-ripening variety in a marginal climate will need chaptalising in most years, an early one in a warm region will arrive over-ripe. Clone and rootstock refine this — later-maturing clones and less vigorous rootstocks are chosen deliberately in warming regions to slow sugar accumulation. Within the season, sugar loading tracks leaf area relative to crop weight. Where potential alcohol is short, growers reduce crop through shoot thinning and green harvest, maximise light interception through row orientation, row spacing and canopy height, and protect leaf function with a rigorous spray programme so the fruit can hang. Where it is excessive the levers reverse: carrying a fuller crop, trimming shoots to cut leaf area, retaining lateral shade over the fruit zone or using shade cloth, and picking earlier — which then reintroduces the acid and phenolic problems below. Elevation and cooler aspects address the same problem at the planting stage.

## Acidity and pH

Acidification is near-universal in warm regions and has two vineyard causes: malic degradation in warm, exposed fruit, and potassium accumulation raising pH. Fruit-zone microclimate is the lever for malic acid; bunches shaded by retained lateral growth or sitting on the cooler side of a north–south row retain more acid than fully exposed ones, so leaf-removal timing and which side of the row is stripped matter more in a warm climate than the textbook instruction to expose fruit suggests. Potassium is a soil and nutrition question: avoiding potassium fertilisation on already-rich soils, using a competitive cover crop, and selecting rootstocks that take up potassium less readily all restrain must pH. Variety and site again dominate — planting acid-retentive varieties, or planting at altitude for diurnal range, prevents the problem rather than correcting it. Deacidification is the mirror case, answered by fruit exposure, crop reduction and later picking.

## Nitrogen: the most directly preventable addition

Nutrient additions are the adjustment the vineyard controls most precisely. Low YAN causes sluggish or stuck fermentations and reductive sulphides, and its causes are predictable: nitrogen-poor soils, severe water deficit, a grass sward competing at flowering, and high yields diluting a fixed supply. Managing soil nitrogen with compost or a legume cover crop, mowing or cultivating the sward before the vine's demand peaks, and holding moderate vine water status will lift YAN into the workable range; around 150 mg/L is the working threshold below which supplementation is normally needed. Foliar nitrogen applied near veraison raises must YAN reliably and is in effect a vineyard-executed nutrient addition. The opposite error is equally real — excess nitrogen gives vigour, shading and rot.

## Tannin, colour and enzyme additions

Oenological tannin, colour-stabilising additions and maceration enzymes are bought to compensate for phenolic immaturity or dilution. The vineyard answers are crop load matched to vine capacity, moderate water deficit from veraison to restrain berry size and raise the skin-to-pulp ratio, early fruit-zone exposure to build thicker skins, and evenness of ripening — which depends on uniform bud load, shoot thinning and, at planting, uniform vine material and soil. Heterogeneous fruit is the commonest hidden cause of a perceived extraction problem, and the fix is picking in passes or by zone, not an addition.

## Fruit health: sulphur dioxide, fining and enzymes

Rot forces sulphur dioxide, fining and often enzymes, and laccase-affected fruit cannot be fully rescued. Canopy openness and airflow, disciplined spray timing, bunch-zone leaf removal after fruit set, vigour control through cover cropping and restrained irrigation, and clonal selection for looser bunches all reduce it. Delivery matters too: intact, cool fruit — night harvesting, shallow bins, short transport, whole bunches where the style allows — arrives with less oxidation and needs less protective addition.

## Conclusion

The vineyard's power over must composition is real but hierarchical. Site, variety, clone and rootstock set the envelope and are the most powerful levers precisely because they are the least reversible; canopy, soil and nutrition management shift composition within it; seasonal decisions fine-tune. Nitrogen, potassium-driven pH and fruit health can very largely be designed out; potential alcohol and acidity can be moved but not eliminated in an extreme vintage. The decision is finally economic: for a high-volume wine, a tartaric addition costs a fraction of the viticulture that would avoid it, and adjusting is the correct answer.
