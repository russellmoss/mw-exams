## Scope: transparency means price discovery, not publicity

I take market transparency to mean the near-zero cost at which any participant can now discover three things: what a given wine sells for anywhere in the world, what it is judged to be worth, and who holds stock of it. Wine-Searcher and Liv-ex do this for price, Vivino and CellarTracker for crowd judgement, the critics' sites for expert judgement, and social media acts as the distribution layer that pushes all of it in front of consumers who were not looking for it.

That distinction matters, because a producer's Instagram account is publicity, not transparency. I therefore say very little about social-media activation and concentrate on what price discovery has done to supply, to demand and above all to pricing — and then take a position on who has captured the value.

## Supply

Transparency has shortened the chain and made it policeable. A producer can now see the shelf price of its own wine in every market it sells to, which means it can see exactly what each intermediary is taking, and can see parallel trade the moment it appears. The response has been tighter allocation control and, in the United States especially, a decisive move into direct-to-consumer selling. Producers no longer need a distributor to be findable.

It has also brought new supply into the market. A decade ago, reaching a global audience required a distributor in each market. Etna, the Swartland, the Jura and the revived Georgian qvevri producers went from obscure to allocated largely through sommeliers, critics and social media rather than through conventional distribution. The same is true at the other end: bulk-market price reporting has made bulk wine values visible to growers who previously only knew what one broker told them.

## Demand

The effect on demand is two-sided, and I think this is where most analysis stops too early.

Search-driven discovery is winner-takes-most. A high aggregate rating compounds, because it raises the wine in the search results that generate the next tranche of ratings. That mechanism built Whispering Angel's dominance of premium rosé and it explains why a handful of names absorb a disproportionate share of demand in every newly opened market.

Simultaneously, demand has fragmented at the tail. A consumer in Melbourne can find, price and buy Trousseau from the Jura. The middle is what has hollowed out: the competent, unremarkable, unrated regional wine that used to sell because it was the only thing on the shelf.

## Pricing

This is the heart of it. A consumer standing in a shop or sitting at a restaurant table can check a wine's price against every other listing in the world in seconds, and that single fact has changed pricing behaviour at every level.

**Retailers** have lost pricing power on known-value items. National price bands have converged, and the margin on any wine a consumer can name has compressed. The most important commercial response is own label and exclusivity — supermarket tiers such as Tesco Finest, the private-label ranges that carry Aldi and Lidl, merchant-exclusive cuvées, and Naked Wines' model of funding winemakers to make labels that exist nowhere else. All of these do one job: they remove the comparator.

Restaurants have lost the ability to defend the convention of pricing a bottle at a multiple of its retail value -- often cited at around three times -- because the guest can see the retail value. The better operators have moved to cash-margin pricing, to growers' wines bought direct that appear on no other list, and to by-the-glass programmes where the comparison is harder to make.

**Producers** now set release prices in public. Bordeaux en primeur is the clearest case: a release priced above the visible back-vintage market on Liv-ex simply does not sell, and the campaign is now negotiated in front of the customer. Producers must also manage price coherence across markets, because with duty and tax differentials visible, the cheapest market sets the reference price everywhere.

The important counter-case is that transparency is not uniformly deflationary. Where supply is genuinely scarce, transparency revealed how far below the clearing price releases had been set, and producers in Burgundy, Napa and Champagne have raised prices to capture margin that was previously going to the secondary market.

## Who benefits

My view is that transparency has redistributed margin to the two ends of the chain and to the platforms in the middle, and that the traditional intermediary is the loser.

The **consumer** is the clear winner below roughly £15 a bottle, where wines are comparable, substitutable and priced against each other in public.

The **producer** is the winner at the scarce end, because it now knows its wine's clearing price and can either take that margin at release or sell direct and take all of it.

The **intermediary** — distributor, importer, traditional merchant — is squeezed from both sides, and survives only by owning something that cannot be price-compared: an exclusive label, a locked allocation, guaranteed provenance and storage, or genuine advice.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that the largest single beneficiary may be neither party but the platforms themselves. Wine-Searcher, Liv-ex and Vivino -- which has claimed a very large user base, in the tens of millions -- monetise the very transparency that erodes their customers' margins.
