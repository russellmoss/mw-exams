## Why fining agents are used

Paper three concerns wine after fermentation, so I treat fining here as an operation on finished wine rather than on juice. A fining agent is added to bind a target fraction of the wine by adsorption or by charge attraction, and is then removed with its bound material by settling and racking or filtration. It has two primary purposes. The first is clarification: removing colloidal material that scatters light so the wine is bright in glass. The second, and more important commercially, is stabilisation: removing the heat-unstable proteins that would throw a haze in a warm warehouse, and the unstable phenolic and colouring matter that would deposit later. Beyond those, fining is used to modify colour — reducing browning in an oxidised white, pinking in a rosé, excess anthocyanin in a wine too dark for its category — and to adjust astringency and bitterness by removing the harsher tannin fractions.

The second reason is time and therefore money. A wine left to clarify by gravity alone may take months and will not reliably become stable at all; fining collapses that into days. That releases tank space, reduces the load on filtration and so the cost of media and the losses to it, and lets a producer hit a fixed bottling and shipping window rather than a hoped-for one. The saving is real for the producer and is ultimately reflected in the price of an inexpensive wine, where cellar time is a significant share of cost. The counterweight throughout is that every fining removes more than its target, so the operative discipline is a bench trial establishing the minimum effective dose.

## a. Entry-level South African Chenin Blanc

The dominant factor is that this wine will be shipped in bulk or in bottle to a distant market, sold within a year, displayed in clear glass and often drunk cold. Heat stability is therefore non-negotiable: a protein haze appearing in a supermarket in July is a returns problem, which is why retailer specifications require heat-test certificates. Bentonite is the answer, because it is the only widely used agent that removes the unstable pathogenesis-related proteins; it is also cheap, mineral and vegan-compatible, which suits export markets.

The factors that shape how it is used are volume loss and aroma. Bentonite lees can account for several per cent of the volume treated, which at this price point matters, so the wine is trialled for minimum effective dose and the lees recovered rather than dumped. Bentonite also strips thiols and esters, and at entry level the fruit is the product, so under-dosing and re-testing beats a safety-margin dose. Where the wine shows browning or hard phenolics from machine harvesting, PVPP is added at a low rate for the same reason. Time also counts: this wine has to move, so speed and predictability outrank finesse.

## b. DOCG Barolo

The factors here run the other way. Barolo requires a minimum of 38 months of ageing, at least 18 of them in wood, so the wine has years of settling and repeated racking in large cask, and clarification is largely achieved without any agent. The price supports the tank occupancy. Consequently the modern default is to bottle unfined, or unfined and unfiltered, on the view that the colloids removed by fining are the ones that carry mid-palate texture and stabilise colour over a long bottle life.

If an agent is used, the purpose is not clarity but tannin. Nebbiolo is high in tannin and can be aggressive young, so the traditional Piedmontese choice is egg-white albumen, which binds the larger, harsher tannin polymers relatively selectively and is gentle on colour. Gelatine is more aggressive and can strip colour and body, which is why it is generally avoided in a wine whose colour is already pale. Cost of the agent is irrelevant at this price; the cost that matters is the risk of taking out what the wine needs at twenty years. The offsetting factor is labelling: egg and milk derivatives are declarable allergens unless residues fall below the regulatory threshold, and using them forecloses a vegan claim.

## c. Vegan AOC Côtes de Provence rosé

Two constraints bind. The vegan claim removes gelatine, isinglass, casein and albumen outright, and if the producer is certified the audit trail matters as much as the residue; chitosan is acceptable only where it is of fungal rather than crustacean origin. The style constraint is colour: a very pale, precise salmon shade is the commercial signature of the appellation, and a wine even slightly too deep or showing bronzing is off-category.

So the choice is bentonite for protein stability, PVPP to remove browning precursors and excess anthocyanin, and plant proteins — pea or potato — as the vegan substitute for animal clarifiers. Activated carbon will correct colour decisively but strips aroma with it, so it is a bench-trial-only, last-resort dose. Timing decides the rest: this wine must be bottled by early spring for the summer season, so fining is chosen for speed and reliability as much as for effect.

## Conclusion

The agent follows from what must be removed, what must be preserved, and how much time and money the wine's price supports.
