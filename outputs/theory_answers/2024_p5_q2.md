## What "recent climate change" means here

I take recent climate change to mean the anthropogenic shift in climate observable over roughly the last four decades — the period in which the current generation of vineyards was planted and the current generation of drinkers formed its tastes. It is not simply warming. It is warming plus increased variability: higher growing-season means, earlier budburst and compressed harvests, more frequent heat events above the mid-thirties, redistributed rainfall and deeper drought, spring frost made more damaging because budburst arrives earlier, and a higher incidence of hail and wildfire.

The evidence is not in serious dispute, and an answer that pretends otherwise is not credible. Global mean surface temperature is now of the order of 1.2 degrees C above pre-industrial levels, and growing-season temperatures in many European wine regions have risen by roughly 1.5 to 2 degrees C since the early 1980s — wine regions warm faster than the global mean because they sit in mid-latitude continental interiors. The consequences are legible in the record: harvest dates in Bordeaux and Burgundy have advanced by roughly two to three weeks over four decades, and typical alcohol levels in dry red wine have risen by roughly one degree over the same period. I recall these as approximate figures rather than precise ones, but the direction and rough magnitude are firmly established.

## Which "wine lovers"

The question is about drinkers, not producers, so I read "wine lovers" as consumers — though production costs pass through to them, which is where much of the answer lies. I distinguish three segments, because the answer differs sharply between them: the everyday buyer, who purchases below roughly the middle of the retail range and mostly by brand, variety or occasion; the engaged enthusiast, who buys by region and producer, follows vintages and cellars modestly; and the collector-investor, who buys allocated wine at the top of the market. I will argue the pros and cons across the supply chain, then resolve the verdict segment by segment.

## The case that it has been good

**Reliability of ripeness in marginal regions.** The single strongest argument. Regions that historically failed in a third of vintages now ripen fruit in most of them. Chaptalisation in the Mosel, Chablis and Champagne is far less routine than it was; the old vintage lottery in Bordeaux, where an enthusiast could buy a wine that never came round, has largely gone. For the enthusiast this is a real and continuing gain: fewer disappointments, and less of the technical intervention that used to substitute for ripeness.

**New regions and new choice.** English sparkling wine is now a commercially serious category that barely existed forty years ago; Tasmania, Nova Scotia, southern Sweden and Denmark, high-altitude Spain and Patagonia have all become viable or better. Consumers have more origins, more styles and more genuine competition than any previous generation.

**Winemaking and style.** Warmer fruit gave winemakers softer tannins and riper aromatics, and the last two decades of technique have been a response to it: earlier picking, whole-bunch inclusion, gentler extraction, cooler ferments, less new oak, less filtration. The generous, approachable red style that resulted has recruited drinkers who found older, leaner wines forbidding. Faults are rarer than they were; the quality floor across the entire market has risen.

**Adaptation has widened the palette.** Bordeaux authorised a small group of additional varieties, including Touriga Nacional and Marselan, for its regional appellations in 2021. Growers are moving to higher altitudes and cooler aspects, and reviving late-ripening local varieties. That produces variety the consumer never had.

## The case that it has been bad

**Sugar and phenolic ripeness have decoupled.** Sugar accumulates faster than tannins and aromatics mature, so a grower chooses between under-ripe flavours and excessive alcohol. Acidity falls and pH rises, which costs freshness, digestibility and ageing security. The wines most damaged are exactly the ones enthusiasts prize: low-alcohol Mosel Kabinett has become genuinely hard to make, and lean, structured styles have broadly given way to riper ones.

**Loss of typicity.** As regional signatures converge on ripeness, the regional distinctions that make wine worth learning about blur. Warm-vintage Burgundy and warm-vintage northern Rhône are closer to each other than they were, which is a loss of information and of pleasure.

**Extremes destroy vintages outright.** The 2021 spring frost in France caused one of the largest single-vintage crop losses on record; the 2020 wildfires on the US West Coast caused widespread smoke taint, with some producers declining to release a vintage wine; drought has cut yields repeatedly in Chile, South Africa, Spain and Australia. Volatility of this order is worse for a consumer than a warmer average, because it removes wines from the market altogether.

**Choice narrows at the bottom.** Volatile yields mean volatile supply. Producers respond by rationalising ranges, dropping marginal cuvées and blending across sites and vintages. The everyday buyer experiences climate change mainly as fewer options at the same price.

## Costs, packaging and logistics

The financial chain is where the harm reaches most drinkers. Frost protection, irrigation infrastructure, netting, water, energy, heat-related labour restrictions and crop insurance have all added cost, and short vintages spread fixed costs over fewer bottles. Transport and glass have inflated for reasons partly climate-related, including the energy intensity of glass production and carbon pricing on it. Heat in transit has forced reefer containers and seasonal shipping windows on routes that once needed neither, adding cost and complexity.

Some of this is genuinely good for consumers: carbon pressure has driven lighter bottles, bag-in-box, cans and bulk shipping with bottling at destination, all of which lower the shelf price and, done properly, the risk of heat damage. But the net effect is upward pressure on price, concentrated at entry level where margins are thinnest, and that is where most wine lovers buy.

## Verdict

Yes, it could be argued — but only for a minority, and only for now. For the collector and for the enthusiast whose tastes run to northern European whites and sparkling wine, the last forty years have been a net gain: better ripeness, fewer failed vintages, new regions, and asset values supported by scarcity. That is a real argument and it should not be dismissed.

For wine lovers taken as a whole, I do not accept it. The everyday buyer, who is most of the market, has gained a marginally higher quality floor and lost on price, choice and stylistic diversity. More fundamentally, the benefits are the front end of a curve. They arise where warming has moved a region towards its optimum, and every region continues past that optimum. Warming that was a gift to Champagne in the 1990s is a problem for the Languedoc now and will be a problem for Champagne later. An argument that rests on being temporarily on the right side of a moving line is not an argument that climate change has been good; it is an observation that the harm has not arrived everywhere at once.
