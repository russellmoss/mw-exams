## The criteria, and how I weight them

I am choosing for humanity, not for my cellar, and that changes the answer. The test is not which two varieties make the wines I would most miss - on that test I would save Riesling and Pinot Noir and be wrong - but which two would leave humanity best equipped to rebuild a global wine culture from nothing.

Six criteria, in the order I weight them:

1. **Adaptability across climates and soils.** With only two varieties left, they must be plantable almost everywhere, or whole continents lose viticulture entirely. This dominates everything else.
2. **Breadth of style from one grape.** Two varieties must reconstruct still, sparkling, sweet, oaked, unoaked, early-drinking and ageworthy wine between them. A one-register variety, however fine, is a catastrophic choice.
3. **Economic weight and dependency.** Plantings and the number of regional economies that survive or fail with the variety.
4. **Viticultural and vinification robustness.** Disease behaviour, tolerance of a broad picking window, forgiveness in the cellar - because the survivors will include growers with no tradition and no expertise.
5. **Longevity and quality ceiling.** Whether the variety can produce wines worth ageing, which is what gives wine its cultural and archival value.
6. **Consumer recognition.** Rebuilding demand is as necessary as rebuilding supply, and a name people already know is a real asset.

Disease resistance sits fourth deliberately. Neither of my choices is resistant to anything, and I will not pretend otherwise; in a scenario defined by a disease that destroys all varieties, resistance to today's pathogens is a weak predictor, and I would rather preserve genetic and stylistic breadth.

## The white shortlist

**Riesling** has the highest quality ceiling and the greatest longevity of any white, transmits site with unmatched clarity, and spans dry to nobly sweet. It fails on criterion one: it needs a cool climate and a long hang, and it would abandon everything from Andalusia to Australia's warm interior. Its plantings are small and consumer resistance to its sweetness ambiguity is well documented. Superb wine, poor insurance policy.

**Sauvignon Blanc** is commercially the strongest white of the past thirty years and adapts from the Loire to Marlborough to Chile. But it is a single-register grape: intensely aromatic, rarely ageworthy, no serious sparkling role and only a narrow sweet one. On criterion two it fails badly.

**Chenin Blanc** is a genuinely strong candidate and the one I nearly chose. It makes dry, off-dry, sweet, sparkling and long-lived wines, and it succeeds in the Loire and the Cape - two very different climates. It loses on economic weight and recognition, and its high acidity and late ripening restrict it more than Chardonnay.

**Airen** still occupies a very large area of central Spain and would win on area alone in some accountings. Its quality ceiling disqualifies it, and its principal use is distillation. It is a useful reminder that economic weight without quality is not a case.

**Muscat and Ugni Blanc** carry claims of a different kind - Muscat as the ancestral aromatic family and a dual-purpose table and drying grape, Ugni Blanc as the base of Cognac and Armagnac. Preserving distillation culture is a real consideration for humanity.

## The claim I take most seriously

The hardest argument against my choice is not another wine grape at all. Sultana is among the most widely planted grapes on earth once table grapes and drying varieties are counted, and it feeds people. If I am genuinely choosing for humanity rather than for wine drinkers, a variety that produces food has a claim that a wine grape does not.

I reject it, but on reasoning rather than reflex. The question is set in a world where all varieties are dying and two may be saved; the sultana's calorific contribution is replaceable by other crops, whereas the cultural, agricultural and economic system that wine supports is not replaceable at all. Saving the food grape saves calories humanity can get elsewhere. Saving the wine grapes saves something it cannot.

## My white: Chardonnay

Chardonnay wins on criterion one decisively: it ripens reliably from Chablis to the Adelaide Hills to Casablanca, buds and behaves predictably, and yields commercially on a wide range of soils. It wins on criterion two more emphatically than any other white in existence - it makes taut mineral wine, rich oaked wine, world-class traditional-method sparkling as one of the principal permitted varieties of Champagne, and botrytised wine at the margin. It carries enormous economic weight and universal consumer recognition, and its best examples age for decades.

Its genetics add to the case: DNA analysis in the 1990s showed Chardonnay to be a natural cross of Pinot and Gouais Blanc, so it preserves the expressed characteristics of a lineage that sits behind a great many European varieties.

I concede its weaknesses honestly. It buds early and is frost-exposed; it is susceptible to powdery mildew and, with thin skins, to botrytis; and it is aromatically neutral, which is why it depends on site and winemaking. That neutrality, though, is precisely what makes it the right insurance policy - a blank grape is a versatile one.

## My black: Cabernet Sauvignon

The same logic, applied to red. The OIV's 2017 varietal survey placed Cabernet Sauvignon as the world's most planted wine grape, at roughly 340,000 hectares, ahead of Merlot, and it succeeds in Bordeaux, Napa, Coonawarra, Maipo, Stellenbosch, Bolgheri and Ningxia - the widest climatic span of any quality black variety.

Against the alternatives: **Pinot Noir** is Chardonnay's natural partner and makes the greatest red wines by many judgements, but it is thin-skinned, rot-prone, mutation-unstable and confined to a narrow cool band - a disastrous choice under criterion one. **Syrah** is the strongest rival, adaptable from Hermitage to Barossa with two distinct registers, and I would rank it second. **Grenache** offers real drought and heat tolerance, which matters in a warming world, but oxidises readily and struggles to hold acidity and colour. **Merlot** is more plantable and earlier-ripening but has a lower ceiling and less structure. **Tempranillo and Sangiovese** are regionally indispensable and globally marginal.

Cabernet's advantages are practical. Thick skins give it disease tolerance and a wide harvest window; late budding avoids spring frost; small berries give colour, tannin and antioxidant structure, so its wines survive poor cellars, long shipping and decades of ageing - the single most useful property in a rebuilding world. It blends generously, which is how a two-variety world would create diversity. Cabernet Sauvignon was shown in 1996-97 to be a natural cross of Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc, tying my two choices into an overlapping genetic pool. And it is the most recognised red wine name on earth.

Its weakness is real: it needs sufficient heat, and unripe Cabernet is herbaceous and hard. In a cooler world it would be the wrong choice, which is the risk I am consciously taking.

## Conclusion

Chardonnay and Cabernet Sauvignon. I would lose Riesling's transparency, Pinot's delicacy, Nebbiolo, Sangiovese and Chenin, and the wine world would be poorer and duller for it. But the question asks what to preserve for humanity, and humanity needs adaptability, breadth of style, keeping quality and recognisability far more than it needs my favourite bottle. These two can be planted almost anywhere, make almost every style between them, age, travel, and be sold to people who have never heard of the appellation - and from that base, everything else could eventually be rebuilt.
