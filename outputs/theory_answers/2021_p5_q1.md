## Two definitions, and the test I will apply

A pioneer is not simply someone doing something unusual. I define a pioneer as a person or institution that does something materially new **and** whose example is then taken up by others, changing the default practice of a significant part of the trade. Three tests follow — novelty, adoption, and consequence — and consequence has to show up in something countable: hectares planted, cases shipped, rules rewritten, costs removed. Someone who is first and whom nobody follows is an eccentric. Someone who is followed but was not first is a populariser. Both are routinely mislabelled.

By "wine industry" I mean the whole chain: plant breeding and nurseries, growing, production, packaging, logistics, regulation, route to market and communication. Most answers to this question name winemakers only, which is where the romance sits and very little of the leverage.

My argument is that today's real pioneers are mostly institutional and technical rather than charismatic, that they cluster at the two ends of the chain — plant material and route to market — and that several figures the trade calls pioneers fail the adoption test outright.

## Plant material: the breeders

The most consequential new thing being done in wine is the reconstruction of what a vine is. INRAE's ResDur programme released fungus-resistant varieties such as Floreal, Voltis, Artaban and Vidoc; the German and Swiss institutes had been breeding PIWI varieties for decades before that; and Andrew Walker's Pierce's disease-resistant selections at UC Davis address a threat no spray programme can manage.

Why they qualify: adoption is measurable in regulation, which is the hardest thing in wine to move. Champagne admitted Voltis on a trial basis, and Bordeaux admitted a short list of new varieties for the same reason. A variety that reduces fungicide passes cuts cost, diesel, soil compaction and operator exposure simultaneously — one intervention, four benefits, and it works at commodity scale rather than only at the top of the market.

The honest qualification is that the planted area remains small. But regulation moving in advance of demand is exactly the signal that distinguishes pioneering from novelty.

## Closures and packaging: the clearest case in living memory

The Clare Valley Riesling producers who bottled their 2000 vintage under screwcap, and the New Zealand Screwcap Initiative that followed in 2001, meet all three tests better than anyone else I can name. Within a decade the closure of choice for an entire hemisphere had changed, and random oxidation and cork taint were removed from a whole category of wine. Standing behind them is the Australian Wine Research Institute, whose long-running closure trial from the late 1990s supplied the evidence that made the decision defensible rather than merely brave — a reminder that research institutes are pioneers too, and are almost never named as such.

The live frontier is now the container rather than the closure: lightweight glass, bag-in-box, cans, kegs and flat or paper bottles. The pioneers here are largely retailers and logistics buyers, not producers. A supermarket that sets a maximum bottle weight for its own-label supply shifts more glass in one buying round than any single winery will in its existence, because it changes a specification rather than a preference. The same is true of bulk shipping in flexitanks with bottling at destination, which took a substantial share of long-haul trade in under twenty years and removed more carbon and freight cost from the industry than every winery sustainability programme combined. Nobody's name is attached to it, which is my point about where pioneering actually happens.

## Systems: certification as a national instrument

Sustainable Winegrowing New Zealand now certifies the overwhelming majority of the country's vineyard area, and export customers treat it as a condition of listing. The pioneering move was not the agronomy; it was changing the unit of action from the estate to the country, so that sustainability became a market-access asset rather than a marketing claim. California's sustainability programmes and the various regional carbon calculators follow the same logic.

At the far end of the same spectrum, Tablas Creek in Paso Robles was certified under the Regenerative Organic scheme in 2020, which is standard-setting rather than commercially significant — a useful contrast, because it shows the difference between a pioneer of practice and a pioneer of scale.

## Route to market: where the economics actually changed

Naked Wines, whose customers pre-fund production, moved working capital risk off small producers and onto consumers who wanted the wine anyway. Whatever one thinks of the wines, the financing structure was genuinely new and it has been imitated. More broadly, direct-to-consumer selling and low-cost digital reach mean a producer of a few thousand cases can now find a global niche audience without a distributor's permission. That is the single largest change in how wine gets sold in my working lifetime, and it has no famous author.

## The critical part: who is not a pioneer

Natural wine is the clearest case. Almost every practice under the banner — ambient ferments, whole bunches, minimal sulphur, skin contact on whites, amphorae — is a revival of pre-industrial technique, not an invention. Its influence on mainstream cellar practice is real and its commercial footprint is trivial next to the attention it receives. Josko Gravner earns the title on style creation; the movement as a whole fails the consequence test.

English sparkling wine transplants an existing model to a new place. That is skilled, commercially interesting and climatically significant, but the varieties, the method, the equipment and the market positioning are all borrowed intact from Champagne. Doing an established thing well in a new latitude is achievement, not pioneering, and the distinction matters because the industry hands out the word for geography rather than for novelty.

Two further categories fail the test for the opposite reason. Celebrity-fronted brands and the newer generation of drinks-industry entrants are exercising existing marketing technique on an existing product; and the many producers now described as pioneers of regenerative or low-intervention farming are, with a handful of exceptions, adopting agronomy developed outside wine altogether. Adopting well is valuable. It is not the same claim.

The inversion is more interesting. Sacha Lichine bought Château d'Esclans in 2006 and built Whispering Angel into a brand of several million bottles a year, in the process establishing pale dry rosé as a year-round premium category rather than a summer afterthought. The trade is sniffy about it. On novelty of proposition, breadth of imitation and measurable commercial consequence, it outscores almost every cult producer of the same period.

And some of today's most consequential pioneers are not in wine at all: the ready-to-drink, no-and-low and premium spirits operators who have taken wine's occasions while the industry argued about oak.

## Conclusion

The real pioneers in today's wine industry are the breeders at INRAE, Geisenheim and Davis; the Clare Valley and New Zealand screwcap groups with the AWRI behind them; the architects of national certification in New Zealand; the retail buyers rewriting packaging specifications; and the builders of customer-funded and direct routes to market. What they share is that their work is adopted by people who never met them. That is the only definition of pioneering that survives contact with an industry this large.
