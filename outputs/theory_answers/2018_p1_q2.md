## Scope

Climate change is not simply warming: growers face a rising mean and, more damagingly, rising variability around it. The question asks what growers now *do* differently, so I take three regions — cool, moderate and warm — and set out the changed practice in each. I organise by region rather than by practice, because the same warming demands opposite responses in Chablis and the Barossa, and that contrast is the answer.

## Chablis: a cool region whose problem is volatility, not heat

Warming has advanced budburst by days to weeks, which lengthens the window in which a spring frost can destroy a crop. Chablis growers have therefore changed the pruning calendar: winter pruning is pushed later, and double pruning — a rough mechanical pre-prune followed by a late hand finish — delays budburst on the retained buds. Where the site allows, the fruiting wire has been raised, since cold air pools at ground level. Above that sit capital defences: aspersion, which sprinkles water so that latent heat of fusion holds the bud at zero as ice forms; candles and straw burners; and wind machines to break the inversion layer. Hail has become the second recurring loss, answered by netting where permitted and, more often, by insurance.

Warmer summers have brought their own reversal. Chaptalisation, once routine in Chablis, is now needed rarely, and acidity retention has become the priority — so harvest dates are pulled earlier and picking runs on acid and pH as much as on sugar. Canopy work has changed with it: growers now leave more leaf on the afternoon side and trim later, where the region's inherited practice was to open the fruit zone as widely as possible. Wetter, warmer springs have raised downy mildew pressure, so spray programmes start earlier and are timed on infection models rather than on the calendar.

The economics are brutal: frost protection is a capital cost carried every year against a loss that arrives in some years, and the insurance premium rises with each event. That is why the practice change here is defensive and capital-intensive.

## Bordeaux: a moderate region where the constraint is legal

Bordeaux's problem is drought and heat spikes on top of earlier ripening. Because irrigation is prohibited in the appellations other than by derogation, water management is soil and plant management: cover crops sown to compete with vine vigour in wet years and ploughed out in dry ones, mulching to hold soil moisture, and — the more durable change — rootstock choice, with deeper-rooting, drought-tolerant stocks replacing shallower ones at replanting. Canopy practice has undergone the same reversal as Chablis for a different reason: systematic leaf removal on the west side is now avoided because sunburn, not under-ripeness, is the risk, and trellises are being raised to increase leaf area per unit of fruit so that sugar accumulation does not outrun phenolic ripeness.

The distinctive change is legal. Faced with alcohols that its style cannot carry, Bordeaux moved to admit a short list of additional varieties, including Touriga Nacional, Marselan and Petit Manseng. This is the point most answers miss: in appellation Europe the binding constraint on adaptation is often the rulebook, and the practice that has to change first is the regulation governing plant material, yields, irrigation and picking dates.

Harvest logistics have changed too — picking earlier in the day or at night, and picking parcel by parcel over a longer window, which raises the labour bill in a region already short of pickers.

## Barossa Valley: a warm region managing water and heat

Here the constraint is physical rather than legal, since Australia does not restrict variety by region. Water is the first practice: drip irrigation scheduled against soil moisture probes and vine water potential rather than a fixed programme, regulated deficit irrigation to stress the vine at a chosen phenological stage, under-vine mulch, and the removal of mid-row cover crops where they compete for scarce water. Water itself is now traded and priced, so an irrigation decision is a cash decision.

Canopy management has been reoriented from exposure to shading: fruit is deliberately kept in dappled shade, leaf removal is minimal and done on the eastern side only, and sprawl or vertical shoot positioning is chosen for the shade it casts rather than for airflow. Sunburn and berry shrivel, not botrytis, are the losses being managed. Pest and disease pressure has shifted with the drier, hotter season — less downy and powdery mildew, more mealybug and mite pressure — so spray programmes have been rebuilt around different targets and shorter windows.

The strategic change is plant material: Mediterranean varieties planted alongside Shiraz and Grenache, and the deliberate retention of dry-grown old bush vines whose deep root systems ride out drought — the reason the Barossa Old Vine Charter has commercial as well as heritage value.

## Comparison

The three regions face the same trend and change opposite practices. Chablis spends capital to protect against volatility at the start of the season; the Barossa spends operating cost on water and shade to protect fruit at the end of it; Bordeaux must first change its law before it can change its vineyard. The common thread is that every one of these is a reversal of received practice — pruning later, shading rather than exposing, watering less rather than more — which is why growers cannot simply do what they have always done more carefully.
