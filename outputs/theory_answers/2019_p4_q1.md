## Terms and scope

An **own label** carries the retailer's own brand name or a name it owns outright and can move between suppliers. An **exclusive label** is a brand owned by a producer or agent but listed by one retailer only. Both hand the retailer control of the proposition; they differ in who owns the intellectual property and therefore who bears the risk. I treat them together because the commercial logic is the same, and note the difference only where it changes the argument.

## Why retailers do it

**1. Margin.** The dominant reason. Own label typically earns the retailer several percentage points more gross margin than the branded equivalent at the same shelf price, because there is no brand owner's marketing budget, sales force or profit to fund. In a category where retail wine margins are thin and promotional depth is punishing, that gap is the whole business case.

**2. Escape from price comparison.** A branded wine is a directly comparable SKU: a shopper or a competitor can price-match it, and the discounters use exactly that to define the market price. An exclusive label cannot be compared, so the retailer sets and holds its own price architecture and defends its promotional calendar. Marks and Spencer, which has sold almost nothing but own label for decades, is the long-standing demonstration; the discounters are the extreme case, with a wine range that is almost entirely exclusive.

**3. Differentiation and footfall.** Wine is one of the few grocery categories where the retailer can genuinely create something a shopper cannot buy elsewhere. Tesco Finest, Sainsbury's Taste the Difference and Aldi's Exquisite Collection are reasons to choose a store, not just products within it, and Costco's Kirkland Signature has demonstrated the same across categories.

**4. The category is unusually hospitable to it.** Wine has weak brand loyalty and few brands with real pulling power. Shoppers navigate by country, region, variety and price, not by brand, so substituting the retailer's name for a brand costs less consumer confidence here than it would in beer or soft drinks.

**5. Supply chain control.** Own label lets the retailer buy in bulk, ship in bulk and bottle at destination, capturing the freight and packaging saving itself; it also lets it switch origin between vintages when currency, harvest size or tariffs move against a source. Bulk shipping economics are a substantial part of the margin advantage and are only available to the party that owns the label.

**6. Speed on trend.** Vegan, organic, lower-alcohol, canned, no-and-low: a retailer can brief and list an own-label answer in months, where waiting for a brand owner to develop one takes years and cedes the category-management initiative.

## Is it good for the category? A SWOT

**Strengths.** The quality floor at entry level has risen materially; a supermarket's technologists impose specifications and audits that many small producers would not otherwise meet. Consumers get more wine for the money, and access improves. Producers with volume and no marketing budget gain a route to a mass market they could never reach with a brand.

**Weaknesses.** Value migrates to the retailer. The producer becomes an anonymous price-taker whose margin is compressed at every negotiation and who builds no equity of its own — so when the contract moves, the business has nothing left. Category-wide, brand-building investment falls, and with it the demand creation that grows total consumption rather than reshuffling it.

**Opportunities.** Exclusive labels done as genuine collaboration — a named grower's cuvée, a region-specific range with the appellation front and centre — can build regional equity even where brand equity is denied, and give the producer a reference the trade recognises. Own label is also the cheapest laboratory the category has for new formats and new styles.

**Threats.** The brand ladder disappears. Brands are what trade a shopper from £7 to £12; without them the trade-up mechanism rests entirely on the retailer's tiering, which the retailer will operate for its own margin, not the category's. Concentration accelerates: fewer, larger suppliers can service the specification and audit burden. And origin transparency is weakened, which is a reputational exposure the category does not need.

## My position

For the shopper and for the retailer, this is straightforwardly good: better liquid at the price, and a defensible margin. For producers it is a bargain that suits high-volume, low-cost operations and slowly hollows out everyone else.

For the category as a whole I think it is, on balance, negative — not because own label is bad wine, but because a category with almost no brands has almost no one investing in making people want wine rather than gin or hard seltzer. Retailers optimise their own shelf; nobody optimises the category. The counter-argument is real: brands in wine have rarely earned their premium, and the retailer has proved a more reliable guarantor of quality at £7 than most brand owners. But the evidence from other grocery categories is that own-label dominance goes with commoditisation and deflation, and wine cannot afford to be commoditised further. The healthy end-state is a balance — own label owning value and the entry tier, a smaller number of genuinely strong third-party brands doing the demand creation above it — and the trend is running past that balance rather than toward it.
