## What a négociant is, and how long it has been there

A Burgundian négociant buys grapes, must or finished wine and sells it under its own name. The format has three sub-forms that behave very differently: the négociant-éleveur, which vinifies purchased fruit; the éleveur proper, which buys wine in cask and raises and bottles it; and the négociant-manipulant scale buyer working in regional appellations. None of these is a courtier — a broker who matches seller to buyer for commission and never takes title — and none is a domaine, which bottles wine from vines it owns or leases. The distinction matters commercially because the négociant carries inventory risk and input-price risk that the courtier does not, and lacks the fixed fruit cost that the domaine enjoys.

The format is old, not new. The Beaune négoce was the mechanism by which Burgundy reached export markets for two centuries: Bouchard Père et Fils and Champy date from the eighteenth century, Bichot and Drouhin from the nineteenth. Domaine bottling is the recent development, spreading through the twentieth century and accelerating from the 1970s and 1980s — which is where the common error about négociant origins comes from. Even now a large share of Burgundy's crop never sees an estate bottling line: many growers sell grapes or bulk wine to the négoce or deliver to a co-operative, and that structural fact is the raw material of the whole business.

## The evolution, and what drove it

**Micro-négoce arrived.** The most visible change of the last fifteen years is the proliferation of small négociant labels founded by trained winemakers without land — Benjamin Leroux and Lucien Le Moine among the established names, with a long tail behind them. The driver is land price: Côte d'Or vineyard became unbuyable on a winemaker's earnings, so buying fruit is now the only realistic entry route. The format's virtue is that it needs a cellar, not a balance sheet.

**Established houses grew their négoce arms.** Where a house owns vineyard, it uses that as the quality core and buys fruit for volume and range. Jadot, Faiveley, Bichot and Drouhin have all expanded holdings while continuing to buy, and I treat their owned vineyard as part of the négociant model rather than as a domaine case: it is a hedge against input cost, not a separate business.

**Supply became the binding constraint.** A run of small vintages — hail in the Côte de Beaune in the early 2010s, the April frosts of 2016 and 2021 — took fruit off the market repeatedly. Growers stopped selling surplus, and grape and bulk prices rose sharply. The négociant format is uniquely exposed to this, because its cost of goods is priced by scarcity it cannot influence.

**Contracting changed shape.** The response has been to secure fruit without buying land: multi-year grape contracts, métayage and fermage arrangements, and equity in growers' operations. Négociants now compete for supply rather than for customers, which is a reversal of the historic position.

**Capital arrived and consolidated the top.** Groups have bought houses — Boisset's long accumulation, and Bouchard Père et Fils passed to Artémis Domaines in 2022. So the population is a barbell: many more small négociants than fifteen years ago, and greater concentration of value in a handful of well-capitalised houses. The number of négociants has not fallen.

## Are current conditions financially sustainable?

Not uniformly, and the split follows the structure above.

The pure-play négociant with no owned fruit faces a squeeze at both ends. Its input price is set by an appellation-scarce raw material bid up by better-capitalised competitors and by growers' own opportunity cost, while its selling price is capped by the consumer's ability to compare it with a domaine bottling of the same climat — which usually carries more prestige at similar money. Between 2015 and 2022 that gap was papered over by demand: Burgundy prices rose fast enough that expensive fruit was still profitable. Since 2023 the fine-wine market has cooled and Asian demand has weakened, so houses that contracted fruit at peak prices are selling into a softer market, and higher interest rates have made the multi-year inventory financing that élevage requires materially more expensive. That combination — peak input cost, softening output price, dearer working capital — is not sustainable, and I would expect failures and absorptions among mid-sized, vineyard-light houses.

Three models remain sound. Integrated houses with substantial owned or leased vineyard internalise the fruit cost and can average their margin across the range. Micro-négociants with negligible overhead, allocation-based selling and no salaried staff can pay high fruit prices because they have almost no fixed cost to cover. And volume houses working in the Mâconnais, the Châtillonnais and regional appellations still buy fruit at economic prices, because the scarcity premium is concentrated in the Côte d'Or.

The format itself is not in question — it is the mechanism by which most Burgundy reaches the market and it will remain so. What is in question is the vineyard-light, Côte d'Or-focused, inventory-heavy version of it. Restoring its economics needs a run of normal-sized vintages, longer and more secure fruit contracts, and a willingness to move down the appellation ladder rather than defend a price the market has stopped paying.
