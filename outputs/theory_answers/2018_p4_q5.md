## My case in one line

Yes, wine should follow - but not everywhere. Below roughly the mid-price point, where the bottle communicates nothing the consumer values, the industry should move on format aggressively and is losing occasions every year it does not. Above that point it should deliberately not follow, because there the glass bottle is doing paid work. The interesting argument is not whether to innovate but where the line sits, and I spend most of this answer on that.

## Why the industry has been slow

Five causes, in descending order of weight.

First, the bottle is not just a container but a *guarantee*. It is inert, it ages the wine, and it signals authenticity in a category with a counterfeiting problem. No other alcoholic drink asks its package to protect a product for thirty years.

Second, the law. Rioja requires that the wine be bottled within the region, and Champagne's second fermentation must take place in the bottle in which the wine is sold. Appellation systems across Europe codify the package, so for a large share of the world's most valuable wine, format innovation is illegal rather than merely unattractive.

Third, structure. Wine is made by tens of thousands of small businesses with one production run a year. Beer and spirits innovation is driven by a handful of multinationals that can amortise a new line over continuous production and national media. Wine has neither the R&D budget nor the iteration speed.

Fourth, the price cue. Consumers read heavy glass as quality, so a producer moving to an alternative format is voluntarily downgrading its own perceived value - a real commercial cost, not a superstition.

Fifth, and least excusably, conservatism: the trade sells to itself, and buyers reject formats they would not personally buy.

## Should it follow?

**Where the answer is clearly yes.** Wine has been losing occasions, not consumers. Beer, spirits and now ready-to-drink formats have taken the outdoor, single-serve, sporting and travel occasions almost by default, because a 75cl glass bottle with a cork cannot be opened at a concert, carried on a hike or sold on an aircraft. American craft brewers moved decisively into cans over the past decade for exactly these reasons and gained shelf space and occasions in doing so. Union Wine Co's Underwood label in Oregon showed the same logic works for wine when the brand is built for the format rather than dropped into it. On-premise, keg wine has grown steadily in the American and British on-trade because it solves a genuine operational problem - by-the-glass waste - rather than a marketing one.

The environmental case is now the strongest of all, and it is the one that will force the issue. Glass is the heaviest and most energy-intensive part of the supply chain, so any producer with a carbon commitment must eventually confront the bottle. Sweden is the proof that consumers accept this at scale: bag-in-box is a leading format by volume at Systembolaget in Sweden, and the sky did not fall.

**Where the answer is no.** Above the premium threshold, the bottle is the product's most valuable communication asset. It is the only piece of unpaid advertising a wine gets in the consumer's home, it enables ageing and resale, and its ritual - the label, the capsule, the cork - is a large part of what the customer is buying. A producer who cans a wine that people give as a gift has destroyed value, not saved carbon. Fine wine should innovate on lightweighting and shipping, not on the format itself.

**The condition that decides it, and where I think the industry has misread the lesson.** Most wine packaging innovation has failed for a reason that has nothing to do with the package: producers copied the format without copying the occasion. Beer cans succeeded because beer already owned a cold, casual, outdoor, single-serve moment; the can served an existing behaviour. Wine has largely put existing wine into new containers and waited for the behaviour to appear. It will not. The industry should follow other categories in *format*, but only where it simultaneously does the harder work of building the occasion - which means new brands, new price architecture and new places of sale, not a line extension of an existing label. That is why I would back a canned brand created for the purpose over any established estate's canned version of itself.

## Conclusion

The industry has been slow because its package is load-bearing in a way that no other drinks category's is - legally, technically and symbolically. But that argument only justifies the top of the market. For the majority of wine, which is drunk within days of purchase and needs no protection, the bottle is an expensive habit that is costing the category occasions it will not get back. So: follow, decisively, below the premium line; hold, deliberately, above it; and recognise that the format is only half the innovation - the occasion is the other half, and it is the half wine keeps failing to build.
