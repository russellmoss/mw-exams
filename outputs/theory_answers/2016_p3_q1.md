## Definition and scope

Spoilage is microbial activity that produces a sensory or compositional defect making the wine less palatable, unstable or unsaleable. It is not the same as microbial presence: *Oenococcus oeni* conducting a clean malolactic fermentation is not spoilage, while the same genus of organism working on residual sugar in a stuck fermentation is.

The question asks specifically about bacteria, so I exclude the fungi and yeasts that are commonly confused with them. *Brettanomyces* is a yeast; botrytis, powdery mildew and downy mildew are fungi; TCA arises from fungal methylation of chlorophenols; reduction is a chemical, yeast-derived phenomenon. The bacterial causes that matter in wine are two, with a third minor case: acetic acid bacteria, lactic acid bacteria, and *Zymomonas* in sweet fortified wines.

## Acetic acid bacteria

*Organisms and mechanism.* *Gluconobacter oxydans* dominates on damaged fruit, *Acetobacter aceti* and *A. pasteurianus* in wine. They are obligate aerobes: they oxidise ethanol to acetaldehyde and then to acetic acid, and esterify acetic acid with ethanol to ethyl acetate.

*Effect.* Volatile acidity gives vinegary sharpness and, more damagingly, ethyl acetate, obtrusive from roughly 150 mg/L, gives nail varnish and glue. At low levels VA can lift a wine; past threshold it is a fault, and past the EU limit of 1.2 g/L for red wine and 1.08 g/L for white and rosé the wine cannot legally be sold. Acetaldehyde additionally binds SO2, so a spoiling wine needs ever more preservative to stay protected.

*Measures.* Oxygen exclusion is the whole defence, because these bacteria cannot work without it: keep tanks full, top barrels, gas headspaces with nitrogen or CO2, avoid a dry exposed cap during long macerations, and check dissolved oxygen at transfers. Maintain molecular SO2 of 0.5 to 0.8 mg/L, which at pH 3.4 means about 25 to 40 mg/L free SO2 and at pH 3.7 roughly double that — a reminder that pH control is a microbiological measure. Add cellar hygiene, clean and sulphured barrels, cool storage, and regular VA analysis so a rise is caught while it is still blendable.

## Lactic acid bacteria

*Organisms.* *Oenococcus oeni*, *Lactobacillus* species such as *L. brevis* and *L. hilgardii*, and *Pediococcus damnosus*. All are tolerant of alcohol and low oxygen, so they are the harder problem: unlike acetic bacteria, they thrive in a sealed tank.

*Effects.* Where sugar remains — a stuck or sluggish fermentation — heterofermentative LAB ferment it to lactic and acetic acid, the classic *piqûre lactique*, and metabolise fructose to mannitol, giving a viscous sweet-sour wine. *Pediococcus* and *Lactobacillus* produce exopolysaccharides that make a wine ropy and oily. Both genera, and *Oenococcus* too, can produce the tetrahydropyridines responsible for mousiness, which is undetectable on the nose and appears retronasally as the mouth's higher pH releases the volatile base. Excess diacetyl overwhelms a wine with butter. Tartaric acid degradation, *tourne*, produces gas, haze and a flat palate. Biogenic amines — histamine, tyramine, putrescine — carry health and export consequences rather than sensory ones. And if sorbic acid has been added, LAB convert it to the compound responsible for geranium taint.

*Measures.* The primary control is denying them a substrate: ferment cleanly to dryness with adequate yeast assimilable nitrogen and sound temperature control, so no sugar is left for them to spoil. If MLF is wanted, inoculate a large, healthy *Oenococcus oeni* population to outcompete *Lactobacillus* and *Pediococcus*, and sulphur promptly on completion — a wine sitting post-MLF without SO2 is the single most vulnerable state in the cellar. Keep pH down; above about pH 3.5 the spoilage species become competitive and SO2 becomes ineffective at the same time. Lysozyme, typically dosed at 250 to 500 mg/L, is active against these Gram-positive bacteria and is the standard tool for blocking MLF or arresting a spoiling one, though it does not touch acetic bacteria. Never use sorbate in a wine that may see LAB. Finally, filtration: a 0.45 micron membrane removes them at bottling, backed by a sterile filler.

## Zymomonas

In sweet and fortified wines *Zymomonas mobilis* ferments residual sugar, producing haze, gas and hydrogen sulphide. It is controlled by sterile filtration and sound SO2, and it is worth knowing because it is the one bacterium that survives conditions designed to stop the others.

## In the vineyard

The vineyard contribution is narrow but decisive: the bacterial load arriving at the winery comes almost entirely from damaged fruit. Botrytis, sour rot, hail, bird and insect damage and split berries carry high populations of acetic acid bacteria and already-elevated VA and gluconic acid, and no cellar practice fully recovers from that. Canopy management for airflow, timely spray programmes, careful harvest, sorting, cool transport and SO2 at reception are therefore microbiological measures, not merely quality ones.

## Conclusion

Bacterial spoilage reduces to a short list of controllable variables: oxygen, sugar, pH, SO2, temperature and hygiene. Acetic bacteria are beaten by excluding air; lactic acid bacteria by leaving them nothing to eat and then removing them.
