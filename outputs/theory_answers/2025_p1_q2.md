## What "greatest threat to vine health" has to mean

A threat can only be ranked against stated criteria, so I use five: how much vineyard area is affected, whether the pathogen kills the vine or only costs the crop, whether it is curable or must be managed indefinitely, whether an effective control exists, and whether the threat is growing or stable. I also draw a distinction the statement blurs — *vine* health is the health of the plant, not the health of the harvest. That distinction is the strongest argument the statement has, and I test it below.

## The case for the statement

The bacterial and phytoplasma diseases share the profile of a maximal threat. Pierce's disease, caused by *Xylella fastidiosa* and spread by sharpshooters, kills the vine, has no cure, and has effectively excluded vinifera from the humid south-eastern United States; its arrival in Southern California in the late 1990s with the glassy-winged sharpshooter showed how fast a new and more mobile vector converts a contained problem into a regional emergency. Xylella was confirmed in Puglia in 2013 and the strains circulating in the Mediterranean have put southern European viticulture inside a quarantine framework it did not previously need. Flavescence dorée is the phytoplasma equivalent: quarantine-regulated in the EU, spread vine to vine by *Scaphoideus titanus*, incurable, and controlled only by compulsory insecticide treatment plus uprooting of infected vines — a control that is a legal obligation rather than a choice and that sits awkwardly with organic certification. Bois noir, with a polyphagous planthopper vector and weed hosts, is less epidemic but chronically present. Crown gall, systemic in the planting material and expressed after winter freeze injury, quietly limits viticulture across the cold continental regions.

The common features are what make the statement plausible: these organisms are systemic and incurable, they kill or force grubbing-up, control is indirect because it targets an insect, and warming is extending vector ranges. Each is also regulated, meaning the cost includes movement restrictions and destruction orders as well as lost vines.

## The rival threats

Against that, three other groups are at least as damaging and, on the criteria above, mostly more so.

Trunk disease is the strongest rival. Esca, Eutypa and the Botryosphaeria complex kill wood and eventually the vine, affect a very large share of the world's mature vineyards, cannot be cured, and there has been no curative chemical treatment since sodium arsenite was withdrawn. Where Pierce's disease is regionally catastrophic, trunk disease is globally chronic — it shortens productive vineyard life almost everywhere, and its cost is paid annually in missing vines and lost yield rather than in a headline outbreak.

Virus complexes are the second rival. Leafroll, spread by mealybugs and scale, delays ripening, suppresses colour and sugar and shortens vineyard life; fanleaf, carried by the dagger nematode *Xiphinema index*, degrades yield and forces long replant fallows. Neither kills quickly, but both are incurable, both are moved by the nursery trade, and both affect enormous plantings.

The mildews are the third, and they are the test of the vine-versus-crop distinction. Downy and powdery mildew rarely kill a vine, so on a strict reading of "vine health" the statement survives. But downy mildew defoliates, depletes reserves and, over successive seasons, does weaken and kill vines; and it is the single largest recurring cost in world viticulture, driving most of the sprays applied to most of the vineyards on earth. A definition of "vine health" that ranks a disease four candidates in a hundred have ever seen above the one that dictates the annual spray programme is a definition doing too much work.

Abiotic threats complete the comparison. Drought, extreme heat, spring frost, hail, flood and fire destroy vines outright and at scale, and no phytosanitary measure addresses them.

## My position

I do not accept the statement as put. The bacterial and phytoplasma diseases are the most *feared* threats and the fastest-growing ones, and on a twenty-year view they may well become the greatest — because they are vector-borne, incurable, and expanding with warming, and because a Xylella strain adapted to a major European wine region would be a category of loss the industry has not faced since phylloxera. That is a trajectory argument, and I would agree with the statement if it were made about 2045.

Today, the greatest threats to vine health are trunk disease and the virus complexes: universal, incurable, uninsurable, and steadily removing productive vines from every region, while attracting a fraction of the regulatory attention Xylella receives precisely because they are undramatic. The reason the ranking matters is practical. Threats you can exclude are managed by quarantine, clean planting material and vector control; threats already inside every vineyard are managed by pruning technique, nursery certification and replanting policy. Concentrating on the exotic risk at the expense of the endemic one is the error the statement invites, and it is the one growers most often make.
