## Mid-priced, and the four levers the question hands you

By mid-priced I mean a wine retailing at roughly £10-20 in the UK: it must carry real processing costs and a recognisable varietal identity, but cannot support hand-sorting, barrel fermentation or long ageing, and it will be drunk within a year or two. That rules some remedies out on cost alone. The question's parameters then set the structure: alcohol, pH and titratable acidity, and so the four tools of chaptalisation, water addition, acidification and deacidification.

## a) English Chardonnay — the problems in this juice

At 8% potential alcohol the juice carries roughly 135 g/L of sugar, short of the 12% a consumer expects and below the minimum strength at which the product can be sold as wine. TA of 14.5 g/L is around double a palatable level. pH 2.8 is the more interesting number: it is a microbiological gift, since at pH 2.8 some 9% of free SO2 is in the molecular form, so tiny sulphur additions protect the wine — but it also puts the juice below the threshold at which malolactic bacteria will function. Fruit this underripe will be malic-dominant and green, and in a cool wet English season on the Kent, Sussex and Hampshire chalk, botrytis and sour rot are likely, bringing laccase with them.

## a) Handling: alcohol, then acidity and pH

Enrichment is compulsory. Zone A, where England sits, permits enrichment of up to 3% vol, taking this juice to 11% — the ceiling, not a choice. Sucrose is the right vehicle at about 17 g/L of sucrose per 1% of alcohol, so roughly 50 g/L, added in stages during fermentation; RCGM works but costs more. Must concentration is exactly wrong here, because removing water concentrates the acid too.

Deacidification then does most of the work, and one method will not be enough. Calcium carbonate is the powerful option: 1 g/L of calcium carbonate removes about 1.5 g/L of tartaric acid as insoluble calcium tartrate and raises pH sharply — but it removes only tartaric, this juice's excess is largely malic, and it leaves residual calcium that can throw crystals slowly and unpredictably in bottle. Potassium bicarbonate is gentler in practice only because it is limited to small corrections, removing roughly 1.1-1.5 g/L of acidity per gram added - similar potency per gram to calcium carbonate - but it forms bitartrate that must then be cold-stabilised out and is impractical beyond about 2 g/L. The right tool for malic-dominant acid is double-salt deacidification: treat a fraction of the must with excess calcium carbonate seeded with calcium tartrate-malate so both acids precipitate together, then blend back to target.

Malolactic fermentation is then essential, and for a specific reason: it converts diprotic malic acid to monoprotic lactic acid, so converting 1 g/L of malic acid drops titratable acidity by roughly 0.5 g/L, softens the sharp malic edge to a rounder lactic one, and adds diacetyl weight that flatters lean fruit. But *Oenococcus oeni* struggles below about pH 3.1, so the sequence matters: chemically deacidify first to lift pH into the low threes, keep SO2 minimal, warm the wine to 18-20C, inoculate a proven acid-tolerant strain with bacterial nutrient, and only sulphur once malic tests clear.

## a) Palatability and stability

The wine will still taste taut, so I would leave 6-10 g/L of residual sugar — the cheapest effective palate correction at this price — plus lees ageing and oak staves rather than barrels. The stability risks are calcium tartrate thrown slowly by the deacidification, which argues for treating must rather than wine; refermentation of that residual sugar, requiring sterile filtration and molecular SO2, which the low pH makes easy; and protein haze, handled with bentonite.

## b) Barossa Grenache Noir — the problems in this juice

The mirror image. At 16.5% potential alcohol this is hot, unbalanced, expensive in duty and at real risk of sticking; TA of 3.9 g/L is flabby. pH 4.2 is the serious problem: at pH 4.2 the figure is around 0.4%, so SO2 is almost useless and the wine is open to lactic bacteria, acetic bacteria and Brettanomyces, while colour is poor because anthocyanins are largely in colourless forms at that pH — and Grenache is anthocyanin-poor and oxidation-prone anyway. Raisined fruit also implies low YAN.

## b) Handling: alcohol, acidity and stability

Water addition is the cheap first move — Australian rules permit watering back high-sugar must to a floor of about 13.5 Baumé — but it dilutes acid and colour further, so it must be paired with acidification. Reverse osmosis or spinning cone give precise alcohol reduction on toll at this volume, though they may cost more than the margin allows.

Acidify pre-fermentation with tartaric: 1 g/L of tartaric acid moves pH by roughly 0.1, so reaching pH 3.5 needs a large addition, best split either side of fermentation and followed by cold stabilisation, since much will precipitate as bitartrate and let pH drift back. Cation exchange on a portion of the wine complements it, lowering pH without adding acid mass.

Ferment with an alcohol-tolerant strain, staged nutrients and temperature capped near 28C — a stuck ferment at pH 4.2 with sugar present would be a spoilage disaster. Press early rather than macerating long, because extended skin contact leaches potassium and pushes pH higher still. Add oenological tannin at crush to bind what pigment there is, blend with Shiraz or Mataro in the Barossa manner for colour and structure, and use oak chips with micro-oxygenation. Stability then demands sterile filtration, screwcap and prompt sale.

## Conclusion

The English wine's problems are chemical and correctable within the law; the Barossa wine's are microbiological and only partly correctable. At mid price I would back the Grenache to reach the shelf in balance, and treat the English fruit as what its numbers say it is — a sparkling base being asked to do the wrong job.
