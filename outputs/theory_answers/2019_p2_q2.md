## Defining the target, and the criteria for evaluation

"Lower alcohol" covers three quite different briefs and the options that serve one are wrong for another. **Moderately reduced** means a wine one to two percent below its regional norm — a 13% Napa Cabernet instead of 15% — where the aim is balance. **Low-alcohol** means a category product at roughly 5-8%, competing on lightness and calories. **De-alcoholised** means at or near zero. I evaluate each option against four criteria: how much alcohol it actually removes; what it costs the wine in aroma, texture and balance; capital and operating cost; and legality, since this is the area where the rules change fastest.

My position: reductions of about one percent are achievable in the vineyard and cellar with little quality cost; anything beyond that requires physical removal, which works but taxes the wine, and the deeper the cut the more the wine must be rebuilt.

## Before the fruit arrives

**Site selection** is the most fundamental and least available option. Cooler sites, higher altitude, higher latitude, maritime influence — Tasmania, Mosel, Marlborough, the Uco Valley's upper terraces — simply accumulate less sugar at flavour ripeness. It is decisive and permanent, but it is a capital decision, not something available to a winemaker with fruit contracted this vintage.

**Variety and clone** rank next: Riesling, Muscadet's Melon, Verdejo, Grüner Veltliner and Gamay reach aromatic and phenolic ripeness at modest sugar, whereas Grenache, Zinfandel and Shiraz do not. The constraint is commercial — the market may want the variety that gives the problem.

**Viticulture** contributes: larger canopies that avoid shutdown, higher crop levels, late leaf removal, and delayed or double pruning to push ripening into cooler autumn conditions. All are worth perhaps half to one percent and none is free of quality consequences.

**Picking date** is the most direct vineyard lever and the most abused. Picking earlier lowers sugar reliably, but sugar accumulation and flavour, tannin and seed ripeness are only loosely coupled, and in warm regions they have decoupled further. Pick early and the wine gains freshness but loses phenolic ripeness, gaining green tannin and pyrazines. Realistically it buys about one percent before the wine tastes underripe. Picking in two passes and blending the fractions is a better version of the same idea.

## In the must

**Water addition** is the cheapest reduction there is, and its availability is purely legal. It is prohibited in the EU as a general practice, permitted in some New World jurisdictions, and the rules move: **Australia now permits water addition to juice or must to reduce potential alcohol to no lower than 13.5%** — a change a candidate must know, because it materially alters what an Australian winemaker may do. Its cost is dilution of everything else: acid, phenolics, colour and flavour all fall with the sugar, so it is a tool for correcting an excessive must, not for making a light wine.

**Must blending** — combining an early-picked, low-sugar parcel with a ripe one — achieves the same arithmetic without diluting extract, and is preferable wherever the fruit exists. Enzymatic treatments such as glucose oxidase work in principle but bring acidity and oxidation problems and are little used.

## During fermentation

**Yeast choice** gives a genuine but small return. Strains differ in ethanol yield per gram of sugar, and low-conversion commercial strains are marketed for perhaps 0.5% reduction; sequential inoculation with non-*Saccharomyces* species such as *Metschnikowia* or *Torulaspora* diverts some sugar to glycerol and other metabolites, buying a similar amount. Both are cheap and quality-neutral to positive. Neither is more than an adjunct, and non-*Saccharomyces* work needs careful management to avoid VA and stuck ferments.

**Stopping fermentation** by chilling, filtering and sulphuring, or by fortification, leaves sugar unfermented. This is how Moscato d'Asti, German Kabinett and Prädikat styles and many Rieslings arrive at 5.5-9% with genuine quality — but it delivers a *sweet* wine, and the residual sugar is not optional. For a dry low-alcohol wine it is no help, and the resulting wine is microbiologically fragile and needs sterile filtration and careful SO2.

**Vessel and ferment conditions** matter only marginally: warmer, open ferments lose a little more ethanol to evaporation.

## After fermentation

**Blending** finished wines of different strengths is the simplest post-ferment tool and quality-neutral, but it cannot take a wine far.

Physical removal is the only route to a large reduction. **Reverse osmosis** passes wine across a membrane, separating a permeate of water and ethanol which is distilled and the water returned; it is gentle, works at ambient temperature, allows precise stepwise reduction, and is widely used for one to three percent cuts. **Spinning cone** strips volatiles in a first pass at low temperature, then removes ethanol in a second, then returns the aroma fraction — the most aroma-protective option, and the standard for deep reduction, but capital-intensive and usually contracted out. **Vacuum distillation** removes ethanol at reduced pressure and temperature; it is efficient and cheapest per litre at scale, but the most damaging to aroma and the most likely to leave a cooked, hollow wine.

All three share the same fundamental problem: ethanol carries body, sweetness, aroma volatility and length. Remove it and the wine loses mid-palate and gains perceived acidity and astringency, so de-alcoholised wines are typically rebalanced with grape must, sugar, glycerol, gum arabic or CO2 — which is legal in some markets and not others. **Legality also governs the categories themselves: the EU threshold for de-alcoholised wine at 0.5% and partially de-alcoholised below the category minimum**, with labelling rules that differ again in the US and Australia.

## Conclusion

For a one percent reduction I would use site, variety, picking date and blending first, with a low-yield yeast, because these cost the wine nothing. Beyond that, reverse osmosis is the best compromise of cost and quality, and spinning cone the best quality outcome where budget allows. For genuinely low-alcohol wine, no technique yet compensates fully for what ethanol contributed; the honest evaluation is that the category is improving but still trades quality for the claim.
