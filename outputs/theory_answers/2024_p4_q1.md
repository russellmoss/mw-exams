## What I am measuring

Commercial success is not volume. I assess each style on four tests: volume and its trajectory; value realisation, meaning average price per bottle and whether it has held in real terms; producer margin and cash characteristics; and durability — whether the style owns a defensible consumer occasion or is renting a fashion. A style can succeed at £5 and at £50, and can grow in volume while failing on value.

That assessment must be made against a category that has turned. After roughly fifteen years of growth, rosé is now in decline across many global markets: OIV and IWSR data show falling consumption in the United States and in France, the two markets that drove the boom, and Provence has moved from managing allocation to managing surplus. Each style below is judged against a headwind, not a tailwind.

## Provence-model pale dry still rosé

The strongest value story in wine of the last twenty years. Provence converted a regional summer drink into a year-round premium category by holding three things constant: a very pale colour, a dry and low-phenolic palate, and a distinctive heavy skittle bottle in flint glass. The colour and the clear glass are the proposition — the consumer buys the shade through the bottle before reading the label — which is why colour stability is a commercial priority here in a way it is not in any other category, and why bottle shape and glass colour function as the premiumisation lever rather than as packaging.

On value it succeeded outright: Côtes de Provence and Coteaux d'Aix built a price ladder from roughly £12 to well over £30, and Whispering Angel and Miraval demonstrated that a rosé could carry a genuine brand premium and celebrity association without discounting. On cash it is excellent: the wine is made and sold within twelve months of harvest, so capital turns far faster than for a red held two years in cellar — a material advantage for a family estate.

Its weakness is that it now defines the category and therefore owns the decline. Export volumes to the US have fallen back from their peak, the style has been copied competently from the Languedoc to Chile at half the price, and the pale colour Provence created is no longer proprietary. Verdict: a genuinely successful style, but one whose premium is now defended by brand and provenance rather than by the style itself.

## White Zinfandel

Commercially the largest rosé success ever achieved, and the clearest argument for judging success over decades rather than seasons. Created at Sutter Home in the mid-1970s from a stuck fermentation, it became one of the biggest-selling wine styles in the United States and peaked in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Its real achievement was recruitment: it brought millions of new, sweetness-preferring drinkers into wine at a low price, on high yields, with no oak, no ageing and a very fast production cycle — an outstanding margin and cash-flow structure.

It has declined structurally ever since, and the reasons are the instructive part. The style was attached to a price point and a generation rather than to an occasion; it built no premium ladder, since nobody ever launched a £25 White Zinfandel; and the deep pink that identified it became a negative signal once Provence set the aesthetic. It also anchored Zinfandel itself at the bottom of the market. Verdict: an enormous historical success on volume, a failure on value and durability, now a managed-decline business.

## Prosecco Rosé DOC

The most recent case and the most revealing, because it was legislated rather than grown. The category was created in 2020, permitting up to 15 percent Pinot Noir blended with Glera and requiring a vintage date, and went from nothing to significant volume within two or three seasons. That speed is the whole point: it succeeded because it attached a new style to an existing distribution machine. Prosecco already held the listings, the brand recognition and the price expectation, so retailers took the rosé without needing to be sold the category.

The commercial risk mirrors that. It trades at a promotional off-trade price point, its margin is thin, it cannibalises white Prosecco rather than creating a new occasion, and it is exposed to a sparkling market that has stopped growing. Verdict: a fast and real success on volume, weak on value, and unproven on durability.

## What separates them

Three themes. Distribution beats product: Prosecco Rosé grew in three years because the route to market pre-existed, where Provence needed two decades to build one. A style survives only if it has a premium ladder — Provence built one, White Zinfandel never did, and that single difference explains their opposite fates. And branding carries more of the load in rosé than in any other category, as Laurent-Perrier Cuvée Rosé has done in sparkling for more than fifty years and as Croft Pink attempted in fortified.

## Conclusion

On my four tests Provence is the most commercially successful, because it alone converted volume into sustained value and built a ladder to defend it. Prosecco Rosé is the most successful on growth and the weakest on margin. White Zinfandel was the largest and is now the cautionary tale. In a declining category the style that survives will be the one with a defensible price position, not the one with the largest volume.
