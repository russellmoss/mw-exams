## Defining the terms: funk, desirable, manage

Funkiness is best defined as the grey area between fault and complexity: aromatic and textural characters produced by compounds that are unambiguously faults at high concentration, but which at low concentration, and in a style that can carry them, read as savouriness, depth and individuality. Desirable is a judgement made against a style brief and a market, not an absolute — the struck-match note that makes a serious white distinctive would be a fault in a supermarket Sauvignon Blanc sold on primary fruit. Manage is the operative word, and the one that sets the shape of this answer: the task is not to remove these characters but to arrive at a chosen level of them, deliberately and repeatably. Controlled funkiness and random spoilage can produce the same molecule; they are wholly different acts of winemaking, because only one of them is specified in advance and stopped at a defined point. I take the question to run across still, sparkling and fortified wine at all price levels, not only low-intervention wine.

## The characters in play, and their mechanisms

*Ethylphenols.* Brettanomyces bruxellensis decarboxylates and reduces hydroxycinnamic acids to 4-ethylphenol and 4-ethylguaiacol — spice and leather at low levels, animal and medicinal above. It works on trace residual sugars after fermentation, favoured by high pH, warm cellars, low free SO2 and oxygen ingress at racking or topping. 4-ethylphenol is detectable in red wine at of the order of 400 micrograms per litre, which is the number that defines the target.

*Reduction.* Hydrogen sulphide, mercaptans and thiols give struck flint, smoke and, past threshold, drains. They arise from nitrogen-stressed yeast and from lees held without oxygen.

*Oxidation.* Acetaldehyde gives bruised apple and nuttiness; flor yeast additionally generates sotolon-driven curry and walnut notes. Acetobacter, needing oxygen, converts ethanol to acetic acid and thence to ethyl acetate.

*Volatile acidity.* Acetic acid and ethyl acetate, from acetic acid bacteria working aerobically, read as lift and perfume just below threshold and as vinegar and solvent above it — the grey area with the narrowest margin of all.

*Skin, solids and lees.* Extended maceration on white grapes brings phenolics, grip and a savoury oxidative edge; high fermentation solids and long lees ageing bring cheese and yeast-derived reductive notes.

## Managing to a target, not eliminating

Four disciplines convert this from luck into method.

First, set the target before harvest, as a sensory specification with an analytical proxy — a 4-ethylphenol ceiling, a free and molecular SO2 regime, a dissolved oxygen band.

Second, generate the character on purpose. Reduction is created by high fermentation solids, low assimilable nitrogen, a sulphide-producing yeast strain and reductive handling. Oxidative character is created by deliberate ullage, cask ageing without topping, or a flor veil. Phenolic funk is created by skin contact time and temperature. These are process settings, not accidents.

Third — and this is the single most useful lever — build the character in a fraction and blend. Fermenting one parcel reductively, or ageing one cask oxidatively, and blending it back at a chosen percentage gives a continuous dial and a repeatable result, where treating a whole tank gives one irreversible shot.

Fourth, arrest at the target. Sterile or 0.45 micron membrane filtration, molecular SO2 of roughly 0.6 mg/L, and dryness to trace sugar stop Brettanomyces where it stands. Reduction is adjusted downward by racking, aeration or copper — residual copper is legally capped at around 1 mg/L — and, importantly, copper additions strip thiols permanently, so this is a decision to take late and small. Oxidation is arrested by topping, inert gas and SO2, and volatile acidity has a hard stop in the EU ceiling of roughly 1.2 g/L for red wine.

## Across the styles

The grey area is not the property of one category. In sparkling wine, reductive base-wine handling and long lees autolysis are used deliberately in Champagne and increasingly in English and Tasmanian sparkling; low dosage exposes those notes rather than hiding them. In fortified wine funk is the category: flor-aged Sherry is built on acetaldehyde, Madeira's canteiro ageing on controlled heat and oxidation, Rutherglen Muscat on decades of oxidative solera. Among white table wines, Jura Savagnin aged sous voile, and skin-contact whites in Georgian qvevri, Friuli and Slovenian Brda, all trade on characters the same examiner would fail in a Chablis. Among reds, traditional Northern Rhône Syrah and old-school Rioja carry ethylphenols as part of their identity, while a modern Napa Cabernet built on fruit purity cannot. The counter-example matters: Amarone and other appassimento reds carry volatile acidity that would condemn a Beaujolais, because concentration, sweetness and dried-fruit character absorb it. Style context, not the molecule, decides.

## Conclusion

Funk is manageable in proportion to how reversible it is. Reduction and oxidation are chemical and can be dialled both ways; skin contact is a decision taken once and blended afterwards. Brettanomyces is alive, and a population that is merely tolerated will keep growing in bottle. So the best management is to generate the character deliberately in a defined fraction of the blend, measure it against a numeric target, and then kill or arrest the mechanism — never to leave it running and call the result complexity.
