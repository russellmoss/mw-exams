## Hue, intensity and stability

Rosé colour has three separable dimensions and different techniques govern each: intensity (pale to deep), hue (blue-pink through salmon to onion-skin orange), and stability — whether that colour survives to the consumer. Rosé is chromatically fragile. It carries a small anthocyanin load with very little tannin to co-polymerise with, so pigment that is not actively protected is simply lost. I therefore read the question as covering how colour is created, adjusted, reduced and then defended, rather than how rosé styles are made, and I work chronologically from fruit to shelf.

## Fruit selection

The first colour decision is taken before the fruit reaches the winery, and it is the one candidates most often skip. Variety sets both the anthocyanin ceiling and the hue: Grenache and Cinsault give pale, salmon-to-orange pinks and oxidise readily; Syrah, Mourvèdre and Tempranillo give more intense, bluer colour; Pinot Noir gives pale colour that is also unstable; Cabernet Sauvignon or a teinturier gives far more pigment than a pale style can absorb. Ripeness pulls in two directions, since anthocyanin accumulates as the fruit ripens but pH rises with it, so later fruit brings more pigment into a less red-favouring chemistry. Fruit health is decisive: botrytis-affected fruit carries laccase, which destroys colour irreversibly and is not controlled by sulphur dioxide alone. Destemming is an underrated lever — stems adsorb anthocyanin and contribute green phenolics, so whole-bunch pressing yields a paler, cleaner-hued juice.

## Getting the colour in: three routes and a fourth

**Direct pressing** takes whole or destemmed berries straight to the press, so colour arises only from the contact and pressure of pressing itself. It gives the palest wines and the finest control, because colour builds progressively through the press cycle and fractions can be cut and blended to a target.

**Skin-contact maceration** holds crushed fruit on skins for a defined period — hours, not days — before pressing, and is the route to a deliberately deeper, fruitier rosé. Duration and temperature are the two controls, and they interact: extraction is faster and less selective when warm, so a cold maceration extracts anthocyanin preferentially over tannin and delivers colour without hardness.

**Saignée** bleeds juice from a red fermentation. The rationale is different in kind: here the rosé is a by-product of concentrating a red, so the winemaker does not really choose the colour — it is whatever the red wine's extraction schedule produced. Saignée juice is typically darker, warmer and more phenolic, and tends to a coarser texture. The choice between these three is fundamentally a choice about who controls the colour, which is why direct pressing dominates wherever pale rosé is the primary product rather than the residue of another.

Blending red wine into white is the fourth route, and this is prohibited under EU law only for still rosé without a protected designation of origin or geographical indication - PDO/PGI rosés and any sparkling wine may use it. Beyond category integrity there is an oenological reason: the red component brings leached skin and seed tannin into a wine with no structure to carry it, giving a harsher texture than a skin-contact rosé of the same depth.

## Adjusting hue

Hue is largely a pH and redox question. Low pH holds anthocyanin in its red flavylium form and keeps the wine pink; higher pH pushes it grey-blue and then dull, so acidification is a hue tool as much as a taste one. Oxidation moves rosé from pink toward salmon and orange as anthocyanin is lost and phenolics brown — a fault in a Provençal style, a signature in a traditional Tavel or a Rioja clarete. Oak ageing contributes a golden cast and ellagitannin that helps stabilise what pigment remains.

## Reducing colour when there is too much

Fining with bentonite, casein or PVPP is the standard remedy for a rosé that has come in too dark, but it is blunt and strips aroma along with pigment. Better options exist. Cut the press fractions and blend to target rather than macerating and correcting afterwards; blend in a paler lot or a paler variety; shorten skin contact and press cooler in the first place; or use a decolourising carbon at a low, bench-trialled dose, which removes pigment more selectively than a protein fining.

## Holding the colour to the bottle

Colour loss between tank and bottle is routinely underestimated. Sulphur dioxide is double-edged: it bleaches anthocyanin reversibly, so the wine looks paler than it is at high free SO2, while also suppressing the oxidation that would destroy pigment permanently. Oxygen exclusion matters more — cool storage, inert gas cover, minimal transfers and low dissolved oxygen at the filler. Copper and iron catalyse oxidation and should be measured and, if elevated, removed. Every clarification and filtration step costs a little pigment, so filtration should be as coarse as microbiological security allows. Finally, packaging: clear glass sells the colour but exposes it, and light degrades anthocyanin, so a pale rosé standing in flint glass under retail lighting fades visibly.

## Conclusion

Colour in rosé is set by variety and fruit condition, delivered by the choice between direct pressing, maceration and saignée, tuned by temperature, time, pH and press-fraction blending, and then defended against oxygen, light and over-processing. The methods that create it are easy; the methods that keep it are where rosé quality is actually decided.
