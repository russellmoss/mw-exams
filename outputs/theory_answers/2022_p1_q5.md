## The decision, and how I would take it

A cover crop is any sown or resident vegetation grown between or under the vines, permanent or temporary. There is no default: the same species is an asset in one block and a liability in the next row. I would work through four steps in order — what am I trying to achieve, what does the site allow, which species deliver it, and when do I sow and terminate — and I would assume throughout that every cover crop carries its reward and its risk simultaneously, so the question is never whether the benefit exists but whether it outweighs what it costs the vine.

## Purpose first

I need to know the objective before the species. The realistic purposes are: devigoration on a fertile site; nitrogen supply on a depleted one; building organic matter and soil carbon; erosion control; trafficability for spraying and harvest in a wet climate; relieving compaction; habitat for predatory insects; weed suppression; and certification or marketing requirements. These pull in different directions — a legume mix that fixes nitrogen is the wrong choice on a vineyard whose problem is excess vigour — so naming the objective decides most of what follows.

## The binding constraint: soil water

Water availability is the factor that overrides all the others. In a dry-farmed Mediterranean vineyard — inland Spain, the southern Rhone, dry-grown Barossa — a permanent sward transpires the same limited winter reserve the vine needs in February and March, and the result is early stomatal closure, stalled sugar accumulation and unripe tannin. There the answer is a winter cover sown after the autumn rains and terminated at bud burst, or resident vegetation in alternate rows only, so the biological benefit is captured in the wet season and the competition removed before the vine needs the water. In high-rainfall maritime regions — Marlborough, Willamette, the Loire, much of Germany — that same competition is the point: a permanent grass sward is the cheapest devigoration tool available and simultaneously carries the tractor through a wet spring. Where irrigation exists, a cover crop is affordable but adds to the water budget, and I would price it as such.

## Vigour, nutrition and species choice

On a fertile, deep alluvial soil producing dense canopies I would sow competitive grasses — perennial ryegrass, fescue, cereal rye — to draw down nitrogen and water and reduce shoot growth. On a low-vigour stony slope the reverse: legumes such as vetch, faba bean, clover or field peas to fix nitrogen, incorporated as a green manure. Brassicas such as mustard and tillage radish are chosen for a different purpose again — a deep taproot to open compaction and a biofumigant effect against nematodes. The risk runs both ways: legumes on a fertile site produce exactly the vigour and canopy density I was trying to avoid, and a competitive grass on a dry site produces water stress I cannot reverse.

## Soil physical condition: erosion, compaction, drainage and access

On slopes — the Douro terraces, the Mosel, Swiss and Austrian hillsides — erosion control makes a cover crop close to obligatory, and the species must establish fast in autumn before the winter rain. Compaction from repeated tractor passes is addressed with deep-rooting species in the wheel rows specifically, rather than uniformly. In regions with wet harvests, drainage and trafficability alone justify a permanent sward. Row width and tractor clearance determine whether an under-vine cover is mechanically manageable at all, and on close-spaced rows it usually is not.

## Frost, soil temperature and humidity

This is the clearest case of a single practice inverting in sign. A tall sward insulates the soil, prevents it absorbing daytime heat and re-radiating it at night, and therefore measurably raises radiative frost risk — a serious issue in Chablis, Champagne and the Marlborough valley floor, which is why growers mow short or cultivate before the spring frost window. In hot regions the same insulation and the same reduction in reflected heat are benefits, lowering root-zone temperature and berry heat load. Likewise humidity: a dense sward raises humidity in the fruiting zone and increases botrytis and downy mildew pressure in a wet climate, while flowering species host predatory mites and parasitoids and suppress the dust that provokes mite outbreaks. Cover crops also shelter rodents, snails and leafhoppers, so IPM cuts both ways.

## Timing, cultivation and cost

Timing is a lever in its own right: autumn sowing after the first rains, termination by mowing, rolling or shallow incorporation at bud burst where water is short, or maintenance through the season where it is not. Alternate-row seeding lets me hedge and reverse the decision annually. Finally, cost: seed, a seeder, and three or four mowing passes are real diesel and labour. On a low-margin bulk block, mown resident vegetation may be the only version that pays; on a premium block, a tailored sown mix earns its cost through vigour control alone.

## Conclusion

I would let purpose and water availability decide, choose species to match the vigour problem rather than the fashion, and manage timing as the tool that separates the reward from the risk.
