## How I am reading this

This is a marketing question, so I assess environmental credentials as a marketing asset: what they do to distribution, to price and to purchase. I am not assessing whether the practices are good agronomy. My position, argued below, is that environmental credentials are now very important in *business-to-business* marketing - they are increasingly a condition of listing - and still only moderately important in *consumer* marketing, where they rarely make the sale and rarely earn a premium on their own. Importance therefore depends almost entirely on who you are selling to and where.

## The footprint, ranked by marketing value

**Vineyard practices.** Organic and biodynamic certification is the only part of the footprint with a legally protected, consumer-recognised mark, which is why it carries most of the commercial weight. Sustainability schemes -- France's Haute Valeur Environnementale certification, the Certified California Sustainable Winegrowing programme, and New Zealand's Sustainable Winegrowing programme, which covers a very large majority of the country's producing vineyard area -- are stronger arguments to a buyer than to a shopper. Marketing value: high with buyers, modest with consumers, and diluted by logo clutter.

**Energy and water use in the winery.** Almost invisible to the consumer, who has no mental model of winery energy. It matters to corporate customers with reporting obligations and it saves the producer money, but as a consumer story it is generic. Low marketing value, real operational value.

**The container.** The most important element commercially, and the one most often under-played. Glass is typically the single largest element of a bottled wine's carbon footprint, so lightweighting, bag-in-box, PET, pouch, can and keg deliver the biggest real reduction - and, crucially, the consumer can see and hold the decision. That is what makes it marketable. The obstacle is that the same consumer reads heavy glass as quality, so the environmental argument is fighting a price cue.

**The closure.** A genuine marketing asset for cork, which has successfully positioned itself as renewable and carbon-storing against screwcap and plastic. Consumers do respond to closures, but for perceived quality reasons far more than environmental ones, so the credential rides on a preference that already exists.

**Transport to market.** The strongest argument for shipping in bulk and bottling at destination, which most large-volume brands now do. It is almost impossible to market to consumers, because "shipped in a flexitank" reads as industrial. Here the credential and the consumer story point in opposite directions.

## Geography decides the answer

This is where I would put the empirical evidence. In Scandinavia the credential is close to a condition of trade: the Nordic monopolies -- Systembolaget, Alko and Vinmonopolet -- write organic requirements into a growing share of their tenders. A producer without certification is not competing for that business at all.

Germany, Switzerland, Austria and the Netherlands follow, with organic grocery ranges long established. The UK is different: the large multiples set supplier standards behind the scenes but sell almost no wine on an environmental claim on the front label. The US market rewards "organically grown grapes" and clean-label cues rather than certification, and has a specific complication in that the legal definition of organic wine constrains sulphur additions. In China, Japan and most of Asia the credential is currently a minor purchase factor next to origin, brand and gifting appropriateness.

## What actually moves behaviour

Two things, from the marketer's point of view. First, environmental claims work as a tie-breaker rather than a driver: they convert a consumer who has already chosen the category and price, which is why they rarely support a premium in isolation. Second, they work far better when bundled with a benefit the consumer wants anyway - health and "clean" perception in organic, convenience and portion control in bag-in-box and can, recyclability in a lighter bottle that also costs less to ship.

I would also set wine against the categories it competes with for attention. Consumers have been trained by food to expect organic, free-range and fair trade as everyday, priced options, and by coffee to expect certification. Wine is behind that curve, which is both a criticism and an opportunity: the expectation already exists and wine does not have to create it.

## My position

Environmental credentials are important, but not where most producers spend money on them. They are close to essential for market access with institutional buyers, monopolies and large multiples, and they will only become more so. They are of moderate and rising importance to consumers, but chiefly through the package rather than the vineyard.

## Conclusion

How important are they? Important enough that a producer without them will progressively lose access to the best-organised markets, and not yet important enough to sell a bottle on their own. I would advise a client to hold certification as a licence to trade and to market the container - because that is the one part of the footprint the consumer can actually see, and the one that changes what goes in the basket.
