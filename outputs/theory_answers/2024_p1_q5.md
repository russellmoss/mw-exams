## Definition, and the variable underneath it

I take low density to mean below roughly 2,500 vines per hectare, medium 2,500 to 5,000, and high above roughly 5,000, with the classic European fine-wine model at around 8,000 to 10,000 vines per hectare — Burgundy, Champagne and the Médoc classed growths, at a spacing of about a metre between vines and between rows. At the other end, dry-farmed bush vines in the Barossa or the Swartland and irrigated blocks in inland Australia and California sit at 1,000 to 2,000 vines per hectare.

Two distinctions matter before assessing anything. First, density is a proxy, not a mechanism: what affects the fruit is the resource available to each vine and the crop each vine carries, so the same yield per hectare can be produced by many small vines or few large ones. Second, row spacing and in-row spacing are separable levers. Narrowing rows raises light interception per hectare; narrowing vines within the row divides the same soil volume among more roots. Growers who need mechanisation can keep wide rows and still raise density in the row.

## The merits of high density

Where soil is poor and water is adequate, dividing a limited resource among more vines gives each a small canopy and a small crop, and that vine reaches balance and ripens fully and early. This is the real argument: high density lowers yield per vine while holding or raising yield per hectare, which is the combination fine wine wants. Narrow rows also intercept more light per hectare in the early season and shade the ground, restraining weeds and moderating soil temperature.

There is a further merit that is often overstated: that competition drives roots deeper and improves drought resistance and mineral expression. The evidence for this is contested, and I would not rest an argument on it. The reliable benefits are per-vine crop load and light interception.

The costs are real. Establishment is several times more expensive in plants, grafting, trellis and labour; narrow rows require specialised or straddle machinery, or none; and per-vine hand operations multiply.

## The merits of low density

Where water is the limiting resource, fewer vines share more of it, and this is the decisive argument for low density across dry-farmed Mediterranean and warm-climate viticulture: old Barossa and Swartland bush vines, traditional gobelet in Rioja and much of Spain, and non-irrigated Douro plantings. A high-density unirrigated block in those climates exhausts the soil profile in a dry summer, stalls ripening and produces stressed, unbalanced fruit — the opposite of the intention. Low density also permits full mechanisation on conventional equipment, cuts establishment cost per hectare dramatically, and allows larger vines with more permanent wood, which buffers against frost and heat and supports vine longevity.

Its weakness is the mirror image: on a low-vigour site with adequate water, low density under-exploits the land, loads each vine with a larger crop, and delays ripening.

## The assessment: density is a dependent variable

The apparent contradiction between these two positions dissolves once density is assessed against site vigour and water rather than in the abstract. High density on a deep, fertile, irrigated soil produces crowded, shaded canopies, poor fruit exposure, botrytis pressure and dilute, herbaceous fruit — the classic error of transplanting a European density onto an alluvial New World site. The correct response there is not more vines but a wider row with a divided or vertically extended canopy — Scott Henry, Smart-Dyson, lyre — as adopted on fertile New Zealand and eastern American sites, which converts vigour into leaf area rather than into shade. Equally, a thin hillside soil is wasted at low density.

The canopy geometry sets a practical ceiling: canopy height should not much exceed the row width, or rows shade one another. Narrow rows therefore imply short canopies, which is only workable where vigour is genuinely low. That single relationship explains most of the world's density map better than tradition does.

## Style, quality level and cost

At high volume — Riverland, La Mancha, California's Central Valley, Languedoc bulk — the design constraint is cost per litre and full mechanisation, so low to medium density on wide rows with irrigation is not a compromise but the correct answer for a wine whose quality is defined as clean, consistent varietal fruit. For sparkling base, high yields at modest ripeness are wanted, again achievable more cheaply with fewer, larger vines, though Champagne itself is high density for historical reasons. For premium Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc, moderate density with canopy division serves a style defined by aromatic intensity, not concentration. Only where the bottle price can amortise the establishment cost — single-site wines with an established market — is high density economically defensible on its own merits.

Two pressures are now moving the balance. Labour scarcity and mechanisation push new plantings towards wider rows, while drought is pushing warm-region growers to reduce density or widen rows so that each vine commands more soil moisture.

## Conclusion

Neither density is inherently superior, and assessing them as rival philosophies is the error. Density should be set by water availability first, then by site vigour, then by mechanisation and establishment cost, and only then by tradition, appellation minimums and style. High density earns its cost on low-vigour sites with reliable water, making wines whose price supports it; low density is the better answer wherever water is scarce, vigour is high, or the wine's price will not repay the investment. The commonest and most expensive mistake is copying a density from a famous region whose water balance is nothing like your own.
