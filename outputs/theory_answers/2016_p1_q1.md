## Defining effectiveness

Effectiveness cannot be assessed without a yardstick, so I set three. First, agronomic efficacy: does the option hold the pest or disease below the threshold at which crop and quality are lost, and does it do so reliably in a bad year rather than an average one? Second, economic effectiveness: what does it cost per hectare in materials, tractor passes, labour and yield forgone, and — the more important figure — what is the cost of the downside when it fails? Third, durability: does it degrade the soil, select for resistance, or fall foul of tightening regulation?

The second test governs the answer, because the fundamental constraint on the organic or biodynamic grower is not the absence of any single product but the absence of a systemic curative option. A conventional grower who misjudges a downy mildew infection can spray a penetrant fungicide and recover the crop. The organic grower cannot, so every decision must be taken earlier, with less information, and with the full loss on the table. That asymmetry — prophylaxis with no rescue — is what makes organic disease control an exercise in risk management by a grower with fewer resources than the neighbour over the fence.

## Two different logics

Organic and biodynamic control are conceptually different, and blurring them produces a weak answer. Organic control is substitution: prohibited synthetics are replaced by permitted contact materials, and the aim is containment — keeping inoculum below the damage threshold, spray to spray, through the season. Biodynamic control is preventive: the aim is a vine in balance, with moderate vigour and an open canopy, on the premise that a balanced vine is less susceptible, so that the contact treatments are needed less often and at lower rates. Biodynamics is organic plus a preventive philosophy, not an alternative toolkit; every biodynamic grower still uses copper and sulphur.

## The organic toolkit assessed

Copper against downy mildew and sulphur against powdery mildew are the load-bearing treatments, and their efficacy is genuinely good in dry, low-pressure climates — Mendoza, the Douro, inland Australia — where I would rate organic control as fully effective and barely more expensive than conventional. In Atlantic and maritime climates the assessment changes sharply. Both are protectant and non-systemic: they wash off, so they must be reapplied after each rain event, which in a wet Loire or Bordeaux season can mean fifteen or more passes against a conventional programme's six. The cost is not the material, which is cheap, but the diesel, the labour, the soil compaction from repeated passes and the physical impossibility of getting a tractor onto saturated ground at the moment cover is most needed. Durability is also poor: copper accumulates in soil and is toxic to soil biology, and the EU has progressively cut the permitted copper rate to a few kilograms per hectare per year averaged over several seasons. Effective, then, but climate-contingent and under regulatory pressure.

Bacillus thuringiensis against lepidopteran larvae is a genuine organic success: cheap, specific, and as effective as the conventional alternative. Pyrethrum is the weak point — permitted, but broad-spectrum, killing the beneficial predators the system depends on. Where flavescence doree, where insecticide treatment is compulsory in designated zones and the organic grower's only permitted option is pyrethrum, the organic grower is forced into the least selective material available. That is a real and under-discussed failure of the system.

I would not credit pheromone mating disruption against grape moth or raptor perches against birds as organic control at all. Both are now standard practice across sustainable and much conventional viticulture, so they neither distinguish the organic grower nor demonstrate the system's effectiveness.

## Canopy management: the most cost-effective lever

The cheapest and most durable control is cultural, and it is the one most growers underweight. Fruit-zone leaf removal shortly after set, shoot thinning to reduce shoot density, careful shoot positioning and timely trimming all lower humidity in the bunch zone, improve spray penetration to the fruit, and cut botrytis and powdery mildew pressure directly. Reducing vigour through a permanent inter-row cover crop does the same by producing a smaller, more open canopy. The economics are attractive: the cost is seasonal labour, incurred once, against a saving of several spray passes and a lower chance of catastrophic loss. This is where biodynamic prevention is genuinely vindicated — its emphasis on balance produces the canopy that makes the sprays work.

## Biodynamic preparations

Assessed against my three tests, the specifically biodynamic materials — the horn preparations, and the herbal teas and decoctions of horsetail, nettle and willow used as preventive tonics — have weak evidence of direct fungicidal efficacy, and I do not think a candidate should claim otherwise. Their cost is low, so a grower loses little by using them, and the practices that surround them (close observation, timely intervention, low vigour) plausibly deliver the benefit attributed to the preparations themselves.

## Assessment

Effectiveness is a function of climate before it is a function of philosophy. In dry climates organic and biodynamic control is effective and economically competitive. In wet maritime climates it is effective in most years and can fail completely in the worst, and the honest assessment is that the system works there only for growers whose margins absorb the occasional lost vintage. Ranked by return on cost, I would put canopy management first, copper and sulphur second as necessary but constrained, biological insecticides third, and the biodynamic preparations last on evidence — while conceding that the discipline they impose may be worth more than the materials.
