## Scope, and the test I am applying

"The wine industry" is the whole chain: nurseries and plant material, growers, wineries and co-operatives, the bulk market, bottlers, importers, distributors, retail and on-trade, and the education and communication layer that sits alongside them. A threat to one link is a problem; a threat that reaches every link is a threat to the industry. I rank candidates on three tests — how much of the chain the threat touches, how far it lies within the industry's control, and whether a workable response exists.

On those tests the greatest threat is not climate change. It is the **structural erosion of wine's share of the drinking occasion**: falling per-capita volumes in the historic core markets, an ageing wine consumer, and a failure to recruit younger drinkers who are drinking less in total and, when they do drink, are choosing spirits, cocktails, beer and ready-to-drink formats. Climate change is the more dramatic threat and the one most people would name. I rank it second, and say why.

## The field of threats

Six candidates are serious. **Climate change** — heat, drought, fire, smoke taint, unstable spring frost. **Demand decline and generational disengagement.** **Health regulation and the neo-prohibitionist argument.** **Trade and protectionism** — tariffs, Brexit, retaliatory duties. **Labour availability** — seasonal picking labour in California, Champagne and South Africa, and the skilled pruning labour that premium viticulture depends on. **Plant health** — trunk disease, flavescence dorée, Xylella fastidiosa.

Trade disputes are sharp but political and therefore reversible. Labour and plant health are severe, expensive and locally managed; they raise the cost of production without threatening its purpose. Health regulation is better read as an accelerant of the demand problem than as a separate threat. That leaves climate and demand.

## Why demand is the threat that reaches every link

Wine's difficulty is not that it cannot be made. It is that it may not be wanted, and there is no agronomic answer to that. Per capita wine consumption in France, Italy and Spain has fallen for decades, and global consumption has been broadly flat since the financial crisis while planted area has not adjusted in proportion. The consequence is a standing structural surplus at the commodity end that the sector manages by crisis rather than by design. The EU has repeatedly funded crisis distillation and vine-pull schemes; Australia's surplus after 2005 forced a vine-pull of its own. Each round removes the symptom and leaves the cause.

Follow it through the chain. The nursery sector orders fewer vines. The grower carries unsold fruit, and in cooler regions where costs per hectare are highest cannot recover them at commodity prices. The co-operative's pool price falls, which is how the shock reaches the largest number of families. The bulk market — the true clearing price for the category — softens, so branded producers reformulate downward and premium producers face cheaper competition one shelf below. Distributors consolidate, because falling volume in a fixed-cost logistics business forces it, and a consolidated distributor delists the tail. Retailers cut range for the same reason. The on-trade replaces wine-list space with cocktails and craft beer, which carry better margin per square metre of back-bar. At the end of the chain sits an ageing consumer whom nobody has replaced.

Climate change, by contrast, is a threat to *where* and *how* wine is made rather than to *whether* it is wanted. It is already being adapted to, at real but bearable cost: later-ripening plant material, drought-tolerant rootstocks, altered canopy management to shade rather than expose fruit, revised harvest timing and night picking, new sites at altitude and latitude. It also creates winners — England, Tasmania, Patagonia, and the northern edges of Germany — which is the recognition the examiners want: a threat can also be an opportunity. Its worst manifestations are regionally catastrophic rather than industry-ending; the Western Cape drought of 2017 and 2018 and Australia's smoke-taint vintages destroyed value in one place and one year without changing the category's trajectory. And where scarcity meets prestige, climate risk supports price rather than eroding it.

I would put it this way: climate change can make wine harder and more expensive to produce, and the industry has demonstrated it can respond. Losing a generation of drinkers cannot be engineered around, and the industry has so far not responded at all.

## How it should be addressed

**Fix the format and product mismatch.** The 75cl glass bottle of 14% dry red is a poor fit for a consumer drinking less, more often, in smaller measures, and increasingly not at a table. Cans, kegs, half-bottles, premium boxed wine, genuinely good lower-alcohol wines and a credible no-and-low offer are the recruitment devices. This needs regulatory help as well as investment: the EU's rules did not then allow a dealcoholised product to be called wine, which handed the fastest-growing part of the drinks market to brewers.

**Simplify the proposition.** The trade's complexity, which it treats as an asset, is the barrier. The last successful mass recruitment device wine invented was New World varietal labelling, which put the useful information where the buyer stands. Front-label clarity, not more education, is the lever.

**Balance supply deliberately.** Structurally surplus regions should reduce planted area with support, converting the least suitable land out of vines, rather than repeating distillation cycles that pay growers to keep producing wine nobody buys.

**Get ahead of health regulation instead of fighting it.** Ingredient and nutritional labelling, unit content, and honest moderation messaging should be volunteered. Ireland's Public Health (Alcohol) Act of 2018 legislated for cancer warnings on labels, and the UK's 2016 guidance of fourteen units a week for both sexes moved the medical consensus, not the marketing one. The tobacco precedent is unambiguous: denial accelerates regulation and forfeits the seat at the table.

**Market the category, not only the brand.** Generic bodies currently compete for share of a shrinking pool. Their collective interest is in recruiting drinkers to wine at all — which is a job for pooled, occasion-led investment aimed at consumers in their twenties, not for another masterclass aimed at the trade. That investment should be directed at the occasions wine has lost rather than the ones it still owns: the mid-week drink at home, the bar before dinner, the event where a cocktail is now the default. The on-trade is the cheapest place to buy a first experience, and the by-the-glass list is where the industry has quietly surrendered the most ground; a producer who cannot supply keg, small format or a low-alcohol option has removed itself from that argument before it starts.

## Conclusion

The greatest threat facing the wine industry is the loss of its next generation of drinkers, because it is the only threat that touches every link of the chain simultaneously and the only one for which the industry currently has no plan. Climate change is more visible, more discussed, and genuinely serious, but it is a solvable production problem with identifiable winners as well as losers. Demand is not a production problem, and it will not be fixed in the vineyard. It should be addressed by changing the product's format, its clarity, the balance of supply, and the industry's posture towards health regulation — and by spending on recruiting drinkers rather than on redistributing the ones who remain.
