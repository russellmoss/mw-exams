## Scope

I read this as a question about taste — what wine consumers now want in the glass — and not about the values they attach to a purchase. Sustainability, organic certification and provenance claims have grown enormously in the same decade, but they change what a consumer will forgive, not what they want to drink, so I leave them aside. I also treat "taste preference" as market-specific rather than global, which is the second half of my argument.

## The changes worth naming

Four shifts have mattered commercially.

**Lighter, fresher, less oaked.** The high-alcohol, heavily extracted, new-oak red that defined the 2000s has lost its consumer. Alcohol is being managed down and oak used as seasoning rather than as flavour.

**Dry rosé became a category rather than a season.** Pale, dry, savoury pink wine turned into a year-round, premium-capable style, which is a genuinely new consumer behaviour rather than a variation on an existing one.

**Sparkling wine became an everyday drink.** The occasion decoupled from celebration, and simultaneously the top of the category moved drier, with brut nature and extra brut now normal in Champagne.

**Texture and freshness at the fashionable end** — skin contact, lees, salinity, low sulphur — which is small in volume but sets the on-trade agenda that mainstream buyers follow three years later.

Two counter-currents keep this honest. Mainstream American taste moved towards *sweeter* reds, not drier ones, and the fastest-growing branded segment there is off-dry. And in China the preferred style remains full-bodied dry red with visible oak. Anyone who describes the decade as a single move towards lightness has only looked at one market.

## Regions that changed their offer, and what it bought them

**Provence** is the clearest case of a region deliberately narrowing its style to own a category. It standardised on pale, dry, fresh rosé, invested in presentation, and pushed the price ceiling upwards. That did not simply sell more rosé; it created a premium rosé occasion that had not existed and that other regions — Languedoc, Navarra, Marlborough — now supply beneath it.

**Prosecco** is the sharpest recent example. The DOC already sold vast volume, which is not the same as adapting. Prosecco DOC Rosé was approved in 2020 and reached shelves that winter, and it did two things at once: it captured the rosé consumer inside an already trusted brand, and it carried a higher price than white Prosecco, so it premiumised the category rather than merely extending it. That is what a successful evolution looks like — new consumers and better margin from the same vineyard.

**Australia** rebuilt an entire varietal offer. Chardonnay was remade over the decade in the Yarra, Adelaide Hills and Margaret River from broad and oaky to tight and mineral, and the same producers moved Shiraz towards a medium-bodied, whole-bunch style and rehabilitated Grenache from the Barossa's old vines. This restored Australia's credibility in exactly the premium on-trade that had abandoned it.

**Rioja and Vinho Verde** show Old World adaptation. Rioja's 2017 rule change, which created Viñedo Singular and village wines, legitimised the fresher, less oak-driven, site-expressive style its younger producers were already making. Vinho Verde moved from cheap spritzy semi-sweet to serious single-varietal Alvarinho and opened a price bracket it had never reached.

## The fortified regions: the hardest adaptation

Fortified wine faced the decade's taste shift head-on — high alcohol, sweetness, an association with an older consumer — and the responses are instructive.

**Port** attacked the occasion rather than the wine. White Port and tonic gave the category a summer, low-strength, mixed serve, and rosé Port aimed at the same consumer. Simultaneously the trade moved out of basic ruby and into aged tawny and single quinta, where the price and the margin are, and the Douro's growers built a genuine dry red table wine business on the back of the same vineyards.

**Jerez** repositioned rather than reformulated. En rama bottlings, single-vintage and single-vineyard releases, and the VOS and VORS age categories reframed Fino and Manzanilla as gastronomic wines sold by the glass at the table. Jerez has been reforming its own rules to give more recognition to vineyard names, ageing zones and unfortified wine styles, an explicit attempt to let style follow demand.

**Madeira** did something similar with single-varietal Colheitas. **Marsala** is the counter-case: little repositioning, and continued decline.

## Where the rules got in the way

Appellation law is the brake on Old World adaptation, and its effect is visible. A Barossa producer changed its Shiraz style in three vintages; a Chianti or Rioja producer needed a consorzio vote. That Bordeaux approved six new varieties for its regional appellations in 2021 is a landmark precisely because it took so long.

## Conclusion

The regions that succeeded did not follow taste — they picked one change and built an offer around it. Provence and Prosecco did that best, because each created a new occasion at a higher price rather than defending an old one; Australia executed the most difficult reversal; and among the fortified regions Port adapted the occasion most successfully while Jerez adapted the audience.
