## a) Smoke taint in grapes and wine

Smoke taint is the ashy, burnt, medicinal character taken up by vines exposed to bushfire smoke. The active compounds are volatile phenols from lignin pyrolysis — guaiacol, 4-methylguaiacol, syringol and the o-, m- and p-cresols — with detection thresholds in the low micrograms per litre. The mechanism that catches people out is that the vine glycosylates them: most of the taint sits in grapes as non-volatile glycosides, so juice analysis for free phenols badly understates the risk. Acid hydrolysis and yeast and oak glycosidases release them during fermentation and, critically, for years in bottle, which is why a wine passed at bottling can be undrinkable at three years.

Degree of taint depends on smoke density and duration, proximity to the fire, and phenological stage — exposure from véraison onwards is far worse than at flowering. Handling: hand-pick to exclude leaf matter, press whole bunches, take free run only, minimise skin contact, ferment cool, avoid glycosidase-active enzymes, then remediate with activated carbon, PVPP or casein fining, or reverse osmosis coupled to a phenolic-adsorbent resin. All of these strip fruit as well as taint. The honest judgement, and the one few candidates make, is that at high taint levels the correct decision is not to make wine at all — declassify, sell the fruit, or claim on crop insurance — because remediation cost plus reputational risk exceeds the value of a mid-priced wine, and the taint returns in bottle.

## b) Volatile acidity

VA is the fraction of a wine's acidity that is steam-distillable, dominated by acetic acid with ethyl acetate as the second compound of consequence — the two are usually discussed together because acetic acid smells of vinegar and its ester of nail varnish. EU caps total VA at 20 milliequivalents per litre for red, 1.2 g/L as acetic acid, and 18 for white and rosé, with derogations upwards for botrytised and long-aged styles; the US TTB limit is 1.4 g/L for red and 1.2 g/L for white. Acetic acid is generally detectable from around 0.6-0.9 g/L depending on the wine's weight, and ethyl acetate turns solventy above roughly 150-200 mg/L.

Acetobacter and Gluconobacter are the usual suspects but not the only ones. Saccharomyces itself produces acetic acid, more so under osmotic stress or nitrogen deficiency — hence high VA in high-sugar musts; lactic acid bacteria produce it from sugars in a sluggish MLF and from citric acid at the end of one; Brettanomyces produces it aerobically. Hygiene, full vessels, inert gas, adequate molecular SO2 and prompt completion of MLF are the controls. VA is not automatically a fault: at low levels it lifts aromatics, and it is part of the accepted character of traditional Amarone and of some classed-growth Bordeaux — the test is integration, not the number.

## c) Metatartaric acid

Metatartaric acid is a polyester of tartaric acid, made by heating tartaric acid under vacuum so the molecules esterify into chains. Added to wine it acts as a protective colloid: the chains adsorb onto the surface of nascent potassium bitartrate crystals and block their growth, so crystals never reach visible size. It does not remove tartrate — it prevents it precipitating.

It is capped at 100 mg/L in the EU and typically dosed at that rate. The advantages are real: it is very cheap, works instantly, needs no energy or capital equipment, and unlike cold stabilisation costs no volume, colour or aroma. The disadvantage is that the ester bonds hydrolyse back to tartaric acid, and the rate is temperature-dependent, so protection lasts perhaps 12-18 months in a cool cellar but only weeks at 25-30C — and once it fails, the wine is less stable than if it had never been treated. Against the alternatives: cold stabilisation is slow and energy-hungry but permanent; electrodialysis is permanent and gentle but capital-intensive; carboxymethylcellulose is a colloid like metatartaric but does not hydrolyse, which makes it the better modern choice for whites. Metatartaric therefore suits inexpensive, high-turnover wine bottled and drunk within a year, and is the wrong tool for a wine that will be aged or shipped through the tropics.

## d) Spinning cone technology

A spinning cone column is a counter-current stripping column: wine flows down over alternating fixed and rotating cones as a thin film, meeting nitrogen or steam rising through a vacuum, which pulls out volatiles at low temperature and so avoids the cooked character of ordinary distillation. It is run in two passes. The first, cooler pass removes a small aroma fraction which is set aside; the second, warmer pass strips ethanol. Recombining the aroma fraction with the dealcoholised base, then blending that back into untreated wine, is what allows a precise alcohol reduction with much of the aroma retained — this separation of the flavour fraction from the alcohol is the point of the technology.

Uses beyond alcohol reduction include desulphiting stored grape juice and concentrate, and partial removal of smoke taint or excess ethyl acetate. Regulation constrains it: the EU permits a reduction of no more than 20% of the alcohol for the product still to be sold as wine, with separate categories now for dealcoholised and partially dealcoholised wine. It is not reverse osmosis, which is a membrane separation at ambient temperature and pressure, and it is nothing like centrifugation, which separates solids by density and removes no volatiles at all.
