## Scope: the barrel as a container

A barrel in this question is a storage and maturation vessel, not a seasoning. The risks are those the vessel creates while the wine sits in it over months or years — oxygen ingress, microbial colonisation of the wood, volume loss, physical failure and contamination — and excess wood character counts as one of them rather than as the subject. I take each in turn with its mechanism, its mitigation and the timing at which the mitigation must happen, because timing is what separates control from damage.

## Oxidation

A 225 L barrique has a high surface-to-volume ratio and admits oxygen through the staves, the head joints and above all the bunghole. Ingress is of the order of 20-30 mg/L per year for a new barrique, falling as the pores fill; every racking adds a further 1-3 mg/L, and each mg/L of oxygen consumes roughly 4 mg/L of free SO2. Unmanaged, the outcome is acetaldehyde, whose sensory threshold in red wine is around 100 mg/L, browning through quinone formation, loss of primary fruit — and, because free SO2 is being spent, simultaneous loss of microbial protection.

The mitigation is a regime, not an act. Free SO2 is analysed monthly and adjusted to hold molecular SO2 at 0.5-0.8 mg/L, which at pH 3.6 means roughly 30-50 mg/L free; at pH 3.9 the same protection needs about double, which is why high-pH warm-climate reds are the hardest wines to store in wood. Barrels are topped weekly for the first month or two, when absorption into new wood is fastest, then every two to four weeks. Silicone or glass bungs seated tight at top dead centre seal better than a loose wooden bung. Racking is limited to what the wine needs — typically every three to four months — and done closed under inert gas rather than splashed. Where a wine must spend years in wood the structural answer is format: 500 L puncheons or 2,000-5,000 L foudres cut ingress per litre sharply, which is why Barolo's minimum of 18 months in wood is conventionally served by large old botti rather than new barriques.

## Microbiological spoilage

Wood is porous and cannot be sterilised; organisms penetrate several millimetres into the stave, beyond the reach of surface cleaning. Acetic acid bacteria oxidise ethanol to acetic acid and then to ethyl acetate wherever oxygen and warmth allow — an ullaged barrel, a wet bunghole, a barrel returned to service unwashed. The EU volatile acidity ceilings of 1.2 g/L for red and 1.08 g/L for white and rose, expressed as acetic acid, make a spoiled lot unsaleable, and the fault is obvious well below them. Brettanomyces ferments residual sugars and cellobiose released from the wood into 4-ethylphenol and 4-ethylguaiacol; the reported sensory threshold for 4-ethylphenol in red wine is of the order of 400 micrograms/L. Lactic acid bacteria will attack residual sugar heterofermentatively once malolactic conversion is done and SO2 is low.

Again the mitigations are timed. Finish malolactic conversion promptly and sulphur immediately afterwards, rather than leaving an unprotected wine in wood for weeks. Hold residual glucose and fructose below the few tenths of a gram per litre that Brettanomyces can still use. Check molecular SO2 monthly, not quarterly. Keep the cellar at 12-15 degrees C, since Brettanomyces growth accelerates markedly above about 18. Clean barrels immediately on emptying with high-pressure water above 80 degrees C, steam or ozonated water, and never leave them damp with lees. Retire suspect barrels rather than repair them: the cost of a barrique does not justify the risk to a blend. Analyse volatile acidity and, where Brett is a live risk, 4-ethylphenol barrel by barrel, so that one infected lot is found while it is still one barrel's problem.

## Volume loss, temperature and physical failure

Evaporation costs of the order of 2-5% of volume a year. In a dry cellar water leaves preferentially and alcohol concentrates; above roughly 75% relative humidity ethanol leaves faster, alcohol falls, and mould colonises the cellar. Both the ullage created and the wine used to fill it are risks. Mitigation is humidity and temperature held near 75% and 12-15 degrees C, topping with sound wine of the same lot, and budgeting a reserved topping volume at the outset for a long elevage. Barrels also weep at the heads after temperature swings and are broken in handling; racking systems, disciplined movement and inspection at every topping catch this early.

## Wood-derived and contamination risks

Excess extraction is a genuine fault when the vessel was chosen for storage: a medium-bodied red left two years in new small oak is dried out and hollow. Match format, age and toast to the wine's structure, keep a proportion of neutral and large wood, taste barrel by barrel at intervals and blend fractions rather than commit the lot. The graver contamination risk is haloanisole taint: TCA and TBA can arrive in the staves and destroy an entire lot invisibly. Buy from coopers who certify their wood, isolate any barrel that smells musty, and analyse rather than guess.

## Conclusion

The risks of wooden storage are systematic and predictable, and every one of them is answered by a scheduled action rather than by judgement in the moment: topping weekly then fortnightly, molecular SO2 verified monthly against the wine's pH, racking closed and infrequently, cellar climate held steady, barrels cleaned on emptying and retired when suspect, and analysis kept at barrel level. What cannot be mitigated inside a barrique — the cumulative oxygen a multi-year elevage demands — is not a management problem but a vessel problem, and the answer is a larger, older cask.
