## Stating the extent up front

Yeast creates most of the volatile compounds in a finished wine that were not in the grape, and it can destroy a wine outright. But it cannot supply what the fruit does not contain. My position is therefore a graded one: yeast's positive influence on style is greatest in neutral or precursor-driven whites and in high-volume wines, and smallest in structurally driven reds where extraction, oak and time dominate. Its influence on *quality* is asymmetric — its capacity to ruin a wine, through stuck fermentation, sulphides or spoilage, is far greater than its capacity to elevate one.

## The organisms involved

*Saccharomyces cerevisiae* completes almost every fermentation, with *Saccharomyces uvarum* and *Saccharomyces bayanus* and their interspecific hybrids used for low-temperature and high-alcohol ferments and for sparkling base wines. An uninoculated must, however, begins with a mixed population: *Hanseniaspora uvarum*, *Metschnikowia pulcherrima*, *Torulaspora delbrueckii*, *Lachancea thermotolerans* and *Starmerella bacillaris*, most of which die out between 4 and 7 per cent alcohol having already contributed acids, glycerol and aroma. The spoilage organisms are *Brettanomyces bruxellensis*, whose teleomorph is *Dekkera bruxellensis*, and the sulphur-tolerant refermenters *Zygosaccharomyces bailii* and *Saccharomycodes ludwigii*. And a distinct group defines whole categories of wine: the flor races of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* — beticus, montuliensis and cheresiensis — that form the velo in Jerez and the voile under which Vin Jaune ages in the Jura.

## How yeast makes style

**Fermentation kinetics.** The first contribution is finishing the job. Strains differ in alcohol tolerance, temperature range, nitrogen demand and fructophilic character, and a wine that sticks at 8 g/L of sugar has a microbiological problem for life. This is the single largest quality decision in strain selection and it is rarely what candidates discuss.

**Esters and higher alcohols.** Yeast alcohol acetyltransferases form the acetate esters — isoamyl acetate, 2-phenylethyl acetate — and ethyl esters of fatty acids, and these are the aroma of most young, fruity white wine in the world. Their formation is strain-specific but heavily modulated by temperature and assimilable nitrogen, which is why a bulk Chilean or Pays d'Oc white can be aromatically engineered by yeast, nutrient and cooling alone.

**Release of bound grape aroma.** Yeast beta-lyase cleaves cysteine- and glutathione-bound precursors to liberate the volatile thiols responsible for Marlborough and Loire Sauvignon Blanc; yeast beta-glucosidase liberates monoterpenes in Muscat and Riesling. Here the yeast is not creating aroma but unlocking what the vineyard supplied — the clearest illustration of the limit on its influence.

**Texture, colour and stability.** Strains differ in glycerol and in polysaccharide and mannoprotein release; mannoproteins soften the palate, help protein and tartrate stability and stabilise colour. Extended lees ageing, as in Champagne and Muscadet sur lie, is yeast influence continuing long after fermentation. Some strains adsorb anthocyanins onto their cell walls and lighten a red; others produce more pyruvate and acetaldehyde and so form more of the stable pyranoanthocyanins that hold colour for decades.

**Modulating alcohol and acid.** This is where current research sits. *Lachancea thermotolerans* produces lactic acid during fermentation, lowering pH in warm-climate musts in Spain and Australia where acidification is otherwise the only tool; *Starmerella bacillaris* diverts carbon to glycerol and can shave a modest amount of ethanol; *Torulaspora delbrueckii* is used in sequential inoculation for aromatic complexity with low volatile acidity. Sequential and co-inoculation of these with *Saccharomyces* is a genuinely modern technique.

## Where yeast destroys or defines quality

Sulphide production is strain-dependent and nitrogen-dependent: a high-demand strain in a low-nitrogen must generates hydrogen sulphide and mercaptans, and low-sulphide strains exist precisely because of this. Some strains produce enough SO2 to inhibit the malolactic bacteria the winemaker was counting on.

*Brettanomyces bruxellensis* decarboxylates hydroxycinnamic acids and reduces the vinylphenols to 4-ethylphenol and 4-ethylguaiacol, which at threshold gives spice and beyond it gives a wine that tastes of nothing else. *Zygosaccharomyces bailii* and *Saccharomycodes ludwigii* are notably resistant to sulphur dioxide and to sorbate, which is why a sweet wine bottled without sterile filtration is at risk of refermenting.

Yet the flor yeasts show that "spoilage" is a matter of context, not organism. Under the velo in Jerez, yeast metabolises alcohol and glycerol aerobically, produces acetaldehyde and consumes oxygen, and creates the entire character of Fino and of Vin Jaune. To say that spoilage yeast expresses terroir is loose thinking, but to say that a film yeast can be the defining quality driver is simply true.

## Inoculated, ambient, and what the choice actually buys

Inoculation buys reliability, reproducibility and a predictable aromatic outcome; ambient fermentation buys a mixed-population contribution, more textural breadth, more vintage variation and more risk. This is not an old-world and new-world divide — most of Burgundy inoculates, much of New Zealand and California does not — and treating it as one is exactly the kind of generalisation that shows a candidate has not looked. Nor is it true that selected yeasts make only short-lived wines; longevity is set by pH, phenolics, SO2 and oxygen management, not by the strain.

## Conclusion

Yeast influences style profoundly and quality decisively, but not independently. It determines which of the grape's precursors are expressed, adds a layer of fermentation aroma, contributes texture and colour stability, and can modestly shift alcohol and acid. What it cannot do is add ripeness, structure or concentration. So: a large influence on style, a larger one on the risk of failure, and a small one on the ceiling of quality, which the vineyard sets.
