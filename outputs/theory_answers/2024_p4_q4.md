## Defining profitability, and the formats in scope

Profitability is hard to isolate in wine because the value chain has many stakeholders and the same format sits in different hands at each stage. I therefore assess it on two measures rather than one: gross margin — cash and percentage per unit sold — and return on the capital invested to make that unit possible. The two frequently disagree, and the disagreement is the substance of this question: a format can carry a fat percentage margin while destroying returns because of the plant it demands, and a thin-margin format can be highly profitable because it needs almost no capital at all.

The formats I consider run from traditional to innovative: standard glass at various weights, including large formats; wine shipped in bulk and packaged in market; bag-in-box; keg; can; PET and pouch; and the newer paper and flat bottles.

## The production view

**Materials and freight.** Glass is normally the largest single non-wine cost in an inexpensive bottle, and its cost is energy-linked and therefore volatile. It is also heavy: a standard 750ml bottle weighs around 400 to 500 grams and a lightweight one closer to 300, and the producer pays freight on that weight over the whole distance to market. Bulk shipping removes it — a flexitank holds of the order of 24,000 litres, roughly two and a half times what the same container carries in packaged bottles — which is why so much branded entry-level wine from Australia, Chile and South Africa is now bottled in the UK or the Netherlands. The margin gain is real but it transfers packaging control, and hence some brand control, to the customer.

**Capital.** This is where formats separate. A high-speed bottling line is a substantial and often prohibitive investment for a producer of any size below the very largest, which is why contract bottling and mobile bottling exist. Canning has the same shape at smaller scale: mobile canning services let a producer test the format for the cost of a day's hire rather than a line. Bag-in-box filling is comparatively cheap. Kegs are the outlier — a returnable stainless keg fleet is working capital permanently tied up in assets that spend most of the year in someone else's cellar or in transit, so one-way PET kegs trade a higher unit cost for a far lower capital commitment.

**Line economics.** Unit cost falls sharply with run length and rises with changeovers, so a producer running six formats on one line loses in downtime what it hoped to gain in market coverage. Minimum order quantities on glass moulds, closures and print add to this. The profitable answer is usually fewer formats and longer runs.

## The retail view

**Percentage margin versus cash margin.** Retailers manage cash margin per unit and per metre of shelf, not percentage. A three-litre bag-in-box at a mid price generates far more cash margin per facing than a cheap bottle, and turns space efficiently, which is why it earns its listing in some markets. Equally, a fine wine at a low percentage margin can outperform because the cash per unit is large.

**Rate of sale and capital.** Retail profitability is a return-on-inventory question. Formats that turn quickly release capital; slow-moving premium formats and large bottles tie it up and carry breakage and shrinkage risk.

**Consumer perception is a hard price ceiling.** This is the decisive retail variable. Cans have solved the technical problems and suit new occasions, but consumers will not yet pay premium prices per litre in a can, so the format's profitability is capped regardless of its production cost. Bag-in-box carries a value association in the UK that it simply does not carry in France or Scandinavia. Glass weight, conversely, still signals quality, which is why heavy bottles persist commercially despite their environmental cost.

**Bag-in-box, precisely.** It is not a general default for house wine: British on-trade house wine remains overwhelmingly bottle or keg. Bag-in-box takes a very large share of Systembolaget volume and a substantial share of French supermarket volume, and it has a growing premium tier in the United States, but its penetration is market-specific and channel-specific. Its genuine functional advantage is that an opened bag-in-box holds condition for around four to six weeks, which is exactly why the format wins at home consumption and at low-volume by-the-glass service, and loses where the occasion is a shared bottle.

**Monopolies shape the economics.** The Scandinavian monopolies have pushed lightweight glass and bag-in-box hard through their tender specifications and climate scoring, and provincial Canadian boards apply similar leverage. For a supplier, format choice in those markets is not a marketing decision but a listing requirement, and the associated production capital has to be justified against a single customer.

**Duty distorts everything.** Where duty is levied per bottle or per litre rather than by value, it compresses the wine's share of the retail price at the bottom of the market: on a bottle retailing in the UK at around six pounds, duty and packaging take the great majority of the price and the wine itself accounts for very little of it. Any format that reduces packaging and freight cost therefore has disproportionate leverage on profitability at that price point, and almost none at fifty pounds.

## Conclusion

No format is profitable in itself. Profitability comes from the fit between format, price point, rate of sale and capital structure. At the bottom of the market, where duty and glass dominate the price, bulk shipment and bag-in-box are the profitable answers for both producer and retailer. In the middle, keg and can can be highly profitable in the right channel, but only if the capital is rented rather than bought. At the top, glass remains the most profitable format because consumer perception, not cost, sets the price — and the producer's discipline should be to reduce its weight without surrendering the perception.
