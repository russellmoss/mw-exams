## Scope and the axes of comparison

I take three: bag-in-box, the PET bottle, and the aluminium can. Consumer perception and acceptance are not the issue here; the question is what each material does to the wine and to the filling operation. I compare them on five technical axes — oxygen barrier, carbon dioxide and moisture retention, light barrier, chemical inertness (scalping, migration, corrosion), and process and mechanical compatibility — and conclude by matching pack to wine style on shelf-life grounds.

## The three specifications

Precision about material matters, because plastics differ enormously. A bag-in-box bladder is a multilayer laminate: an inner low-density polyethylene contact layer chosen for sealability and inertness, a barrier core of ethylene vinyl alcohol (EVOH) or metallised PET, and an outer protective layer, with a moulded polyethylene tap fitment. A wine PET bottle is either mono-PET, which is a poor oxygen barrier on its own, or barrier PET — multilayer with an amorphous nylon or EVOH core, or an oxygen-scavenging layer, or an internal plasma-deposited silicon oxide coating. A can is aluminium with an internal organic lacquer, typically epoxy-based or a BPA-non-intent polyester or acrylic, and it is that few-micron liner, not the metal, that contacts the wine.

## Oxygen

This is the decisive axis and it separates the three cleanly.

Bag-in-box has the highest ingress, and it is continuous. Oxygen enters through the film, through the seal seams and above all through the tap fitment, which is the weakest point; the oxygen transmission rate is quoted per pack per day and the pack therefore has a permanent oxygen demand. That dictates the whole fill specification: bags evacuated before filling, dissolved oxygen below about 0.5 mg/L at fill, and free SO2 set higher than for the same wine in glass and calculated from pH to a molecular target. Working life is roughly six to nine months.

PET sits in the middle and is the most variable, which is why specifying the construction matters: mono-PET gives around nine to twelve months, and barrier or scavenger constructions can be extended to two years or more. The scavenger, though, is consumed, so the pack's protection has a finite life that is not visible on shelf.

The can is the best oxygen barrier of the three — the metal wall is absolute — but it is not an oxygen-free pack, because headspace at seaming and oxygen dissolved in the wine remain. Undercover gassing and inert purge at the seamer are the controls.

## Carbon dioxide and moisture

The comparison reverses in part here. Aluminium retains CO2 completely, so the can is the only one of the three genuinely suited to sparkling wine; indeed it requires dissolved CO2 of roughly 1.5 to 2.6 g/L for internal pressure to give the thin wall its rigidity, so an under-carbonated can panels and dents.

PET loses CO2 slowly through the wall, so sparkling wine in PET loses pressure over months, and it also transmits water vapour, so a long-stored bottle loses volume and shifts alcohol and analysis marginally.

Bag-in-box is the opposite problem: it cannot tolerate CO2. Dissolved CO2 inflates the bladder, stresses the seams and fitment and causes weeping taps, so it is stripped to a low release specification by nitrogen sparging. The bag also needs a deliberate small headspace, since a brimful pack filled cold and stored warm splits at the seam.

## Light

The carton of a bag-in-box and the aluminium of a can both exclude light completely, so lightstrike is engineered out of both — a real advantage over flint glass for thiol-driven whites and rosés. PET is the exception: clear PET transmits UV and visible light and requires a UV blocker in the resin, or pigmentation, or the pack must be secondary-packed.

## Inertness: scalping, migration and corrosion

Glass is inert; none of these three is.

Polyethylene contact layers scalp: hydrophobic aroma compounds partition into the polymer, and volatile thiols and esters are the most vulnerable, which is why bag-in-box film selection is an organoleptic decision, not just a barrier one. PET scalps less than polyolefins but carries its own risk in acetaldehyde migration from the resin, which is why wine-grade PET must be low-acetaldehyde and recycled content food-grade and traceable.

The can has the most demanding chemistry. Wine is acidic and conductive, so any pinhole, scratch or coverage defect in the liner exposes aluminium, and corrosion follows with hydrogen evolution, metallic and reductive off-odours including hydrogen sulphide, and eventually a leaking can. Free SO2 and chloride accelerate it, so for canning free SO2 is normally held low, of the order of 25 to 35 mg/L, chloride and copper are specified and monitored, and liner integrity is verified by enamel-rater testing. That is a real technical constraint: the pack that has the best oxygen barrier forces the lowest SO2.

## Process and mechanical implications

Filling differs fundamentally. Bag-in-box is an aseptic fill into a pre-sterilised bag that cannot be sterilised in the winery, so sterility moves upstream to membrane filtration and supplier control. Cans require counter-pressure filling, a double-seam that must be dimensionally measured every shift, and can be tunnel-pasteurised. PET can be filled on adapted glass lines but is heat-sensitive and thermally deformable unless heat-set, and its light weight makes line stability and top-load strength the constraints.

## Conclusion

The three are not interchangeable. The can offers the best oxygen and CO2 barrier and total light exclusion, but constrains SO2 and demands corrosion control, and it suits sparkling and fresh single-serve wines. PET is the compromise pack, adequate for a year and only with barrier construction, its weakness moisture and light. Bag-in-box has the shortest life and the largest fill specification burden, but excludes light and suits high-volume everyday wine with a stated best-before date. In every case the technical answer is the same: match the pack's ingress rate to the wine's intended life.
