## The test I am applying

Blind tasting is not relevant or irrelevant in the abstract. It is a method, and a method is relevant to the extent that it answers the question being asked of it. So the useful test is not "is blind tasting good?" but "what assessment task is in front of us, and does removing the label improve the answer to it?" Assessment itself is plural: it can mean judging intrinsic quality, judging typicity, judging value for money, judging drinkability now, judging suitability for a listing, or judging a candidate's competence. My argument is that blind tasting is highly relevant to some of these and actively unhelpful for others, and that most of the confusion in the trade comes from applying it to tasks it was never suited to.

## The practice

The term covers several distinct practices that deserve separating. Single-blind tasting conceals the label but discloses the flight — twelve Chardonnays, a Rhône vintage report — and is the normal working method of the trade. Double-blind conceals everything, and is the harder discipline used in examinations and in wagers. Competition judging is single-blind within declared classes, usually with price and sometimes vintage disclosed. And there is the semi-blind of a professional tasting where bottles are bagged but the room knows the producers involved. These are different instruments with different accuracies, and lumping them together is the first mistake.

Blind tasting also has an inescapable structural feature: it strips context. That is precisely its virtue and precisely its limitation, and everything that follows is a consequence of it.

## Where blind tasting is highly relevant

For the buyer, it is the single most relevant assessment tool available. A merchant or supermarket buyer choosing between forty samples at a price point is assessing one thing — which liquid best serves the customer at that cost — and label, relationship and the supplier's hospitality all corrupt that judgement in predictable directions. Blind assessment against a written specification is not merely useful here, it is the professional standard, and a buyer who tastes sighted is being paid to make a decision they are systematically unable to make.

For competition judges the relevance is equally high, because a medal claims to be an assessment of the wine rather than of the entrant. The credibility of the whole apparatus rests on the labels being covered. That said, competitions demonstrate the method's limits as clearly as its value, and I return to that below.

For students and examiners it is the most relevant technique there is, because what is being assessed is not the wine at all but the taster. My own recent experience of the MW practical exam is a good illustration. Thirty-six wines in three days, entirely unseen, is a poor way to judge those wines — I would not want to publish a note on any of them — but it is an excellent way to establish whether a candidate can read structure, infer climate, deduce winemaking and place a wine in the market from the glass alone. The exam is not assessing the wines; it is using the wines to assess me. Once that distinction is clear, most arguments about blind tasting's unfairness answer themselves.

For producers, blind tasting is relevant in a narrower but important way: benchmarking. Tasting your own wine unlabelled alongside the competitive set, ideally assessed by someone with no stake in it, is the only reliable check on the self-flattery that afflicts every cellar. Blending trials, quality-control triangle tests and technical assessment of faults are all necessarily blind, and no serious winery does them any other way.

## Where its relevance is limited

For the journalist and the critic the position is more finely balanced. Blind assessment protects against the accusation that ratings follow reputation and access, which is a real and damaging accusation. But a critic's job is not only to score; it is to explain, to place a wine in a producer's trajectory, and to advise on when to drink it. Those tasks require exactly the context that blindness removes. A young classed-growth claret, a Barolo from a structured vintage, or a mature Rioja assessed cold and out of context will be systematically undervalued relative to a soft, immediately expressive wine. The honest position is that blind tasting is highly relevant to the score and largely irrelevant to the commentary, and the better critics tell readers which method produced which.

For the consumer, blind tasting is of low direct relevance, and I think the trade is dishonest about this. Nobody drinks blind. Wine is consumed with a label, a price, a place, a companion and an occasion, and the pleasure it gives is measurably affected by all of them. To tell a consumer that their enjoyment of a wine is invalid because they would not have picked it out blind is to assess something they were never trying to do. Blind tasting's relevance to consumers is indirect: it improves the quality of what reaches the shelf.

The method also has intrinsic limitations that constrain its authority everywhere. Flight order, palate fatigue, temperature and glassware all move results. Wines that are loud — oaky, sweet, high in alcohol, aromatically forceful — reliably outperform subtle ones in large blind flights, which is why blind competition results skew towards a particular style and why delicate wines, aged wines and oxidative styles such as fino sherry are so poorly served by the format. Some categories cannot sensibly be assessed blind at all: a wine whose entire proposition is provenance or age cannot be judged as though provenance did not exist.

## What the science says

The evidence on taster reliability is genuinely uncomfortable and any honest answer must engage with it. Studies of repeat pours at a large American wine competition found that experienced judges frequently failed to score the same wine consistently within a single sitting, and the experiment in which trained tasters described a white wine dyed red using red-wine descriptors showed how strongly expectation shapes perception. But I read this as an argument about how blind results should be used, not against blindness. It tells us that a single blind score is noise and a consensus of several tasters over repeated exposures is signal — which is exactly how well-run panels and examination boards already operate.

## Conclusion

Blind tasting is highly relevant, but not universally so, and its relevance is a function of the task. Where the question is comparative and the answer must be uncontaminated by commercial interest — buying, competition judging, blending, quality control, examining — it is indispensable, and the 1976 Paris tasting is the standing demonstration of what it can reveal that sighted assessment cannot. Where the question is about context, maturity, trajectory or pleasure, it is the wrong instrument, and forcing it on those tasks produces confident answers to questions nobody asked. My position is that blind tasting should be defended vigorously as the trade's assessment method and abandoned entirely as a test of anybody's enjoyment.
