## What counts as a preservative

A preservative is a substance added to wine to prevent decomposition, whether by microbial growth or by undesirable chemical change, oxidation above all. That definition fixes the scope narrowly. Fining, protein and cold stabilisation, filtration and must treatments all protect a wine, but they are physical or clarifying operations rather than preservative additions, and I set them aside; so too inert gases, which exclude oxygen rather than react with it. What remains is a short list: sulphur dioxide, ascorbic acid, sorbic acid, dimethyl dicarbonate (DMDC), lysozyme, chitosan and, most recently, fumaric acid. I compare them on five axes — what they act against, whether they work unaided, dose and legal ceiling, sensory and cost penalty, and consequence for the label.

## What each acts against

SO2 alone is both antimicrobial and antioxidant, and that is the most important contrast in the question. Its molecular fraction inhibits yeast and bacteria; its free fraction binds acetaldehyde, scavenges quinones and mops up hydrogen peroxide, slowing browning. Every other option is a specialist.

Ascorbic acid is purely antioxidant, and only as a fast oxygen scavenger; it does nothing to microbes. Sorbic acid, DMDC, lysozyme and chitosan are purely antimicrobial, with sharply different spectra. Sorbic acid inhibits yeast but not bacteria. Lysozyme lyses gram-positive lactic acid bacteria and leaves yeast and acetic acid bacteria untouched. DMDC kills yeast on contact and is markedly weaker against bacteria. Chitosan is used chiefly against *Brettanomyces*, and fumaric acid to inhibit malolactic bacteria. The choice therefore starts from the named threat: a wine with residual sugar faces a yeast problem, a high-pH red a bacterial one, and a fragile aromatic white bottled with oxygen pickup a purely chemical one.

## Whether they work unaided

The second contrast is dependency, and it is decisive. SO2 works alone; nothing else reliably does.

Ascorbic acid is the clearest case. It consumes oxygen quickly but yields hydrogen peroxide, so without a working minimum of about 30 mg/L free SO2 beside it, it accelerates the browning it was added to prevent. Sorbic acid fails differently: lactic acid bacteria metabolise it to the geranium taint, so it also requires SO2 cover. DMDC has no residual activity whatever — it hydrolyses within hours to methanol and carbon dioxide, so it only works on wine already filtered to a low cell count, and only if the filler is aseptic. Lysozyme allows early SO2 to be reduced or delayed, but it is a partial substitute, never a replacement. The honest conclusion of the comparison is that the alternatives are complements to SO2, not options instead of it.

## Dose, pH and legal ceiling

SO2 is dosed to a molecular target of roughly 0.5-0.8 mg/L, which requires around 30 mg/L free at pH 3.4 and nearer 75 mg/L at pH 3.8. That pH dependence is itself a contrast: sorbic acid likewise weakens as pH rises, whereas DMDC and lysozyme are largely indifferent to it. EU organic ceilings sit 50 mg/L below the conventional ones for dry wines - 100 mg/L red and 150 mg/L white and rose below 2 g/L residual sugar - and at least 30 mg/L below for all sweeter categories.

Against that, sorbic acid is capped at 200 mg/L in the EU and used close to it, 150-200 mg/L. Ascorbic acid is capped at 250 mg/L and is added in-line immediately before the filler. DMDC is capped at 200 mg/L and dosed at the cap. Lysozyme is capped at 500 mg/L and used at 250-500 mg/L. Fumaric acid is permitted at low tens of grams per hectolitre to block malolactic fermentation, and is the newest of the group.

Cost tracks that spread. SO2 as potassium metabisulphite is trivially cheap; sorbate and ascorbic acid are cheap; lysozyme and DMDC are expensive, and DMDC additionally demands dedicated metering equipment and careful handling because it is acutely toxic before it hydrolyses.

## Sensory and cost penalty

Excess SO2 is pungent, mutes fruit and bleaches young red colour, and it is the option with the greatest capacity to spoil the wine it protects. Sorbate at legal dose can read as a slightly confected note even before any geranium fault appears, which is why it is used almost exclusively in off-dry commercial wines rather than fine wine. Lysozyme can strip colour from reds and leave a protein instability in whites that then needs bentonite. Chitosan can flatten aroma if overdosed. DMDC's advantage is precisely that at legal dose it is sensorially silent — you buy microbiological security without touching the wine.

## Label and market consequence

SO2 above 10 mg/L total requires an allergen declaration in the EU and US, a real constraint for producers courting sulphite-averse consumers. Lysozyme is egg-derived: it is an allergen requiring declaration and it is neither vegan nor acceptable to many organic schemes. Chitosan from crustacean shell is not vegan, though the fungal-sourced form is. DMDC requires no declaration at all but is indefensible in natural-wine positioning. Organic and low-intervention certifications narrow the whole list at once.

## Conclusion

No option matches SO2's combination of dual action, independence, low cost and legal headroom, and the comparison shows why no single alternative has displaced it. The others are best understood as targeted risk reducers that let total SO2 fall: ascorbic acid buys oxygen protection at bottling, sorbic acid guards residual sugar, lysozyme and fumaric acid control bacteria, and DMDC delivers sterility without sensory cost. The skill is in matching the specific instability to the specific agent rather than reaching for more sulphur.
