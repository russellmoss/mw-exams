## Scope, and what I am assessing

**Drought tolerance** here means the ability of a vineyard to complete ripening at commercial yield and quality on a water supply below the vine's evapotranspirative demand. It is a property of the whole system — rootstock, scion, soil, floor, canopy and water delivery — not of the plant alone, and it is achieved in the vineyard. I therefore leave aside winery correction and confine myself to viticulture. Nor is this a question about climate change: drought is a permanent condition of Mendoza, inland Australia and much of the Mediterranean, and the techniques below are already standard there.

My assessment in one line: the highest-leverage tools are the ones fixed at establishment — rootstock, variety and soil — while in-season tools buy time; and no single tool is sufficient, because they interact.

## Plant material: rootstock, cultivar and new crosses

Rootstock is the most effective and most underused lever. The *rupestris* and *berlandieri* parentages — 110R, 140Ru and 1103P — root deeply and confer the best drought tolerance, while Riparia Gloire and 101-14 are shallow-rooting and fail early under deficit. Where drought comes with salinity, as in the Murray-Darling and parts of Mendoza, Ramsey combines vigour with salt exclusion. Rootstock also lets a grower change drought behaviour without changing the wine, which is why it is the first choice in an established appellation.

Cultivar change is more powerful but slower and often illegal. Drought-adapted varieties are a real inheritance: Grenache, Carignan, Monastrell and Bobal in Spain and the Midi; Assyrtiko on Santorini, farmed unirrigated in a near-desert; Touriga Nacional and the Douro field-blend varieties; Xinomavro and Agiorgitiko. Isohydric varieties such as Grenache close stomata early and protect the plant; anisohydric ones such as Syrah keep transpiring and risk hydraulic failure — a distinction that should drive selection. Very few candidates for this exam discuss **new crosses and hybrids**, but they matter: breeding programmes are now selecting for water-use efficiency alongside disease resistance, and interspecific crosses with drought-adapted American and Asian species are the medium-term answer where the traditional variety cannot be kept.

## Soil analysis and amendment

Soil is the vineyard's reservoir and is routinely ignored. Detailed soil survey — pits, texture, depth to restricting layer, EC and organic matter — tells the grower how much plant-available water the site actually holds, and where within the block it varies. Raising organic matter with compost and mulch increases water-holding capacity and lowers soil temperature; ripping compaction pans before replanting restores rooting depth; gypsum improves sodic soil structure so water infiltrates rather than runs off. These are slow, cheap and cumulative.

## Floor and canopy management

Cover crops cut both ways and must be assessed, not assumed. They build structure, infiltration and organic matter, but they compete for water; the workable compromise across Mediterranean Europe is alternate-row cover, mown or terminated at flowering, with under-vine mulch instead of cultivation. Straw or bark mulch reduces evaporation directly.

Canopy management is the omission the examiners see most. Transpiration is proportional to exposed leaf area, so a smaller, well-positioned canopy simply uses less water. Lower bud numbers, shorter shoot trimming and modest leaf area per unit crop reduce demand; but leaves must be retained on the afternoon side, since a stressed vine with exposed fruit sunburns. Bush vines in La Mancha, Sicily and the southern Rhône shade their own fruit and are a drought-adapted training system, not a backward one. Wider row spacing and lower density reduce competition where water is the limiting factor — the opposite of the density logic in a wet region.

## Irrigation regimes and measurement

Where irrigation exists, the gain is in scheduling. Regulated deficit irrigation and partial rootzone drying hold a controlled mild stress from set to veraison, reducing berry size and vegetative growth while protecting function. The measurement tools now decide the schedule: pressure chamber stem water potential, sap-flow sensors, soil capacitance probes, and airborne or satellite remote sensing — infra-red and thermal aerial photography maps canopy temperature and NDVI maps vigour, so water is directed to the blocks that need it and stress is mapped rather than guessed. Subsurface drip cuts evaporative loss further.

## Law, allocation, and what water actually exists

Two constraints are decisive and frequently ignored. Irrigation is restricted or requires derogation across much of the EU appellation system, and those rules are now changing under drought pressure — which is itself a viticultural variable a grower must track. And where irrigation is permitted, the water may not be there: allocations have been cut in the Murray-Darling, in Chile's central valleys and in the Western Cape. Planning drought tolerance around irrigation you may not be allowed, or able, to apply is the central planning error.

## Research

The research base is where the next tools come from and it is thinly known: INRAE's long-running variety-adaptation trials in Bordeaux, which underpinned the decision to admit drought-adapted varieties including Touriga Nacional and Marselan; rootstock breeding at UC Davis; the AWRI and Australian work on deficit regimes; Geisenheim on canopy and water relations.

## Conclusion

Drought tolerance is achieved by interlocking these levers, not by choosing among them: a deep-rooting rootstock under an adapted variety, on a soil rebuilt to hold water, carrying a restrained canopy, with a mulched floor and a metered deficit schedule. Any one alone fails; the combination is what carries a vineyard through a dry decade.
