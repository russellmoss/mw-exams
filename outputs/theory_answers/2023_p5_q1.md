## What traditional packaging is, and what I am forecasting

Traditional wine packaging is the longest-established combination still in general use: a 750 ml glass bottle, sealed with natural cork, dressed with printed paper front and back labels, finished with a tin or polylaminate capsule, and shipped in a twelve-bottle cardboard or wooden case. Five components, each with a different cost structure, function and vulnerability. Most discussion of this subject collapses into glass versus its alternatives, which addresses a fifth of the package; the closure, the label and the capsule are where the fastest change is actually happening.

By "the future" I mean the ten to fifteen years over which a producer's bottling-line and packaging investment is amortised. My forecast is that traditional packaging will not be displaced but stratified. Glass, cork, paper and the wooden case consolidate at the premium end, where they are functionally and semiotically justified and where the consumer will pay for them. The commercial tier below roughly £10 retail migrates decisively to bulk shipment, lighter glass, alternative closures and, for a growing minority, alternative containers. Traditional packaging survives by ceasing to be the default and becoming a marker of category.

## The container

For most commercial wine, packaging and distribution dominate the carbon footprint, typically exceeding the combined vineyard and winery contribution, and the glass bottle is the largest single item within that. Three responses follow, and they are not equally radical.

*Lightweighting* is already mainstream, and it is buyers rather than producers who are forcing it. Jancis Robinson's decision to publish bottle weights in her tasting notes is the example every commentator reaches for, but it is a reputational nudge; the harder lever is procurement. Systembolaget, Sweden's retail monopoly, has imposed maximum bottle weights in its tenders, of the order of 420 g for a standard still-wine bottle, and a monopoly buyer issues a mandate rather than a preference. Several UK multiples now set comparable specifications.

*Bulk shipment with filling in market* is the bigger lever and gets less attention. A flexitank carries roughly two and a half times as much wine per shipping container as the same wine packaged in glass, so the saving compounds across freight, fuel and handling. It is why most New Zealand, Australian, Chilean and South African wine on UK supermarket shelves is filled in Britain, and it also explains why bulk and lightweighting are the same commercial argument arriving from two directions.

*Substitution* is real but bounded by shelf-life. Bag-in-box is the majority of still wine volume in Sweden and Norway, so consumer resistance is a cultural variable, not a constant. Cans suit single-serve and outdoor occasions, and aluminium is infinitely recyclable and achieves collection rates above 90% in markets with deposit-return schemes. Flat rPET bottles that fit a letterbox and paper bottles with a thin polymer liner both cut weight sharply. All of them share one constraint: oxygen transmission. A three-litre bag holds quality for months, not years, and a can's internal lacquer is challenged by low pH and free sulphur. That is why substitution will take the fast-turning commercial tier and leave everything intended to be cellared alone.

## The closure

The closure debate is the one part of this subject where the industry has already run the experiment. New Zealand converted to screwcap almost completely within a few years of the Screwcap Wine Seal Initiative in 2001, and Australia followed; a generation of consumers in those markets attaches no price signal to a screwcap at all. Cork's response has been technical rather than rhetorical: individual-cork TCA screening and microagglomerate technical corks sold at specified, graded oxygen transmission rates, which for the first time lets a winemaker choose a closure by intended drinking window instead of by prestige. Against that, natural cork carries the only genuinely strong environmental story in the whole package, since the cork oak landscapes of Iberia are managed, biodiverse and carbon-sequestering, and the tree is not felled.

My view is that the closure ceases to be a status marker and becomes an oenological specification. Cork holds the fine-wine tier on cultural and environmental grounds; screwcap owns the commercial middle and, in aromatic whites, much of the top.

## The label

This is where the most consequential change of the decade is already legislated. EU rules oblige wines produced from 8 December 2023 to carry a nutrition declaration and ingredient list, satisfiable off-label by a QR-linked electronic label. That converts the label from a fixed printed surface into a gateway to a maintained digital record. The industry benefit is direct and financial: regulatory changes no longer force a reprint and a write-off of label stock, which matters enormously to a producer carrying many SKUs across many markets. The same channel then carries provenance, allergen, sustainability and anti-counterfeiting data at no extra print cost, which is why I expect adoption well beyond the markets that require it. The physical label meanwhile becomes a recyclability problem — metallised foils and aggressive adhesives contaminate the glass stream, and wash-off adhesives are a precondition of any refill scheme.

## The capsule and the case

The capsule is the most vulnerable component in the package and the one nobody defends. Its original function, protecting cork from weevils and rodents in cellar storage, has been obsolete for decades; it is a mixed material that complicates recycling; and under screwcap it is pure theatre. Producers across several markets have already dropped it, and I expect its disappearance below the fine-wine tier to be the least contested change discussed here.

The twelve-bottle carton persists because it matches pallet and manual-handling economics, but e-commerce growth has already shifted volume toward six-bottle and moulded-pulp single-bottle packs, and the wooden case survives only where it functions as provenance and resale packaging for wines that trade at auction.

## Who is driving change, and who is resisting

Change is being pushed by monopolies and multiple retailers writing specifications, by regulators through extended producer responsibility, deposit-return schemes and the EU's packaging reforms, by energy cost — glass furnaces are gas-intensive, and the 2022 European energy shock raised prices and lengthened lead times — and by younger off-trade consumers whose occasions are increasingly informal.

Resistance is equally structural. Producers have sunk capital in bottling lines. Appellation law entrenches glass and sometimes a specific bottle: Alsace AOC requires its wines to be bottled in the flûte, and the Bocksbeutel is reserved to Franken. The gift and on-trade occasions still trade on weight and ceremony. Above all, ageing and the secondary market require glass, because no alternative yet offers decade-scale barrier performance or provenance credibility.

## What the future actually is

Traditional packaging becomes a premium format rather than the industry standard. The bottle survives but loses roughly a third of its average weight and much of its below-£10 volume to bulk-plus-alternatives. Cork survives, technically re-specified. The paper label survives physically but surrenders its informational role to the e-label. The capsule largely disappears. The wooden case narrows to investment-grade wine.

The threat to the industry is not that the bottle dies; it is that in defending an aesthetic it did not design, wine is slower than beer, spirits and soft drinks in a regulatory environment that will not wait. The wines that will lose are those in the middle — priced too high for a box, too low to justify 500 g of glass.
