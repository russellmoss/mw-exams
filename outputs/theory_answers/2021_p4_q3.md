## The winery I am representing

I will answer as a real case, because the right strategy is unrecognisable in the abstract. My winery is a family-owned Paso Robles estate making 100,000 cases: Cabernet Sauvignon and Rhône-variety blends, a little Chardonnay, sold at roughly US$20 to $28 on the domestic shelf, with a strong tasting room and wine club. Ninety per cent goes into the United States, split between our own direct-to-consumer channel and two state distributors. Ten thousand cases currently go abroad, to a Canadian agent and a UK importer.

## The number decides the strategy

The first and most important factor is arithmetic. Moving from 10% to 25% of 100,000 cases means finding a home for 15,000 additional cases, since a standard 20-foot container carries just over a thousand cases.

That is a small number, and recognising it dictates everything that follows. It cannot justify chasing mass-market distribution across many markets: a single national listing with a British or German multiple would be several times this volume, at a price that would sit below our cost of production and would visibly undercut our US shelf price. Equally, it is far too much to place with a scattering of small agents in fifteen countries, each of which would cost me two visits a year for two hundred cases.

The realistic shape is therefore three markets, each taking 4,000 to 6,000 cases, sold through the premium independent and on-trade channels where our price point belongs. This is a depth strategy, not a breadth strategy, and I would rather deepen the two markets I already have than add five new ones.

## Price architecture: the constraint that decides everything

The second factor, and where most export plans fail, is whether the wine can reach the foreign shelf at a price that is coherent with its domestic price. Working backwards: our FOB is roughly $110 a case. Add freight and insurance, import duty, an importer margin of about 30%, a wholesale margin, retail markup and sales tax, and a $24 California wine lands on a UK shelf well above £20 — because UK excise duty on still wine is over £2 a bottle before VAT is added.

At that price we are competing with classed-growth Bordeaux and Barossa Shiraz of real reputation, not with the $24 domestic competition we beat comfortably. So my strategy has to accept either a lower FOB, which is margin-dilutive against a tasting-room sale where we keep the whole retail dollar, or a genuinely premium positioning. I would choose the second, and I would tell the board plainly that this project reduces our average margin per case. Canada is the obvious deepening opportunity, and the challenge is structural: the provincial monopolies control listings, and the LCBO is widely regarded as one of the largest single purchasers of wine in the world. I would build private-order and licensee business rather than pursue a general listing.
- **The United Kingdom** is the best market for our style but the hardest on price, and its post-Brexit import certification requirements are still unsettled. I would work the independent merchant and restaurant trade, not the multiples.
- **Sweden** I would decline. Systembolaget buys by blind tender against a published specification; it is a superb volume opportunity at a price we cannot meet, and winning it once tells you nothing about next year.
- **Japan** I would add as the third market: importer-led, on-trade weighted, appreciative of Californian wine, and willing to pay for provenance.

The concentration risk deserves a word. The punitive duties China imposed on Australian wine from late 2020 removed that country's largest export market almost overnight, and that is the argument for spreading 15,000 cases across three markets rather than betting it on one.

## Budget and resourcing

The incremental 15,000 cases generate a gross profit measured in the low hundreds of thousands of dollars, so the export budget must be a fraction of that. I would plan on an all-in annual spend of the order of $150,000: my own salary contribution, samples and sample freight, four market visits, two trade fairs, label and compliance work, and a modest depletion allowance for each importer. That is what is affordable. Plans built on brand-marketing budgets, national advertising or a dedicated in-market team are not.

## Measurement and timing

Finally, I would set the plan over three years rather than one, and measure depletions rather than shipments. Shipping 15,000 cases into a warehouse is easy and is how export businesses are destroyed; selling them through to consumers, at a price that holds, is the thing being asked for.
