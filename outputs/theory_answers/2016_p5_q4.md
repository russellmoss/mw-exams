## What a commodity is

A commodity is a good defined by three conditions. It is **fungible**: one producer's output is interchangeable with another's, so the buyer is indifferent to source. It is **specified to a standard**: it can be graded, so that a contract can be written and settled without inspecting the particular lot. And its price is **set by a market rather than by the seller**, which makes every producer a price-taker. Wheat, copper and crude oil satisfy all three.

The word "just" adds a fourth test, and it is the one the question really turns on. "Just another commodity" implies not only that wine can be traded like a commodity but that there is nothing left over — no cultural, aesthetic or provenance value that resists the trade. So I ask two things of each segment: does it meet the three conditions, and if it does, is that all it is?

The answer varies so completely by segment that any single verdict on "wine" would be false. I take the four segments where the global trade actually operates.

## Grape contracts

At the fruit stage wine is unambiguously a commodity, and the evidence is that the market itself says so. Grapes are contracted by the tonne against a specification — district, variety, sugar, rot tolerance, sometimes a maximum yield — and the price is published: the California Grape Crush Report publishes average prices per ton by district and variety every year, which is precisely the price-discovery apparatus of a commodity market. Growers hedge nothing, take the posted price, and are substitutable within a district. When there is a surplus, contracts are not renewed and vines are pulled; when there is a shortage, plantings follow. This is agricultural commodity behaviour without qualification.

## Bulk wine

Finished wine traded in bulk is a commodity that has moved one step down the chain. It is quoted per litre by country, colour, variety, vintage and analysis; brokers such as Ciatti make a market in it; it ships in flexitanks, and a flexitank moves more than twice the wine of a container of packaged bottles, which is what made the trade viable at scale. Bulk wine now accounts for something approaching two fifths of the volume of world wine exports.

The commodity conditions are all met, and the consequence is exactly what commodity theory predicts: a buyer sourcing an own-label Chilean Sauvignon Blanc can switch to a South African one at renewal on a cent per litre, and the producer has no defence. Origin here is a specification, not an identity.

## High-volume brands

This is where the answer inverts, and it is the most interesting segment. The liquid inside a large commercial brand is bought as a commodity — often on the bulk market described above — but it is sold as something that is emphatically not one. The whole purpose of a brand is to defeat fungibility: to make the consumer unwilling to accept the identical wine under another label, and therefore to move price-setting from the market back to the seller.

So the same wine can be a commodity upstream and a differentiated good downstream, and the value created in that transformation is the brand owner's margin. The important implication is that commoditisation is not a state of the product but a position in the chain. It is happening most aggressively at the point where the wine is anonymous, and it is being actively resisted at the point where it meets the consumer.

## Fine wine

By any production definition, fine wine is the opposite of a commodity: non-fungible by construction, since the whole proposition is that this vineyard in this vintage from this producer cannot be substituted, and made in quantities that cannot expand.

And yet fine wine has been made to behave more like a commodity than any other segment. Liv-ex trades on a standard-in-bond specification, with settlement rules, published indices and a bid-offer spread; a case of a given château and vintage in bond is genuinely interchangeable with any other such case, which satisfies the fungibility condition exactly. Wine funds hold it as an asset class, en primeur is a forward purchase, and price is discovered on an exchange rather than set by the producer once the wine leaves the estate. The market has taken the least fungible product in the industry and engineered fungibility into it — not by changing the wine but by standardising the contract.

That is a genuine paradox and it deserves to be stated plainly: fine wine is not a commodity in production and is close to one in trade.

## Where the analogy breaks

The opposing case is strong and must be stated at full strength. Vintage variation defeats standardisation: a commodity is the same every year by definition, and wine is not, which is why no true futures market with delivery obligations has ever established itself. Supply cannot respond to price on a commodity timescale — a vine takes three years to crop and appellation rules often forbid expansion at all, so the elasticity that defines commodity markets is absent. Most wine is consumed within a year of purchase for reasons of pleasure, occasion and identity, which no commodity satisfies. And crucially, the industry's own economics depend on refusing commodity status: appellations, brands, scores, cellar doors and the entire apparatus of provenance exist to prevent substitution. An industry that spends its whole marketing budget resisting a description is evidence against that description.

## Conclusion

To a great extent at the bottom, hardly at all in the middle, and in a curious re-engineered form at the very top.

By volume, most of the world's wine is a commodity, and increasingly so: fruit and bulk are traded on specification and price, and the growth of bulk shipping has extended commodity behaviour further up the chain than it once reached. By value, most of the world's wine is not a commodity, because the value is concentrated in exactly the segments where substitution is refused.

I concede what the opposing case gets right: wine's cultural weight, its vintage variation and its inelastic supply mean it will never be *just* another commodity in the way copper is, and anyone who models it that way will misprice it. But I would not take much comfort from that. The commodity zone has been expanding, and it expands by taking wines that were sold on identity and re-describing them as a specification. The industry's central strategic problem is not whether wine is a commodity — it is which of its wines it is prepared to let become one, and whether the rest can be defended. Those defences are legal, cultural and reputational, and none of them is permanent.
