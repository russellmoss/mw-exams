## Introduction

Tannin profile means the quantity, the size distribution and the origin of the phenolic polymers in a wine, and therefore how it is perceived: grippy or supple, drying or fine. It is managed by controlling three things — how much is extracted, from where, and how it is subsequently polymerised or removed. Grapes must arrive at phenolic ripeness for any of this to work, but the question is about vinification, so I take that as read and run through the process in order: pre-fermentation, fermentation and cap management, pressing, post-fermentation operations, additions and oak, and then the other wine styles and the commercial end of the market.

## Before fermentation

Destemming decides whether stem tannin — greener and coarser, but firming — is in the vat at all; whole-bunch inclusion is a deliberate tannin decision as much as an aromatic one. Crushing sets skin surface area. A cold soak at 8 to 12 degrees Celsius extracts anthocyanins and aroma in water, but very little tannin, because condensed tannin needs ethanol as a solvent; that asymmetry is exactly why it is used. Pectolytic and maceration enzymes break down cell walls and raise the rate of extraction thereafter.

## During fermentation

Ethanol is the main variable: as it climbs, tannin extraction accelerates and shifts towards seed tannin, which is harsher. Temperature is the second — fermentation at 28 to 30 degrees Celsius extracts far more than a cool ferment. Time on skins is the third.

Cap management determines how much of the potential is realised. Punch-down submerges the cap with high wine-to-skin contact and, done gently by hand or firmly by pneumatic ram, is the most controllable tool. Pump-over irrigates the cap from above; it extracts less per operation but adds oxygen, which helps yeast and starts tannin polymerisation early. Délestage (rack and return) empties the tank, aerates strongly, and lets the seeds settle out so they can be removed — a targeted way of cutting harsh tannin. A submerged cap gives slow passive extraction. Rotary fermenters achieve in two to three days what a static vat takes ten to do; they are the right tool where throughput and consistency matter and they extract efficiently by design. The timing decision matters as much as the technique: working the cap hard early, while alcohol is low, favours skin tannin and colour; working it late favours seed tannin.

## Pressing

Pressing is where much of the profile is actually decided, and it is under-used as a lever. Free-run is softer and lower in tannin, phenol and potassium; press fractions rise steeply in tannin, and in pH, with pressure. Keeping fractions separate — light press, hard press — gives the winemaker discrete blending components to taste and assemble, and hard press fractions can be fined with gelatin, egg albumen or PVPP to strip aggressive tannin before any is blended back. Free run itself can be fined for the same purpose. Pressing off before dryness leaves the last, harshest extraction in the marc.

## After fermentation: polymerisation

Extended maceration of two to four weeks continues extraction but, more importantly, allows tannins to polymerise and to bind with anthocyanins, so the wine tastes softer despite containing more phenolics. Micro-oxygenation does the same job deliberately, dosing of the order of a few millilitres of oxygen per litre per month, higher during the post-fermentation phase and far lower after malolactic fermentation. Barrel ageing supplies the same slow oxygen ingress, plus oak ellagitannins, plus lees that adsorb tannin. Fining pre-bottling is the final subtractive step, and it is selective: gelatin binds the largest, most astringent polymers, egg albumen polishes gently, PVPP removes the small bitter phenolics, and casein or plant proteins serve vegan specifications. Blending is the last lever of all — assembling free run, press fractions and differently handled lots to a target profile.

## Additions

Oenological tannins are the most neglected tool. Added at crush, condensed and gallotannin preparations bind and stabilise anthocyanins and inhibit laccase; added post-fermentation they restore structure and mid-palate to thin or early-pressed wines. They are legal, widespread and cheap, and no serious answer on tannin should ignore them.

## Other styles

In white wine the object is usually to avoid tannin: whole-bunch pressing, low press pressures, cutting hard fractions, and PVPP or casein fining. Skin-contact and orange whites invert that, extracting deliberately. In sparkling the press regime does the work — Champagne takes only 2,050 litres of cuvée from a 4,000 kg marc precisely to leave phenolics behind. In sweet wine botrytised fruit brings laccase, and gentle pressing plus protective handling limits phenolic pick-up. In Port, extraction must be complete before fortification arrests it, hence lagares, robotic treaders and autovinifiers working the cap intensively over two or three days.

## The commercial end

Judicious tannin management arguably matters more at entry level, where the wine has no time in bottle to resolve. Early pressing before dryness, thermovinification and flash détente to get colour with minimal tannin, rotary fermenters, oak staves for a little ellagitannin, oenological tannin for structure and gelatin fining for smoothness are all standard, and all deliver a soft, approachable profile to specification.
