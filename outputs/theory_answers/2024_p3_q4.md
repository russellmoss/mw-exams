## Sampling first

Volatile acidity is the fraction of a wine's acidity that is steam-distillable, overwhelmingly acetic acid, reported as g/L acetic acid or in meq/L. Before any analysis I would fix what is being tested, because the diagnosis depends on it: pull at least six bottles from the batch — different pallets, plus the start, middle and end of the bottling run — together with the retention samples kept from bottling day, and record lot code, bottling date, line and closure batch. Bottles are held upright at cellar temperature until opened.

## Confirming the fault

Sensory assessment comes first and is a screen, not a confirmation. Acetic acid is generally perceptible in red wine from around 0.6-0.9 g/L, giving vinegar and a sharp lift; its ester, ethyl acetate, becomes obtrusive from roughly 150-200 mg/L with nail-varnish and solvent character, and can dominate at modest acetic acid levels. I would run the suspect wine blind against a sound reference bottle of the same wine as a triangle test with three or four tasters, so the finding is not one palate's opinion.

Confirmation is analytical. The OIV reference method is steam distillation in a Cash still and titration of the distillate with standardised sodium hydroxide to phenolphthalein. It is not specific: free and bound SO2, sorbic acid, salicylic acid and carbonic acid distil over too, so the result must be corrected for them or the wine will be condemned for sulphur it legitimately contains. Where the correction is awkward — a wine with high SO2 or added sorbate — an enzymatic acetic acid assay (acetyl-CoA synthetase/NADH) is specific and preferable, and HPLC or GC will quantify acetic acid and ethyl acetate together. FTIR is fast and fine for screening a batch but is a calibrated predictive method and any exceedance must be confirmed by reference or enzymatic assay.

The number is then read against three benchmarks: the producer's own release specification; the legal ceilings — the EU ceilings of 1.2 g/L for red and 1.08 g/L for white and rose, while the US TTB limit for red table wine is 1.4 g/L; and, critically, the recorded pre-bottling analysis of the same lot. Alongside VA I would measure free and total SO2 and calculate molecular SO2 from pH, residual glucose and fructose, malic and lactic acid, acetaldehyde, ethyl acetate, and dissolved oxygen, and plate the wine on selective media for acetic acid bacteria, lactic acid bacteria and Brettanomyces, or run qPCR for speed.

## The diagnostic logic

Two comparisons do most of the work. First, does the retention sample from bottling day show the same VA? If it does, the problem predates bottling and the line is exonerated. If it does not, VA has risen in bottle, which requires both an organism and oxygen. Second, is the elevation batch-wide or sporadic? A uniform result across pallets points at the wine — a blend component, a barrel lot, the ferment. Scattered high bottles point at the filler, a section of the run, or individual closures.

## Vineyard to bottle

**Fruit condition.** Sour rot and botrytis, split berries after harvest rain, and drosophila vectoring acetic acid bacteria all deliver must that is already at 0.3-0.4 g/L before fermentation begins; warm machine-harvested fruit sitting in bins on a long haul does the same. The evidence is in intake records, grading notes and the must analysis. A wet, late harvest in a marginal red region is the classic case.

**Pre-fermentation and fermentation.** A long cold soak with inadequate SO2 lets apiculate non-Saccharomyces yeasts such as Hanseniaspora produce acetic acid and ethyl acetate in the lag phase. Saccharomyces itself makes more acetic acid when stressed — nitrogen-deficient must, very high sugar, high fermentation temperature. A sluggish or stuck fermentation is the worst outcome, leaving sugar as substrate with no alcohol protection. And the cap is the textbook site: a warm, dry, under-worked cap in an open vessel is an aerobic bed for acetic acid bacteria.

**Malolactic conversion.** A slow malolactic at high pH with residual sugar present lets lactic acid bacteria ferment that sugar heterofermentatively to acetic acid — piqure lactique. High pH is the compounding factor throughout: above about pH 3.8 the molecular SO2 needed for protection is difficult to reach within legal total limits, which is why warm-climate reds are disproportionately represented in VA problems.

**Elevage.** Barrels are the commonest culprit in the months after fermentation: poor topping leaving ullage, free SO2 allowed to lapse between checks, wood already colonised by acetic acid bacteria or Brettanomyces, and oxygen picked up at every racking. Monthly barrel-by-barrel VA records will show which lots drifted and when.

**Blending and pre-bottling.** A small volume of high-VA press wine or one bad barrel folded into the blend will lift the whole lot. Check the component analyses against the final blend arithmetic.

**Bottling and after.** Only now does the line come into it: a filter integrity failure, a biofilm in the filler bowl or hoses, high dissolved oxygen and headspace oxygen at fill, or a faulty closure. Membrane filtration at 0.45 micron with bubble-point testing, line swabs and ATP records answer these. Post-bottling, a container shipped warm across the tropics with an oxygen-permeable closure gives acetic acid bacteria exactly what they need.

## Conclusion

Confirmation is a controlled sampling plan, a sensory screen and a specific analytical method read with its interferences understood. Identification is a comparison against the wine's own analytical history, working back from bottle to vine rather than stopping at the filler. One caution belongs at the end: raised VA is not automatically a fault. Some traditional reds — Chateau Musar, whose reds have long carried volatile acidity above modern commercial norms, is the standing example — integrate it stylistically, so the technician's report should state the number, the legal position and the trajectory, and leave the quality judgement to a tasting decision.
