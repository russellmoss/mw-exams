## Where the category actually stands

The numbers frame the problem. Sherry shipments have fallen substantially from their 1970s peak. Port has held volume better but shows the same mix shift: France has long been among the largest volume markets for Port, largely for inexpensive ruby as an aperitif, while aged tawny, colheita and LBV have grown as standard ruby has declined. Madeira is small and stable, with a large share of its volume going to the kitchen. Vins doux naturels, Marsala and Australian fortifieds are all in structural decline.

Against that, vermouth and aromatised wines have grown strongly on the back of the cocktail revival. That is the most instructive datapoint in the category: a fortified product returned to growth not by changing the liquid but by changing the occasion.

## Four trends and what the category can do with them

**1. Moderation and the cocktail.** Fortified wines sit between roughly 15 and 22% abv, which reads badly against a moderation trend — but the serve, not the strength, is what the consumer experiences. A 50ml pour of aged tawny or a fino in a highball with tonic delivers less alcohol than a large glass of wine. The category's opportunity is to sell a smaller, colder, mixed serve: Port and tonic is already an established Portuguese serve with genuine international traction, and the rebujito does the same job for manzanilla. This recruits the drinker who will never open a 75cl bottle of anything at 20% for herself.

**2. Experience and tourism.** Fortified regions have an unusual advantage: the production sites are visitable, photogenic and concentrated. The Gaia lodges and the Douro quintas, the Jerez bodegas and the tabanco revival, and the Madeira lodges are all places where a visitor forms a lasting attachment that no advertising could buy. Cellar door also converts at full margin and captures the customer's contact details, which turns a tourist into a direct-purchase relationship. This should be treated as a primary channel rather than as public relations.

**3. Heritage and authenticity.** Younger drinkers reward provenance, small production and process transparency, and fortified wine has more of all three than any other category. En rama bottlings, single-vineyard and single-cask releases, VOS and VORS age designations, almacenista and colheita bottlings, and vintage-dated releases all give the trade something specific to talk about. Crucially they also carry the price the category needs.

**4. Gastronomy and the by-the-glass problem.** Sherry's route back into the on-trade has been food — the tapas and fine-dining pairing argument is well made and works. The obstacle is commercial rather than cultural: fino oxidises fast once open, so a restaurant carrying it by the glass risks waste. Half bottles, argon systems and short-dated stock rotation are the mundane fixes, and solving them matters more to sales than another masterclass.

## What is actually constraining them

It is tempting, and wrong, to assume these are old-fashioned businesses that failed to notice the modern consumer. The evidence contradicts it: the category itself invented en rama, the age-designation system, the Port and tonic serve, single-quinta bottlings and the modern lodge visitor experience. The binding constraints are structural.

Stock economics come first. A solera or a tawny stock is decades of working capital and it is also the product; a house cannot pivot quickly without drawing down the average age that defines its wines, and it cannot scale quickly if demand returns. Second, the legacy markets that carry the volume — UK cream sherry, French ruby Port — are declining faster than the premium tiers are growing, so the mix improves while the business shrinks. Third, ownership is fragmented and marketing budgets are small relative to spirits competitors occupying the same aperitif occasion. Fourth, duty regimes that tax by alcoholic strength penalise fortified wine directly and worsen its price competitiveness against table wine.

## My position

The category should stop trying to win back volume and commit to selling less at a much higher price, while using the cocktail and aperitif serve as the recruitment mechanism rather than as the destination. Concretely: retire the low-price cream and standard ruby tiers rather than defending them, invest in the on-trade by-the-glass serve and the cellar-door experience, and lead communication with age, place and process.

The counter-argument deserves stating. Premiumisation shrinks a category faster than it enriches it, and abandoning the volume tiers removes the entry point through which most drinkers first meet fortified wine at all. That risk is real. But the volume tiers are being lost regardless, and the vermouth precedent shows recruitment can be rebuilt through the serve rather than through cheap bottles. Better to lose that volume deliberately and keep the margin than to defend it and lose both.
