## Defining the tiers

By "entry level" I mean Chardonnay retailing under about £8 a bottle at UK retail, where duty, VAT, freight and margin leave roughly £1 or less per bottle for the wine itself — a figure that fixes every technical decision that follows. By "high end" I mean wine retailing above about £40, where the winemaking cost per bottle is immaterial against the price and the constraint is quality of raw material, not budget. The two are not points on one scale but different production models: the first optimises consistency and cost per litre at volume, the second optimises expression of a specific parcel and accepts variability.

I compare Burgundy and South Eastern Australia because both produce at each tier, which lets the comparison run across price and across region at once. I follow the process in sequence, since that is where the tangible differences sit.

## Intake and pressing

Entry level: machine-harvested at scale, destemmed and crushed, pressed hard in a large pneumatic press to maximise recovery — 700 litres or more per tonne — with press fractions blended back. Juice arrives warm and oxidised; some producers hyperoxidise deliberately, letting polyphenol oxidase strip oxidisable phenolics that are then settled out, which buys stability later.

High end: hand-picked in crates, whole-bunch pressed without destemming, long slow cycle at low pressure, and only 500 to 650 litres of free-run per tonne taken forward, with the hard press cut away entirely. Protective handling under an inert atmosphere in Burgundy's more reductive houses; deliberate juice oxidation in others. The tangible difference is not the equipment but the yield taken and the fractions rejected.

## Juice preparation and fermentation

Entry level: cold settle or, more commonly at volume, flotation with gelatine or pea protein for speed; bentonite added to juice to remove heat-unstable protein before ferment; juice clarified hard to low solids. Fermentation in stainless steel of 50,000 litres or more, cooled to 14-16°C, with a selected aromatic yeast strain and DAP to guarantee a clean, fast, reproducible finish. Aroma is engineered in — thiol- and ester-driven, fresh, fruit-forward.

High end: settled overnight only, kept deliberately turbid because the solids carry lipids and nitrogen that a barrel ferment needs; indigenous or partly indigenous yeast, accepting a slower and warmer ferment at 18-22°C in a 228-litre piece or a small foudre. Warmth and barrel volume create the reductive, struck-match character and the fatty acid profile that defines the style. Higher risk, and the answer to why: complexity comes from a slower, less controlled ferment.

## Malolactic and lees

Entry level: malolactic is usually blocked in Australia to keep malic acidity and crisp fruit, and acidification with tartaric is routine; where it is used, it is inoculated and fast. Lees handling is minimal — the wine is racked clean quickly to free the tank. Texture is bought in, using inactivated yeast preparations or mannoprotein additions at bottling rather than time.

High end: full spontaneous malolactic through the following spring, lees kept for 11 to 18 months with periodic bâtonnage or, increasingly, none at all — the choice between the two being a deliberate style decision about mannoprotein release versus reductive protection. The mechanism is the same in both tiers; the difference is that one buys it with time and one buys it in a bag.

## Oak and maturation

Entry level: oak flavour from staves, chips or powder in tank, dosed to a target and often combined with micro-oxygenation to soften phenolics over weeks. It is a flavour addition, not a maturation vessel, and it delivers vanillin and coconut without the slow oxygen ingress that changes structure.

High end: barrel maturation with 20-50% new French oak, tight-grained, over 12-18 months, where the wine is simultaneously receiving oxygen at a low, steady rate through the stave and evaporating in a cold cellar. A new barrel costs of the order of €800 and is used over three or four vintages, adding well over €1 a bottle — which is precisely why the technique does not migrate down.

## Stabilisation to bottle

Entry level: cold stabilisation or, more cheaply, CMC or potassium polyaspartate for tartrate stability; bentonite to protein stability; sterile membrane filtration and screwcap, because a wine shipped worldwide and drunk within two years must be absolutely stable in a warm warehouse.

High end: light or no fining, coarse filtration or none, bottling at the estate, more tolerance of tartrate crystals because the market accepts them. The wine is protected by lower pH, higher extract and a slower supply chain instead of by intervention.

## Where the regions diverge

The tier gap is wider in Australia than in Burgundy. Australia's entry level is built for scale — multi-region blending across a large geographical indication, large-format oak alternatives, engineered aroma — while its high end, in Margaret River or the Yarra Valley, has largely converged on the Burgundian toolkit of whole-bunch pressing, wild ferment and barrel maturation. Burgundy's entry level, by contrast, is made in the same cellars and often in the same way as its top wines, differing mainly in fruit source, yield and the proportion of new oak. In Burgundy the tier is set principally by the vineyard; in Australia it is set by the winemaking. That is the sharpest contrast the question offers, and it is a difference of production model, not of skill.
