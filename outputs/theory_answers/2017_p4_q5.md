## Scope

At over one million nine-litre cases — nine million litres, or roughly twelve million bottles — I am a volume producer, not a boutique. My objective in China is therefore distribution and repeat purchase at scale, not allocation-driven scarcity, and my plan must be judged on sell-through and cash collected, not on cases shipped. I set out the market, then the plan, then the risks and their management.

## The market I am entering

China's imported wine market was reset by the anti-extravagance campaign from 2012, which removed the gifting and official-banquet trade almost overnight and left behind a smaller but far healthier base of genuine consumption. Growth since has come from private consumption in tier-one cities and, increasingly, tier-two and tier-three cities, from younger and more female consumers, and overwhelmingly from red wine, which carries auspicious associations.

The tariff position shapes competitive advantage more than quality does. For a wine without a trade agreement, duty, consumption tax and value-added tax compound to add of the order of 48% to the CIF value. The China-Australia Free Trade Agreement, in force since December 2015, phases the tariff on Australian wine to zero by January 2019, and Chile and New Zealand already enjoy zero tariffs — a structural advantage of several points of retail price against European competitors. France has led imported bottled wine by both volume and value, giving Bordeaux enormous brand recognition and, unhelpfully for me, defining what "imported wine" means to many consumers.

Channels are unusually fragmented and unusually digital at the same time: provincial distributors, a growing modern-trade grocery sector, an on-trade concentrated in hotels, and e-commerce that is far more important than in any Western market. Tmall, JD.com and Yesmywine matter, Singles Day on 11 November is the retail event of the year, and WeChat functions as social network, payment system and storefront simultaneously. Domestic production is a real competitor, not a curiosity: Ningxia and Shandong are the significant regions and Changyu, COFCO's Great Wall and Dynasty the significant companies, with substantial scale advantages in distribution.

## How I would capitalise

**Route to market: a hybrid, not a single national importer.** I would appoint one national importer to hold the licence, manage customs and set a single national price list, and beneath it a limited number of regional distributors on defined territories with agreed sell-through targets. This keeps pricing control central while buying provincial reach.

**Enter through cross-border e-commerce first.** A flagship store on Tmall Global or JD Worldwide tests demand, price point and label appeal with small stock commitment and cash up front, and generates the consumer data that a distributor would otherwise own. Only then would I commit to general-trade volume.

**Segment the portfolio.** A clearly branded volume red at the mass price point through modern trade and e-commerce, carrying the marketing weight; a mid-tier for the on-trade and for gifting occasions; and a small premium tier purely to give the brand a ceiling. Attempting a single price point in a market this stratified wastes the range.

**Invest in Chinese-language brand equity.** Choose and register my own Chinese-character brand name, with a meaning and a sound I control; put Mandarin-speaking commercial staff on the ground; fund trade and sommelier education, which builds the advocacy layer the market lacks.

## Risks, and how I would manage them

**Credit risk** is the first-order commercial risk, since payment cultures are slower and enforcement is hard. Managed by letters of credit at sight or cash in advance for new accounts, trade credit insurance, and strict credit limits reviewed against sell-through rather than sell-in.

**Counterfeiting**, historically involving refilled bottles of recognised brands, is managed by registering the trademark early — China is a first-to-file jurisdiction, so a name I have not registered can legitimately be taken by someone else, including my Chinese name — and by tamper-evident closures, serialised labels and NFC or QR authentication that also captures consumer data. Marketplace monitoring and take-down enforcement should be budgeted, not improvised.

**Price control and grey market.** Parallel imports undercut official pricing, and Hong Kong abolished its wine duty in 2008, making it a persistent source of cheaper stock. Managed by a single published price list, contractual territory restrictions, market-specific SKUs or back labels that make diverted stock identifiable, and by allocating rather than dumping volume when a distributor pushes for a discount.

**Distribution risk.** Over-dependence on one partner, stock ageing in unconditioned warehouses in a hot climate, and a distributor buying for the discount rather than the consumer. Managed by multiple regional partners, contractual storage standards with audit rights, and paying incentives on depletion rather than on purchase.

**Language and cultural risk.** Managed by local staff, professionally translated labelling that meets the mandatory Chinese back-label requirement, and consumer research rather than assumption.

## Conclusion

The prize is real but it is a distribution prize, not a prestige one. I would enter through cross-border e-commerce, build a controlled hybrid distribution structure behind it, own my Chinese brand name from day one, and hold pricing discipline absolutely — accepting slower volume growth in exchange for a business that collects its cash and keeps its price position.
