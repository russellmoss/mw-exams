## Introduction

Colour management means hitting a target hue and intensity for the style, and then holding it through bottling and shelf life. It runs across every category — pale whites, rosés at a commercially specified shade, deeply coloured reds, amber skin-contact whites, blanc de noirs sparkling, tawny fortified — and the target may equally be maximum colour, minimum colour, or a particular oxidised colour. I take the options first, by style and in process order, then the harder half: the challenges of fixing the colour and of defending it against rot, oxygen, fining and time.

## Where colour comes from

Anthocyanins sit in the hypodermal cells of the grape skin; the pulp is colourless in almost all varieties, the teinturiers such as Alicante Bouschet and Saperavi being the exception that proves the rule. Extraction is therefore a skin-contact decision. Anthocyanins are water-soluble and extract early — within the first few days — whereas tannin needs ethanol and extracts late, which is the asymmetry every red winemaker exploits.

## The options, by style

*White wines.* The task is to prevent colour. Whole-bunch pressing at low pressure, cutting hard press fractions, and SO2 at crush of the order of 50 mg/L to inhibit polyphenol oxidase all limit phenolic pick-up. The counter-intuitive option is hyperoxidation: deliberately oxidising the must so that browning precursors polymerise and are removed as solids, leaving a wine that is paler and more stable thereafter. PVPP and casein fining remove existing browning.

*Rosé.* Three routes, and the choice sets both colour and structure. Direct pressing of red grapes gives the palest wines, in the Provençal manner. Short maceration, typically two to twelve hours, gives more colour and more phenolic weight. Saignée bleeds juice off a red ferment, giving a deeper wine and, as a by-product, a more concentrated red — but the rosé is a residual, not a designed wine. Blending red and white is prohibited for still rosé in the EU, and permitted for Champagne.

*Red wines.* Cold soak at 8 to 12 degrees Celsius extracts anthocyanin with little tannin; warmer ferments, longer cap contact and more frequent pump-overs extract more of both. Pectolytic and maceration enzymes raise the extraction rate. Thermovinification and flash détente, heating the must to around 70 to 80 degrees Celsius, deliver colour with very little tannin, which is exactly the specification for a soft commercial red.

*Skin-contact whites, sparkling and fortified.* Extended maceration of white grapes makes deliberately amber wine. In sparkling, a blanc de noirs depends entirely on press fractions taken gently enough to leave the skins unbroken. In fortified wine, Port fixes colour by extracting hard before spirit arrests everything, while tawny and Madeira styles pursue the opposite: colour deliberately oxidised to amber over years in cask.

## Fixing the colour: pH, tannin and stability

Extracting colour is the easy part. Free anthocyanin is unstable and its colour is pH-dependent: at typical wine pH of the order of a quarter of free anthocyanin sits in the coloured flavylium form, the rest being colourless or blue-shifted. Acidification with tartaric acid therefore brightens and intensifies colour directly, and it also improves microbial stability — one adjustment doing two jobs.

Lasting colour is polymeric. Anthocyanins condense with tannins, often via acetaldehyde bridges, into pigments that are stable and no longer bleached by SO2. This is why tannin management and colour management are the same problem: adding oenological tannin at crush supplies condensation partners, controlled oxygen through pump-over, micro-oxygenation or barrel ageing supplies the acetaldehyde, and co-pigmentation with colourless phenolics stabilises colour in the short term. Oxygen is therefore not the enemy here but a tool, dosed deliberately.

## The challenges

*Rot.* Botrytis-infected fruit brings laccase, which oxidises anthocyanins rapidly and, unlike the grape's own polyphenol oxidase, is not controlled by SO2. It causes oxidasic casse and irreversible colour loss. The answers are sorting, rapid processing, heat treatment to denature the enzyme, and bentonite.

*Oxidation post fermentation.* Uncontrolled ullage, sloppy racking and high dissolved oxygen brown a white and turn a red from purple towards brick. Deliberate oxidation is a style; accidental oxidation is a defect, and the difference is whether it was measured.

*Subtractive losses.* Fining is the commonest cause of unintended colour loss: gelatin and egg albumen strip pigmented tannin, PVPP strips browning but also colour, and over-fining cannot be reversed. Tight pad and membrane filtration removes colloidal colour. Colouring-matter deposit in bottle, metal casse from iron and copper, and light strike in clear rosé bottles complete the list.

*SO2 bleaching.* Free anthocyanin binds SO2 reversibly and loses its colour, so a young red looks markedly paler immediately after a sulphur addition and a rosé can appear to have lost its shade. The winemaker must know this is temporary and partial, and must not respond by re-extracting or by cutting the SO2 the wine needs for protection — a real trap when colour is being judged against a specification.

*Measurement and consistency.* All of this is only manageable because colour is quantifiable — absorbance at 420, 520 and 620 nm, or CIELab coordinates — against a specification that a retail buyer will hold the producer to, vintage after vintage.
