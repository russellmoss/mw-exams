## Defining the return, and the test

**Return on investment** is net return divided by capital employed, expressed annually. I will use three related measures, because a single one misleads: cash ROI on capital employed, internal rate of return (which prices the timing of the cash), and payback period. My hurdle is a weighted cost of capital in the high single digits to low double digits; anything below that is philanthropy, and there is a great deal of philanthropy in wine.

US$10 million sounds large and is not. It buys neither a significant estate in a classified region nor a mid-sized branded winery. That constraint drives the whole answer: the highest ROI will come from the parts of the chain where capital is not sunk into land. I assess each link in turn against capital intensity, margin and payback, then allocate across short (0-3 years), medium (3-7) and long (7-15) horizons.

## Walking the supply chain

**Vineyard land.** The most capital-hungry and the lowest-yielding. Prime Napa Valley vineyard land has been reported changing hands at prices in the hundreds of thousands of dollars per acre, so the whole inheritance buys only a modest number of acres and no winery. Vines take several years to a first commercial crop and longer still to reach full yield, so the cash return is negative for years and thereafter modest; the real return is capital appreciation, which is a long-horizon bet on appellation reputation. Cheaper land in Alentejo, La Mancha or inland Australia is available at a small fraction of that, but earns commodity grape prices, which are cyclical and periodically loss-making.

A purpose-built winery costs several dollars per litre of annual capacity, and new French oak barriques run into the hundreds of euros each. It is an asset that depreciates, is used six weeks a year and carries fixed overhead all twelve months. Buying processing capacity is the classic way to destroy ROI. Buying it as **contract processing capacity**, sold to others, is a different and better business.

**Brand ownership without assets.** The negociant or virtual-winery model: buy fruit, wine or bulk under contract, own the brand, the packaging and the route to market. Capital goes into working capital and marketing rather than land, healthy gross margins are achievable at brand-owner level, and the cycle runs to well over a year between paying for fruit and collecting cash. Because ROI is a ratio, keeping the denominator small is worth more than any improvement in the numerator.

Importers and distributors are fragmented and consolidating, and change hands at high single-digit to low double-digit multiples of EBITDA. Margins are thin, but the asset is the customer relationships and the licences, and in the USA a distribution licence is a legally protected position. Owning route to market also de-risks the brand investment above.

**Retail and direct-to-consumer.** Bricks-and-mortar retail is low-margin and rent-heavy. DTC is the exception: a direct sale captures the full retail price against an ex-cellar price that is a fraction of it, which is the single largest margin gain available anywhere in the chain.

Fine wine as a financial asset is liquid, index-tracked and requires no operating skill, but it yields nothing and carries storage and insurance costs of a low single-digit percentage of value a year. Short horizon: it generates trading cash within two to three years and is the engine of the portfolio.

**US$3 million** into a controlling stake in an importer-distributor in a growth market, both to secure route to market for the brand and because the equity value is realised on exit at a multiple of EBITDA. Medium horizon.

**US$1 million** into fine wine stock and cash reserve — liquidity, ballast, and the working capital cushion that stops a bad vintage forcing a distressed sale. Short to medium.

I deliberately buy no vineyard. If the long horizon must be covered, a long lease on planted vineyard with a purchase option gives the supply security without the capital.

## Risks and conclusion

The main risks are dependence on contract fruit supply in a short vintage, currency exposure between production and sales markets, and the illiquidity of a private wine business at exit. They are managed by multi-year supply contracts, natural currency hedging through the distributor, and building the brand to be attractive to a trade buyer.

My logic is simply that ROI is a ratio, and in wine the denominator is what kills it. The greatest return comes from owning brands and route to market, not from owning dirt — accepting that owning dirt is the more romantic answer, and the one the question invites.
