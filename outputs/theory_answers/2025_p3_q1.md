## The specification I am buying

This is a technical decision and I treat it as one: consumer perception of closures and the marketing case are outside what I assess here. The specification is a closure that will hold an effective seal for the full five years the wine may spend in bottle, deliver an oxygen dose that is not merely low but consistent bottle to bottle, introduce no taint and take nothing out of the wine, run on my line, and let me predict my sulphur dioxide regime. My position is that for a dry white whose selling point is fruit and acidity, I want near-inert preservation with a small, specified oxygen ingress rather than a closure chosen to permit development. Five years is a shelf life to be protected, not a maturation to be engineered.

## Oxygen transfer, and the units that make it mean something

An OTR figure is meaningless unless it states a mass of oxygen entering a defined volume over a defined time. I work in milligrams of oxygen per litre of wine per year, converting the per-closure-per-day or per-year figures suppliers quote by dividing by the 0.75 L pack. Ranked by steady-state ingress, a screwcap with a tin/Saran liner is tightest, of the order of a few tenths of a milligram per litre per year; a supercritical-CO2-treated microagglomerate cork sits above it but within a narrow specified band; natural cork is higher on average and, more damagingly, far more variable bottle to bottle. That variance is the real case against staying with cork: a five-year guarantee is a distribution problem, not an average problem, and it is why bottles from one batch show at three years as three different wines. Saranex liners and extruded synthetics are higher again and I would not consider them for this brief.

Both tails cost me. Too much ingress and I lose varietal thiols and esters, gain acetaldehyde and browning, and burn free SO2 — each milligram per litre of oxygen consumes roughly four milligrams per litre of free SO2. Too little and any reductive tendency in the wine hardens into hydrogen sulphide and mercaptans that will never blow off.

## The larger picture: total package oxygen

For the first year or two, closure OTR is the smaller number. What dominates is total package oxygen at filling: dissolved oxygen in the wine plus oxygen in the headspace. Air is about 21% oxygen by volume, so each millilitre of air in the neck carries roughly 0.28 mg of oxygen; five millilitres of headspace is about 1.4 mg, or near 1.9 mg/L in a 750 mL bottle — enough to consume something of the order of 7-8 mg/L of free SO2 within months. A screwcap holds a larger and more repeatable headspace than a cork, which compresses on insertion and rebounds. So changing closure obliges me to re-engineer the fill: nitrogen sparging of line and tank, inert jetting or vacuum-and-gas at the capper, dissolved oxygen measured at the filler rather than assumed, and TPO measured on bottles pulled from the run. A tight closure fitted over a careless fill buys nothing.

## Seal integrity, taint and scalping

Screwcap seal integrity depends on the glass finish — the BVS 30x60 finish, thread quality and bore consistency — and on capping torque and pluck, which must be tested every run; a skirt dented in transit is a leaker, and leakers under screwcap fail suddenly rather than gradually. Cork seal depends on diameter, jaw compression and bore. On taint, haloanisoles are natural cork's principal liability and the strongest technical argument for change: technical microagglomerate corks are sold on measured, guaranteed release levels, which converts a random risk into a specified one. Extruded synthetics carry the opposite fault, scalping esters and thiols into the polymer over years — for an aromatic white held five years that is disqualifying.

## Fitting, life span and what it does to my winemaking

Fitting and capital differ sharply. A technical cork drops into my existing corker; a screwcap requires a new bottle finish, hence a whole new glass SKU and inventory, a capping monobloc, and torque QC. On effective life span, microagglomerates are sold with a stated effective life — five, ten or thirty years — which maps directly onto this brief, whereas natural cork's performance decays unpredictably and screwcap liners are durable but assume upright storage.

The consequence for handling the wine is that a tighter closure lets me bottle at a lower free SO2, because less will be spent, but only if I fix reduction upstream rather than relying on ingress to cure it: adequate yeast-available nitrogen, lees handled before they turn stressed, disciplined copper use since residual copper is itself a later reductive driver.

## Conclusion

I would change, to a specified-OTR microagglomerate cork or a tin-lined screwcap, and choose between them on the bottling line I am prepared to buy. But I would not commit on supplier data: I would bottle a split trial from one tank across two or three OTRs, hold it through the real supply chain, and taste blind at six, twelve and twenty-four months against measured free SO2 and dissolved oxygen before converting the whole production.
