## The business I am selling

I am setting my parameters first, because the strategy is entirely determined by them. Assume an estate producing 8,000 cases — 96,000 bottles — from around 20 hectares of estate fruit, at an average ex-cellar price of 15 euros a bottle, giving turnover of roughly 1.4 million euros. Premium here is a commercial statement, not a quality one: the wine sells on provenance and scarcity, it is never price-promoted, and I have no advertising budget.

Two consequences follow and shape everything below. First, my job is allocation management rather than demand generation — at this volume I do not need many buyers, I need the right ones. Second, my scarcest resource is my own time, so every routing decision is really a decision about where I spend selling days.

## Scope: depth before breadth

My objective is to place 60 to 70 percent of production in the on-trade at full margin, with the domestic market and cellar door carrying the cash-flow base. I will do that in four or five export markets, of which three carry most of the volume.

The reasoning is arithmetical. A producer of this size spread across twenty markets gives every importer too little to care about, and an importer who cannot make a living from a brand will not send a salesperson to sell it. The minimum viable volume per market is whatever makes my winery matter to one salesperson in one distributor's portfolio — I set that floor at around 400 cases. The ceiling is a risk rule: no single market takes more than a quarter of turnover, because losing an importer should not be an existential event.

Market selection criteria, in order: a specialist on-trade with a sommelier culture that pays for provenance; cost and length of the route to market; duty, tariff and logistics; and whether I already have a champion there.

## Route to market, market by market

**United States.** Three-tier is a legal constraint, not a choice: I must appoint an importer and then distributors state by state, or a national importer-distributor. Two margins stack — an importer takes of the order of 25 to 30 percent and a distributor a similar margin again, so my ex-cellar price roughly triples by the time it reaches a restaurant list, before the restaurant applies its own multiple, and restaurants commonly price a bottle at two and a half to three times their wholesale cost. I would therefore enter three or four metropolitan markets rather than the country: New York, Chicago and San Francisco have the depth of independent restaurants that suits an allocated wine. Portfolio fit matters more than size when choosing between houses such as Skurnik, Polaner Selections, Kermit Lynch and Vine Street Imports.

**United Kingdom.** A single national importer with real on-trade specialism — Liberty Wines, Hallgarten, Berkmann and Boutinot are the obvious comparison set — reaching independent restaurants and hotel groups. Direct supply to a few London groups is legally possible and margin-rich, but I would decline it: competing with the sales force I depend on is the fastest way to lose it.

**Canada and the Nordics.** Provincial liquor boards and the Scandinavian monopolies control listing and logistics, and the on-trade is supplied through them via an agent. Here the strategy is not selling but tendering: choosing the right agent and working to the board's listing calendar and specifications.

**Japan, South Korea, Hong Kong and Singapore.** Importer-led, sommelier-driven, and comfortable with very small parcels — structurally ideal for an estate of my size. Hong Kong removed duty on wine and Singapore levies it by volume of alcohol, which deepens fine-wine lists in both.

**Domestic and near-EU.** Direct to restaurant is feasible and keeps the whole margin, but it consumes my selling days. I will do it within a day's drive of the winery and for the cuvées with the least volume.

## The cost of each route

The choice between direct and distributor is a cost question, not a philosophical one. Direct, I keep the intermediary margin but pay for logistics, credit risk, receivables where payment terms of 60 to 90 days are normal, and my own travel. Through a distributor I surrender that margin and buy a licensed warehouse, a sales force, credit control and local compliance. At 8,000 cases the arithmetic is decisive: I cannot fund receivables in five countries, so distributors everywhere except the doorstep.

My selling budget is then small and specific: samples and freight, two visits per key market per year, one trade tasting per market, and a jointly funded promotional mechanic — by-the-glass support or a staff incentive — rather than any consumer spend.

## Winning the placement and keeping it

On-trade sales are won by people, in sequence. Get the wine into the sommelier's mouth. Target one or two reference accounts per city first, ideally by the glass, because the restaurants other restaurants copy do the selling for me. Train the distributor's sales team, not only the buyer — a rep carrying four hundred wines forgets a small estate unless it has a story tellable in one sentence. Bring sommeliers and importers to the estate: for a small premium winery, site visits are the highest-return money I spend. Then defend the placement through list awards and sommelier competitions.

## Measurement, and when to leave

Per market I track number and quality of accounts, repeat rate, cases per account and, above all, price realisation on the list. Reviewed annually, a market that will not repeat gets exited rather than discounted into — discounting a premium wine in the on-trade is not reversible.

## Conclusion

Few markets, entered deeply; distributors wherever law or receivables demand them; direct sales kept for the doorstep; and the budget spent on the people who recommend the wine rather than on advertising it. At 8,000 cases my constraint is never demand. It is the cost of reaching it, and the strategy is the discipline of not spreading that cost too thin.
