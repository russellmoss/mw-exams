## The decision is made about the occasion, not the wine

The single most useful fact about this market is that a large share of wine is bought for a purpose other than drinking it: gifting, business entertaining, banqueting, and demonstrating status at a table. The chooser is frequently not the drinker, and the buyer's risk is social rather than hedonic. That reframes every subsequent choice: the shopper is buying a signal that will be read by someone else, which is why recognisability beats intrinsic quality and why a wine that is unknown to the recipient has negative value however good it is.

## How the decision is actually made

**Colour and health.** Red wine accounts for the large majority of still wine consumed, reinforced by the auspicious associations of the colour and a widely held belief in cardiovascular benefit. White and sparkling are growing from a small base, mainly in tier-one cities.

**Country before region, region before variety.** France, and within it Bordeaux, remains the default prestige cue; "Lafite" functions as a generic byword for expensive wine rather than as a specific product. Varietal literacy is low relative to the UK or the US, so the country of origin carries the quality signal that a variety carries elsewhere. Australia and Chile compete on the same shelf with a structural advantage, since the phase-down under the China-Australia free trade agreement removed the tariff on Australian wine from 2019 and Chile enjoys equivalent access.

**Price as the quality proxy, and packaging as the price proxy.** With little basis for independent judgement, buyers read price directly as quality — which makes discounting dangerous — and read packaging as price. Heavy glass, gold, deep punts, château imagery, medals and a Chinese brand name that sounds auspicious all do real work. Numerology matters: eight is favourable, four is avoided.

**Channel and voice.** E-commerce is central rather than supplementary. JD.com and Alibaba's Tmall are major wine retailers, the 11.11 Singles' Day event is a genuine sales spike, and discovery increasingly happens on WeChat through key opinion leaders and social selling rather than through critics or the specialist press. Domestic wine from Ningxia, Shandong and Xinjiang, led by Changyu and COFCO's Great Wall, competes with strong distribution and rising credibility, particularly in Ningxia.

## One market or several?

The generalisation to avoid is "the Chinese consumer". A young, often female, digitally native drinker in Shanghai or Shenzhen, buying for herself, exploring white and sparkling and using an app rather than a distributor, behaves much more like a Hong Kong or Singapore consumer than like a buyer in a tier-three city where gifting and the banquet still govern. The addressable market for a boutique white is the first group and is small; the volume is in the second and is not accessible on the same terms. Producers who treat the market as one thing typically price for the second and market to the first.

## How this differs from established markets

In the UK the gatekeeper is a handful of grocery buyers; in the US it is a licensed three-tier system; in China it is several hundred provincial importers and sub-distributors, with relationship capital deciding listings and with stock frequently sitting in warehouses rather than moving to consumers. Where a UK brand builds via retailer listings and a US brand via distributor pull, a Chinese brand builds via platform ranking and social proof. And in established markets the wine's own name travels; in China the Chinese-character name — often coined by the importer — is the brand the consumer actually knows.

## Implications for producers

**Register the trademark before you ship.** Trademark registration in China operates first-to-file, so a producer who lets an importer coin and register the Chinese name can lose control of its own identity and be unable to change partner. This is the single most consequential and most commonly ignored implication.

**Price with the whole chain in view.** Import duty, consumption tax and VAT compound to add roughly half again to the landed cost, before an importer, a provincial distributor, a sub-distributor and an outlet each take a margin. An FOB price set for the UK will land absurdly on a Chinese shelf. Cross-border e-commerce shortens the chain and is worth using, but it does not build the on-trade presence that creates the prestige cue in the first place.

**Design for the signal.** Packaging, format and a defensible origin story are not vanity; they are the product attributes the buyer can actually evaluate.

## Implications for distributors

Choose partners on capability, not on the size of the first order — the recurring failure is a large opening shipment that never sells through and is then discounted, destroying the price positioning permanently. Invest in education of the distributor's own sales team, since they, not the label, explain the wine. Expect long payment cycles and high inventory risk, and monitor grey-market and counterfeit exposure, which erodes both price and trust.

My overall assessment is that China rewards patience and brand discipline and punishes opportunism — and that the volume-led "gold rush" thesis, already looking tired by 2019 as total consumption stopped growing, is precisely the generalisation a candidate should not repeat.
