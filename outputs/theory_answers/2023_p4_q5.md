## How I am segmenting this

A distributor is not one thing, so a single list of virtues would be worthless. Three supply-chain models behave differently:

**Three-tier (United States).** The producer sells to an importer, the importer to state distributors, and neither may sell to the consumer. Margins stack, and several US states have franchise laws that make terminating a distributor difficult and expensive — so partner selection is close to irreversible.

**Single national importer-distributor (UK, much of Europe, Japan, South East Asia).** One partner buys, warehouses, sells and often markets. The relationship is concentrated: it is the market.

**Monopoly and agency (Nordics, most Canadian provinces).** The monopoly is the buyer; the agent is a paid representative who does not own the stock. The Nordic monopolies award most listings by blind tender against a written specification, so "selling" here means tender-writing and category-timing.

Positioning cuts across all three. A mainstream brand needs distribution breadth, promotional execution and logistics; a prestige brand needs placement quality and price discipline, and would rather be in forty right accounts than four hundred. The elements below matter in both, but not equally.

## A shared plan, written down

The commonplace is that both parties need shared objectives; what makes it real is that the objectives are quantified and jointly signed. Good practice is an annual business plan agreed before the year starts: case volume by SKU, points of distribution by channel, target shelf and list prices, the marketing contribution and who spends it, and a quarterly review at which both sides are held to it.

Bad practice is visible and common. The producer sets a shipment target the distributor never accepted, then loads stock at year end to hit it. The distributor takes the wine, discounts it to clear, the shelf price falls, and the brand's positioning is damaged by its own producer's budget pressure. Prestige houses avoid it by allocating below demand; mainstream brands rarely can.

## Price architecture and margin transparency

Both parties must model the full chain: ex-cellar, freight and duty, landed cost, importer and distributor margin, retail or restaurant margin, shelf price. Good practice is agreeing that model and the promotional mechanics — billbacks against agreed activity rather than permanent ex-cellar reductions, which are almost impossible to reverse.

The bad-practice case is instructive: a producer quietly gives a lower price to a second market or a large buying group; the wine reappears as parallel imports undercutting the appointed distributor, who stops investing because someone else harvests the demand. Grey trade is usually the producer's fault, and it destroys more distributor relationships than poor sales do.

## Depletions, not shipments

The single clearest marker of a real relationship is what gets reported. Shipments measure what the producer sold to the distributor; depletions measure what the distributor sold to the trade. Good practice is monthly depletion and inventory reporting by account, which tells the producer where the wine actually is and how many weeks of stock sit in the warehouse. Bad practice is a producer who celebrates a record year that is in fact three months of unsold inventory in a Californian warehouse, and discovers it when reorders stop.

## People, and time in market

Distributors carry hundreds of suppliers; a rep sells what they know and like. Good practice is investment in the sales force rather than the buyer: staff trainings, work-with days riding along on accounts, incentives, a funded ambassador for larger brands, and hosting the sales team at the estate. Bad practice is the producer who sends samples and a price list and then complains about performance, or who visits once a year to see the buyer and never meets the people who actually sell.

## Contract, and the discipline of review

Exclusivity, territory, minimum volumes, brand ownership, intellectual property, term and — critically — termination and stock buy-back. Payment terms of 60 to 90 days are normal, and longer in parts of Asia, so credit terms and consignment arrangements belong in the agreement rather than in an argument later. Good practice is an annual review against the agreed plan with a defined remedy period. Bad practice is a handshake exclusivity in a US franchise state that the producer then cannot exit.

## Trust and cultural fit

None of the above survives without candour, and the operational test of trust is whether bad news travels early: a missed forecast, a competitor listing lost, a vintage that will not arrive. Portfolio fit matters as much as size — being the small prestige brand in a volume house means being ignored — and so does personal continuity, since these relationships are held by individuals.

## Conclusion

The key elements are a jointly agreed and measurable plan, a transparent price architecture defended by both sides, depletion-level information, sustained investment in the distributor's people, and a contract that makes review and exit orderly. Their weighting shifts by model: in the US, partner selection and price discipline dominate because exit is hard; with a single national importer, information flow and people investment matter most; in the monopolies, the discipline is tendering and forecasting. What is constant is that the distributor is a customer to be sold to, not a channel to be pushed through.
