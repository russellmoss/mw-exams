## What a hybrid is, and what it is not

A hybrid is an interspecific cross: a vine whose parents belong to two or more different species of the genus Vitis. In practice that almost always means Vitis vinifera crossed with an American species — V. riparia, V. rupestris, V. berlandieri, V. labrusca, V. aestivalis, V. arizonica — or with the Asian V. amurensis. It must be distinguished from a crossing, which is an intraspecific cross of two vinifera parents: Muller-Thurgau, Pinotage and Cabernet Sauvignon itself are crossings, not hybrids.

Precision here matters, because the terminology is routinely confused. Lambrusco is not a hybrid but a family of vinifera varieties; Tinta Negra, Madeira's workhorse, is likewise vinifera. Labrusca is not a hybrid either but a native North American species in its own right, from which hybrids are bred. Marechal Foch is a genuine hybrid, and it is a black variety, not a white one. Vidal Blanc, Seyval Blanc, Baco Noir, Chambourcin, Rondo and the newer disease-resistant varieties are hybrids; Norton is essentially V. aestivalis-derived.

## The current role: rootstocks first

By area, the overwhelming current use of hybrids is below ground. Almost all the world's vinifera is grafted onto interspecific hybrid rootstocks bred after phylloxera — SO4, 5BB and 420A from berlandieri x riparia; 110R, 1103P and 140Ru from berlandieri x rupestris; 101-14 from riparia x rupestris; 41B from a vinifera x berlandieri cross for high-lime soils. This is hybrid viticulture at global scale, and it is the reason the industry's objection to hybrids is a cultural rather than a technical one.

Above ground, hybrids occupy three current niches. First, cold and humid regions where vinifera is unreliable: eastern North America, where Vidal is the basis of Canadian icewine, and where cold-hardy University of Minnesota releases such as Marquette and Frontenac have made viticulture possible well north of the vinifera line. Second, emerging cool-climate regions — Scandinavia and Poland have been developed largely on hybrids, and English growers have used Seyval Blanc and Rondo. Third, high-disease-pressure European vineyards, where the fungus-resistant PIWI varieties are planted specifically to cut spray passes; Solaris and Cabernet Cortis came out of the German breeding programmes at Freiburg, and there is now meaningful planting in Germany, Switzerland and northern Italy. Volume table wine in Brazil's Serra Gaucha remains largely labrusca-derived.

## Breeding programmes and where they lead

The first wave of hybrids in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was a response to phylloxera and mildew, and it produced varieties of poor quality that the EU eventually excluded from quality wine. Modern breeding is a different exercise: marker-assisted selection allows breeders to pyramid several resistance genes for downy and powdery mildew into a vine that is genetically close to vinifera after repeated backcrossing, which shortens the programme and largely removes the foxy labrusca character. Geisenheim, Freiburg, Julius Kuhn, Agroscope in Switzerland and the Udine programme in Friuli are the European centres; INRAE's ResDur programme released Artaban, Floreal, Vidoc and Voltis in the late 2010s.

The forward case rests on two pressures. Disease is the first: UC Davis released Pierce's disease resistant varieties derived from Vitis arizonica at the end of the 2010s, addressing a bacterial disease with no cure and a range that warming winters are extending; and in Europe the withdrawal of fungicide actives and the restriction of copper leave wet-climate and organic growers with a shrinking toolbox. Climate is the second, in both directions: cold-hardiness opens new latitudes, while drought and heat tolerance from American species are now breeding targets for regions becoming too hot.

## The case for

Enthusiasm is strongest where spraying is hardest. A resistant variety can remove most fungicide applications, and with them the diesel, the compaction, the water and the labour of repeated tractor passes — a substantial economic argument at a time when skilled vineyard labour is the scarcest input in most regions, and a strong environmental one for growers under retailer or certification pressure. For Scandinavia, Poland and much of eastern North America the argument is simply that there is no viable alternative. Regulation has begun to follow: the EU reform of 2021 opened PDO wines to interspecific hybrids for the first time, Bordeaux admitted a short list of climate-adapted (not primarily disease-resistant) varieties, and Champagne admitted the fungus-resistant hybrid Voltis on a trial basis. Wine identity in Europe is built on variety and place; admitting new varieties dilutes an appellation's meaning and its market. Consumer recognition of hybrid names is negligible, so the grower plants a thirty-year asset with no established price. Quality remains contested — the older hybrids carry aromatic and structural faults, and the newer ones have short track records with no evidence yet on ageing. Resistance is not permanent: pathogens overcome single resistance genes, which is why pyramiding matters and why a resistant variety should still be sprayed occasionally to protect the trait. And there is a legitimate argument that gene editing within vinifera, which retains the variety name and the market, may reach the same objective by a route the classic regions can accept.

## Conclusion

Hybrids are already universal as rootstocks and indispensable at the climatic frontier. Their future is not the replacement of the classic varieties but a substantial and growing share of three specific territories: new cold regions, high-disease-pressure and organic vineyards, and blending components admitted at the margins of established appellations. I expect adoption to be driven by regulation and by the loss of crop-protection chemistry rather than by consumer demand, which will remain the binding constraint.
