## What the term denotes, and where it fractures

By "natural wine" I mean wine from grapes farmed without synthetic herbicides, pesticides or fertilisers — in practice usually certified organic or biodynamic — hand-harvested, fermented with indigenous yeasts, and bottled with as little added and as little removed as the producer judges possible: no acidification, no enzymes, no concentrate, no cultured yeast, usually no fining, often no filtration, and either no sulphur dioxide or a small addition at bottling.

That definition is a description of intent, not a threshold, and I want to be honest that the category genuinely does not agree with itself. Two producers can both claim the term while disagreeing over temperature-controlled fermentation, cross-flow filtration, a cultured yeast used to restart a stuck ferment, or 25 mg/L of SO2 before shipping. Sulphur is the fault line. For one wing any addition is a breach of faith; for another, a modest dose is the difference between a wine that survives a container crossing the equator and one that arrives refermenting and mousy. Any legal text would have to pick a side of that argument, and the movement itself has not.

**My position:** I agree with the statement. The category does not need a legal definition, and a serious attempt to write one would harm the producers it is meant to protect. What the market does need is verification of the claims producers already make, and enforcement against those who make them falsely — a different instrument, already half-built.

## What already governs the category

The claim that natural wine is ungoverned is simply wrong, and the evidence sits in three layers.

The first is the one quasi-official definition that exists. The Vin Méthode Nature charter, drawn up by a French producers' syndicate and accepted by the French authorities in 2020, requires certified organic fruit, hand harvesting, indigenous yeasts, no additives, and no severe physical intervention — no flash pasteurisation, reverse osmosis, cross-flow filtration or heavy fining. Crucially it issues two logos: one for wines with no added sulphites at all, one permitting up to 30 mg/L total sulphur dioxide added after fermentation. Membership is annual and revocable. It is opt-in, it names the disagreement rather than resolving it, and it was written by practitioners.

The second layer is private association. Vini Veri and VinNatur in Italy, the latter with a protocol capping total sulphur dioxide at around 50 mg/L and a programme that lab-tests members' wines for pesticide residues — a harder audit than most appellations impose on themselves. RAW Wine requires exhibitors to declare total sulphur dioxide and sets a ceiling of around 70 mg/L. The Natural Wine Association in the UK publishes its own guidelines. They disagree with one another, which is the point: three ceilings, 30, 50 and 70 mg/L, describing three defensible readings of the same idea.

The third layer already has the force of law. Almost every natural producer is certified organic or biodynamic, and EU organic wine rules cap total sulphur dioxide at roughly 100 mg/L for dry reds and 150 mg/L for dry whites and rosés, against conventional ceilings of 150 and 200 mg/L, alongside a restricted additive list. Demeter goes further. The farming half of the natural claim is therefore already legally certified and audited; the winemaking half is covered by charters that go beyond what any regulator would dare impose.

## How strict, and could it be enforced?

This is where the case for legislation collapses, and it is a trilemma. Write the definition strictly enough to mean what the movement means, and it excludes a large share of self-declared natural producers — including many whose wines are the reason anyone cares about the category. Write it loosely enough to be politically passable, and it captures a great volume of ordinary organic low-intervention wine and hands the term to precisely the industrial players it was coined against. Write it at any level and you face the enforcement problem: no laboratory can tell you from the glass whether the yeast was indigenous, whether a cross-flow filter was used, or whether the tank was chilled. Sulphur is measurable; almost nothing else in the charter is. Enforcement would therefore rest on documentary audit of the cellar — which is exactly what Vin Méthode Nature and VinNatur already do, at their own cost.

There is a further cost that is rarely stated. To define "natural wine" in law is to declare, by statute, that the other ninety-nine per cent of the world's wine is unnatural. No regulator should be asked to codify that, and the wider industry should not want them to.

## Who would actually gain, and who would lose

**Producers.** Mostly small, undercapitalised, frequently farming under ten hectares. A further certification is a real cost in fees and paperwork against a tiny production. Against that, established natural producers do suffer free-riding — and it is their complaint, not the consumer's, that drives most calls for a definition.

**Distributors, retailers and the on-trade.** The strongest commercial argument for definition: a monopoly buyer or a supermarket category manager cannot build a listing around a term they cannot specify. But specialist importers already buy this category on relationship and charter membership, and no legal text will make natural wine a supermarket category, because unfiltered, low-sulphur wine cannot survive that supply chain.

**Regulatory bodies.** The EU took years to produce organic wine rules that amount to an additive list and two SO2 numbers. A "natural" category would require labelling law, control bodies and third-country equivalence for a fraction of one per cent of volume. It is a poor use of scarce regulatory capacity.

**Quality-assurance professionals.** The one place I concede real force. Unfiltered wines bottled with little or no SO2 are microbiologically live: refermentation, Brettanomyces, mousiness, volatile acidity. But a definition does not make an unstable wine stable. That is a technical and commercial problem, solved by cold chain, competent cellar hygiene and honest trade expectations, not by a statute.

**Consumers.** The real grievance is deception, and deception is already illegal. If a wine made with commercial yeast, nutrients and 120 mg/L of SO2 is sold as natural, that is a misleading-claim matter under existing consumer-protection law in the EU, UK and US. The gap is enforcement, not definition.

**Society.** The one genuine risk is health-halo drift — "natural" implying "healthier". That too is existing labelling law, and it would be made worse, not better, by the state putting its name to the word.

## The strongest counter-argument

The best case against me is that unprotected terms get captured: "sustainable", "artisan" and "old vine" all demonstrate it, and Vin Méthode Nature proves a workable definition can be written.

I accept the first point and it is why I do not argue for doing nothing. But Vin Méthode Nature is my model, not my refutation. It is an opt-in charter given official recognition — a protected claim, not a statutory category. It works precisely because practitioners wrote it, it can be revised annually, and producers who dislike it can simply not join. That is the opposite of a legal definition of natural wine, and it is enough.

## Conclusion

Natural wine does not need a legal definition. It needs three things: enforcement of existing misleading-claim law against those who borrow the term; official recognition of opt-in producer charters that already audit and already verify; and mandatory declaration of total sulphur dioxide, the one variable that is both measurable and genuinely contested. Definition without verification protects nobody. Verification without definition protects everyone who actually needs protecting.
