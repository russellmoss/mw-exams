## Scope and the two options

Bulk wine means unpackaged wine moved in volume from the country of production to be packaged elsewhere, as opposed to wine shipped in its finished retail package. That definition excludes bottled, canned and bag-in-box freight entirely. Two systems carry it: ISO tanks and flexitanks. Both move wine intermodally in the container network; they differ in construction, cost, reusability and the protection they give the wine, and I take each in turn before setting them against each other.

## ISO tanks: the description

An ISO tank is a stainless steel pressure vessel built to the standard intermodal container footprint, so it sits directly on a standard truck chassis and transfers without decanting to rail wagon or container ship. Capacity is typically around 24,000 litres, and they are built either as a single vessel or divided into three or four sectional compartments. They are insulated, fitted with sanitary valves and top manways, and can be nitrogen-blanketed. They are durable assets, cleaned and recertified between loads and used for many years.

## ISO tanks: risks and benefits

The benefits are control. Stainless steel is inert and impermeable, so there is no risk of taint or oxygen ingress through the wall. The vessel can be inerted and pressure-tested, and it can be part-discharged, so a buyer can draw down over several bottling runs rather than commit the whole load at once. Insulation dampens temperature swings across a long ocean leg. Sectional tanks allow three or four different wines, or three grades of the same wine, to travel in one movement — decisive for a producer shipping small volumes of several lines. And because the tank is reusable, the packaging waste per shipment is negligible.

The risks are commercial and logistical rather than qualitative. Tanks are expensive to buy or lease and there are far fewer of them, so availability on a given lane is limited. They must be repositioned empty, and dead freight on a one-way trade lane such as the southern hemisphere to Europe is a real cost the wine has to carry. Cleaning is a genuine hazard: a tank that previously carried a non-food or strongly flavoured cargo, or one poorly rinsed after caustic, will taint a load, so certification of previous cargo and cleaning is essential. Tare weight is high, reducing the payload allowed under road weight limits.

## Flexitanks: the description

A flexitank is a flexible bladder fitted inside a standard 20ft dry container, filled and discharged through a valve at the container door and restrained by a bulkhead. Capacity is usually 16,000 to 26,000 litres. They are almost always single use. Current bags are multilayer polyethylene incorporating an ethylene vinyl alcohol copolymer barrier, which is an effective barrier both to oxygen and to the organic compounds that would otherwise migrate in and taint the wine.

## Flexitanks: risks and benefits

The benefits are cost and availability. A flexitank is a fraction of the cost of an ISO tank, needs no repositioning, and uses standard dry containers, which are available everywhere and on every lane. Fill and discharge are quick and need no specialist terminal. The bag is filled full, so there is no ullage and no headspace to inert — an underrated quality advantage over a partly filled tank.

The risks are real and are why the trade has not standardised on them. The bag can fail: a rupture or valve leak at sea loses the whole load and contaminates the container. There is no insulation and no temperature control, and container decks can exceed 40°C in the tropics, cooking a wine on a long equatorial routing — the single largest quality risk in bulk shipping. The bag cannot be part-discharged, so the receiving winery must have 24,000 litres of clean, available tank space on the day. Oxygen ingress, while low, is not zero over a six-week voyage, and unlike a tank the bag cannot be blanketed. And a single-use polymer bag is a waste and recycling problem that sits awkwardly with the sustainability case for bulk shipping.

## Risks common to both, and how they are managed

Bulk wine is a young, unpackaged wine spending weeks unsupervised. Free SO2 is typically set higher at fill, of the order of 35-45 mg/L for a white, to allow for depletion in transit; the wine is stabilised and filtered before loading; and agitation in transit keeps it in contact with whatever oxygen is present. Retained samples are taken at fill and re-analysed on arrival so any change can be attributed. Beyond the wine itself, bulk transfers the bottling decision, and therefore control of the finished product, to the destination — a commercial risk to brand integrity, and a legal one, since several appellations, Rioja among them, require bottling within the region, which removes the option altogether.

## Choosing between them

On protection of the wine, ISO tanks win: inert walls, insulation, inerting and part-discharge. On cost and availability, flexitanks win decisively, which is why they carry the great majority of bulk volume. On environmental performance both beat packaged freight by a wide margin — a 20ft container of packaged wine carries of the order of 9,000-10,000 bottles, some 7,000 litres, where a bulk load in the same box carries roughly three times the volume, a commonly cited saving of the order of 30-40% in transport emissions per litre, before counting that glass is the largest single component of a packaged wine's carbon footprint and is best made near the consumer.

My judgement is that value and lot size decide it. High-value wine, small lots, multiple lines, or a long tropical routing justify an ISO tank. Large single lots of commercial wine on established lanes go in flexitanks, with the residual risk managed by shipping in cooler months, specifying reputable bag suppliers, and holding retained samples.
