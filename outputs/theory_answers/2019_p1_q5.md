## Framing the decision

A rootstock is chosen once and lives for thirty years or more, so it is the least reversible decision in establishing a vineyard — far harder to correct than trellis, pruning or even variety, which can be field-grafted. It is selected against a soil and a scion, not from a preference list. Everything below follows from a proper site survey: soil pits, texture, depth, active lime and pH, electrical conductivity, nematode assay and phylloxera history.

## Soil pests: phylloxera, and which nematode

Protection from soil pests is the founding reason grafted vines exist. *Daktulosphaira vitifoliae* kills own-rooted *vinifera*, so unless the site is genuinely phylloxera-free and expected to remain so — deep sands as in parts of Chile, Colares or the Riverland, or an isolated, quarantined region — a resistant rootstock is not optional. Resistance is not uniform: *rupestris* x *berlandieri* and *riparia* x *rupestris* crosses are strongly resistant, while AXR1, with *vinifera* parentage, failed catastrophically in California in the 1980s and is the standing warning against trading resistance for vigour.

Nematodes require more analysis than they are usually given, and the species matters. Root-knot nematodes (*Meloidogyne* spp.) are the direct feeders, damaging roots in warm sandy soils and stunting young vines — Ramsey, Freedom and Harmony are the standard answers in California and inland Australia. The more serious problem is *Xiphinema index*, the dagger nematode, which vectors grapevine fanleaf virus: the nematode itself does modest damage, but the virus it transmits destroys yield and fruit set permanently, and it survives on root fragments for years after a vineyard is pulled. That drives a different response — a long fallow before replanting, and rootstocks with *rotundifolia* parentage such as 039-16 or Nemadex bred specifically to resist the vector. A grower who plants a *Meloidogyne*-tolerant rootstock into a fanleaf site has spent money on the wrong problem.

## Site limitations: chemistry, water, structure

Rootstocks are the tool for soil conditions that cannot be changed. **Chemistry**: high active lime induces iron chlorosis, so calcareous sites need *berlandieri*-rich material — 41B and Fercal tolerate high active lime, whereas *riparia*- and *rupestris*-based stocks yellow and die back. Acid soils with aluminium toxicity, and salinity in irrigated arid regions, call for salt-excluding stocks such as Ramsey and 1103P; salt sensitivity is why 101-14 fails under saline irrigation. **Water**: 110R, 140Ru and 1103P root deeply and tolerate drought, while Riparia Gloire and 101-14 root shallowly, suit moist fertile soils and will collapse in a dry summer without irrigation; conversely, in waterlogged or heavy clay sites the *riparia* types tolerate wet feet better than *rupestris*. **Structure and fertility**: shallow, compacted or infertile soils need a stock that explores volume; deep alluvium already supplies plenty and needs restraint.

## The scion: compatibility and health status

Graft compatibility is real and variety-specific — some scion/rootstock combinations union poorly, express delayed incompatibility years later, or show graft-union swelling and premature decline. Nursery advice and local trial data, not theory, decide this. Equally important is the health status of both partners: certified virus-tested material for scion and rootstock alike, because leafroll and fanleaf are propagated through the graft and no rootstock choice will rescue an infected planting. Clonal selection of the scion interacts with the rootstock decision, since a high-yielding clone on a vigorous stock compounds a problem that either alone would not create.

## Vigour, and what it depends on

Vigour conferred is the property growers ask about first and understand least, because it is not a fixed rating. The same rootstock is vigorous on deep fertile clay and restrained on shallow gravel. It must be matched to variety, planting density, target vine size, soil nutrient status and salinity together: a devigorating stock such as Riparia Gloire or 3309C suits a fertile, high-density planting of a vigorous variety; a vigorous stock such as Ramsey or 140Ru suits a poor, dry, wide-spaced site that must fill its space. Get this wrong and every subsequent decision — trellis, pruning weight, canopy work, spray coverage — is fighting the rootstock for the life of the vineyard.

## Yield, ripening date and fruit composition

Rootstock also acts directly on the wine. It shifts crop level through bunch number and berry size; it advances or delays ripening, so *riparia*-based stocks are chosen in marginal climates such as the Mosel and Champagne partly because they ripen early, while a late stock such as 140Ru can push harvest past the useful window in a cool site. It influences potassium uptake and hence must pH, water uptake and hence berry size and concentration, and nitrogen supply and hence fermentation behaviour and thiol expression. These are quality outcomes, and they are the point of the exercise.

## The considerations are not independent

The critical skill is seeing the interactions. A hot sandy site with root-knot nematodes and marginal water needs nematode tolerance and drought tolerance in one plant — 1103P and Ramsey answer both — but Ramsey's vigour then demands wide spacing and a divided canopy, and its potassium uptake pushes must pH, which the winery must correct. A calcareous drought-prone site wants lime tolerance and deep rooting, and 140Ru delivers both but delays ripening. Every choice trades one property for another.

## Conclusion

The critical considerations are pest pressure identified to species, the specific chemical, hydric and physical limitations of the soil, the compatibility and health of the scion, the vigour the site will express, and the consequences for yield, ripening and fruit composition. There is no simple, single answer: the rootstock is selected as the compromise that best fits this site, this scion and this wine, and the discipline lies in knowing which property is being sacrificed.
