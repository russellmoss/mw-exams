## Scope, and the loaded word in the proposition

I read this as a question about the industry's balance sheet, not the consumer's. The claim is that a knowledge asymmetry between seller and buyer works in the seller's favour, and I test it as I would any other asymmetry: who captures the rent, at what cost, and over what horizon. Whether limited knowledge is bad for the drinker is a different question and I do not answer it.

"Blessing" is also loaded, and deliberately so. It implies not merely that the industry benefits passively but that it has an interest in the ignorance persisting — that the trade uses limited knowledge to its advantage. That reading is the one worth testing, because it is the one that makes the proposition uncomfortable. My conclusion, argued below, is that limited consumer knowledge is a short-term blessing to particular sectors and a long-term liability to the industry as a whole, and that the sectors it enriches are not the ones that carry the eventual cost.

## The blessing, sector by sector

**Producers.** Limited knowledge is what allows a wine to be sold on its label rather than its liquid. It permits generous latitude in what goes into a bottle: blending across sites and vintages, a wide tolerance on what "old vines" or "single vineyard" mean in practice, and the use of terms such as Reserve, Vieilles Vignes and Grand Vin, which carry no legal definition in most jurisdictions and which sell precisely because the buyer assumes they do. It also protects the mediocre. A consumer who cannot identify reduction, volatile acidity or premature oxidation will not return the bottle, and a producer who ships a flawed lot will hear nothing. That is a real, quantifiable saving.

**Retailers.** The asymmetry is the foundation of the modern wine aisle. Own-label works because the shopper has no benchmark against which to judge what the wine is worth, so the retailer captures a margin no branded supplier could. Deep-discount mechanics — an inflated recommended price against a permanent "half price" — function only where the consumer cannot value the wine independently. Category management by price band rather than by quality assumes, correctly, that price is being used as a proxy for judgement.

**Educators.** An entire profitable sector exists because of the gap. Wine education, from introductory courses to this examination, is a business whose addressable market is precisely the distance between what consumers know and what they wish they knew. A perfectly informed consumer base would abolish it.

**Commentators and journalists.** The same logic. Demand for a trusted proxy — a score, a recommendation, a list — exists only because buyers cannot judge for themselves. The critic's authority is a direct function of the reader's uncertainty, and the fine-wine market's dependence on scores is the purest expression of it.

**The fine-wine trade.** Scarcity narratives, en primeur, allocations and secondary-market pricing all rest on a buyer who accepts a valuation he cannot independently derive. It is not an accident that the periods of steepest price inflation coincided with the entry of large numbers of new and inexperienced buyers.

Taken together, this is a serious case. Across five sectors, limited knowledge lowers cost, widens margin, sustains pricing power and creates whole businesses. In the short term the proposition is simply true.

## The curse — what compounds over time

**It caps the category.** A consumer who does not know what he likes is a risk-averse consumer. He buys the same three brands, defaults to price as a signal, and does not trade up, because trading up requires the confidence to spend more on something unfamiliar. The industry's entire premiumisation strategy depends on a consumer who knows enough to want better — and that is the consumer the asymmetry prevents from existing. Beer and spirits have spent two decades simplifying and explaining themselves and have taken share while doing so.

**It makes fraud invisible until it is catastrophic.** Where buyers cannot detect adulteration, adulteration persists until a regulator or a journalist finds it, by which point the damage is category-wide rather than firm-specific. The 1985 Austrian diethylene glycol scandal, which cost Austria its export market for a generation, the 2008 Brunello di Montalcino investigation into unauthorised varieties, and the conviction of Rudy Kurniawan in 2013 for counterfeiting rare Burgundy and Bordeaux each punished honest producers hardest. Ignorance does not prevent the reckoning; it defers and enlarges it.

**It builds no loyalty.** A consumer whose choice rests on price and packaging can be taken by anyone with better packaging or a lower price — including, increasingly, categories that are not wine at all. The knowledge that the trade withholds is precisely what would make its customers hard to poach.

**It hands power to intermediaries.** If the consumer cannot judge, someone must judge for him, and that someone captures the value. Retailer buyers, critics and platforms have accumulated exactly the leverage that an informed consumer would have retained for himself — and producers pay for it in listing terms and margin.

**And the exploitation is a withdrawal, not an income.** Every unregulated quality term, every phantom château, every anchored discount spends a small amount of the trust the category holds. That account is finite, and the recent and rapid loss of confidence in alcohol's health claims shows how quickly a reserve of credibility can be drawn down.

## Which consumer?

The proposition is not uniform. For the novice, limited knowledge is a blessing to the seller and a barrier to entry to the category — good for the transaction, bad for recruitment. For the involved enthusiast, knowledge is what has driven the growth of every interesting margin in the trade over thirty years: grower Champagne, single-vineyard bottlings, obscure varieties, the entire specialist merchant sector. That business exists because consumers learned. For collectors and investors, limited knowledge is the ignorance the counterfeiter needs. For new buyers in fast-growing markets, it produced spectacular short-term prices for a few labels and a counterfeit problem that suppressed the same market afterwards.

## Verdict

The proposition is true for the short term and for parts of the trade, and false for the industry as a whole over any horizon that matters. Limited knowledge is a blessing to whoever is selling this bottle today and a liability to whoever needs to sell the next one at a higher price. It suppresses exactly the behaviour — curiosity, trading up, loyalty — on which the industry's stated strategy depends, and it converts every fraud into a category-wide crisis.

**What the other side gets right.** I concede the strongest counter-argument: no consumer market operates on full information, wine is genuinely and irreducibly complex, and an industry that waited for an educated consumer would sell very little. Some asymmetry is not exploitation but the ordinary condition of a specialist product, and the trade's job is to make the wine reliably good rather than to make every buyer an expert. That is fair. But there is a difference between tolerating an information gap and being organised around one — and the proposition, read honestly, describes the second.
