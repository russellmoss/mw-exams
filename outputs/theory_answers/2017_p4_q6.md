## Position and scope

I own a brand with an almost uniquely durable asset: a classification of sixty-one red chateaux in 1855, altered only once, when Mouton Rothschild was promoted in 1973. My question is not whether to sell but through which mechanism, at what moment, and at what cost to that asset. I evaluate five options, each on two axes — the commercial (cash, price realisation, risk, cost) and the brand-building (scarcity, distribution quality, provenance, consumer relationship) — because the two frequently point in opposite directions.

## The mechanism, and what has changed

La place de Bordeaux comprises roughly 300 negociants and some 90 courtiers. I set a release price in tranches, the courtier brokers, the negociant takes title and distributes worldwide; the courtier takes about 2% and the negociant of the order of 10%. I receive cash eighteen months to two years before the wine is delivered, employ no sales force and carry no stock or receivable risk.

What has changed is the proposition to the buyer. En primeur worked because early buyers were paid for their risk in discount. The 2010 to 2013 campaigns broke that: releases priced at or above the market value of drinkable back vintages, so purchasers lost money, and the trade stopped recommending it. Simultaneously, price transparency arrived — Liv-ex and Wine-Searcher mean a release price is judged against every back vintage within minutes of publication, where in 1990 it was judged against a merchant's circular. Liv-ex has published a reclassification of the 1855 list by market price, and it does not match the original order, which is the sharpest available evidence that the classification is a brand asset that individual estates can gain or lose value against. Meanwhile selection has tightened: many classified estates now bottle under half the crop as the grand vin, which is a brand decision with an immediate revenue cost.

## Option 1: continue en primeur through la place

**Commercially** this remains hard to beat. It converts an unfinished wine into cash two years early, transfers price and credit risk to the trade, and reaches a hundred markets without a single employee. **For the brand**, the campaign is an annual global publicity event and the negociant network sustains distribution depth no estate could replicate — witness that Opus One, Almaviva and Masseto now use la place precisely for that reach. Against it: I lose all control of price after release, and the consumer's experience of losing money on my last three vintages attaches to my name, not to the negociant's.

## Option 2: withdraw and release physically, when ready

**Commercially** demanding. Latour left en primeur after the 2011 vintage and now releases only when drinking, which means financing several years of stock, building cellar capacity and forgoing early cash — but capturing the appreciation that previously went to the trade, and setting price against a known market rather than a guessed one. **For the brand** it is powerful: perfect provenance, controlled release timing, and a message that the wine is sold when it is ready rather than when the cash is needed. It is available only to an estate with an exceptionally strong balance sheet and a name that will not be forgotten in the intervening years — which is precisely why it reinforces the brands that can afford it.

## Option 3: a hybrid — en primeur plus ex-chateau library releases

Sell the bulk en primeur, and release small parcels of mature vintages ex-chateau through the same negociants. **Commercially** it adds a high-margin revenue line without abandoning early cash. **For the brand** it establishes the chateau as the guarantor of provenance, which is increasingly what fine-wine buyers pay for, and it lets me manage scarcity actively.

## Option 4: direct to consumer and direct to the top on-trade

**Commercially** the highest margin per bottle by a distance, and it captures consumer data la place has always denied me. **For the brand** it builds relationship and controls the story. But it is limited: cellar door and wine tourism can absorb only a fraction of production, and any serious volume taken direct is read by the negociants as a breach — the risk of losing my distribution machine outweighs the margin. This is a supplement, backed by tourism investment of the kind the region has made around the Cite du Vin, opened in 2016.

## Option 5: portfolio and brand extension

Building the second wine into a brand in its own right, adding a white, or licensing the name. **Commercially** it monetises the fruit that selection excludes and reaches a far wider consumer base. **For the brand** it is the standard luxury risk: extension broadens awareness but dilutes scarcity, and a second wine sold too widely and too cheaply erodes the grand vin above it.

## Conclusion

I would keep the majority of the crop on la place, because the distribution and risk transfer are worth the commission, but price the release firmly below the market value of the best available back vintage — restoring the buyer's margin is the single most valuable brand act available to me. I would add a structured ex-chateau library programme and invest in direct relationships at the margins. Full withdrawal is right only for the handful of estates whose name survives absence; for everyone else it is brand-building bought with commercial risk they cannot carry.
