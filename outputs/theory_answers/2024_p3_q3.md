## The distinction

The line between the two is functional, not chemical. An **ingredient** is a substance used in production that remains in the finished wine and performs a function there. A **processing aid** is used deliberately during production for a technological purpose, is not intended to remain, and is removed or reduced to insignificant residues before sale.

On that test, the main processing aids are the fining agents — bentonite, gelatine, isinglass, casein and other milk proteins, egg albumin, PVPP, pea and potato proteins — together with filtration media such as kieselguhr and perlite, pectolytic and other enzymes, yeasts and yeast nutrients, and gases used only for handling. The main ingredients are the grapes, must or wine itself and then sulphur dioxide and other preservatives, sorbic acid, ascorbic acid, dimethyl dicarbonate, tartaric, malic and lactic acid for acidification, deacidifying carbonates, rectified concentrated grape must or sucrose for enrichment, sweetening reserve, and the stabilisers that work by staying in the wine — gum arabic, metatartaric acid, carboxymethylcellulose, potassium polyaspartate.

Two complications matter. First, category follows use: SO2 used to sterilise a barrel is an aid, SO2 carried in the wine as preservative is an ingredient. Second, and this is where the labelling law bites, a number of processing aids are also **allergens** — casein and other milk derivatives, egg albumin and lysozyme, and the sulphites arising from SO2. Because trace residues cannot be excluded, these must be declared whether or not they are held to remain, which cuts straight across the aid/ingredient distinction.

## What the EU actually changed

Regulation (EU) 2021/2117, part of the 2021 Common Agricultural Policy reform package, amended the wine common market organisation to bring wine within the general food-information regime it had been exempt from. For wines produced from 8 December 2023 and sold in the EU, two new obligations apply.

- A **nutrition declaration**. The **energy value** must appear on the physical label. The remaining nutrition declaration — fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, salt — may be given on the label or by electronic means. Values may be established by calculation from generally accepted data for the wine category rather than by analysis of every lot.
- A **list of ingredients**, which may likewise be given by electronic means, typically a QR code or URL on the back label.

Three conditions on the electronic route are the detail that distinguishes a good answer. The e-label must not collect or track user data; it must not carry any marketing or promotional content; and **allergens must appear on the physical label itself** and cannot be displaced to a QR code. Wine produced before 8 December 2023 may be sold until stocks are exhausted, so both label generations sit on shelf together. The rules apply to imports sold in the EU, not only to EU wine.

## Assessment: what this does for consumers

**Availability is not communication.** The physical label gains only the energy value and the allergens; the substance sits behind a code that requires a phone, a signal in a supermarket aisle and a willingness to stop and scan at the point of decision. I expect scan rates to be very low, and the effect on the great majority of purchases to be nil. The transparency gained is real but latent — it is available to the motivated consumer and to journalists and campaigners, which is a genuine if indirect form of accountability.

**Comprehension may fall as disclosure rises.** A list reading "potassium metabisulphite, carboxymethylcellulose, dimethyl dicarbonate" is unfamiliar and reads as chemistry on a product marketed as agricultural. The likely behavioural effect is not informed choice but reinforcement of the natural-wine framing and of "no added" claims, some of which are meaningless. Disclosure without context can mislead.

**Allergens and sulphites: a small, real gain.** Sulphites above 10 mg/L have been compulsory across the EU since the mid-2000s, so the new element here is the certainty that milk and egg derivatives will be named on the pack. The clinical risk from fining residues is very small, but for the allergic consumer, certainty at arm's length in a shop is worth having.

**Vegan and vegetarian suitability is the change consumers will wrongly assume they got.** Ingredient listing does not deliver it. Gelatine and isinglass are processing aids and are not allergens, so neither the ingredient list nor the allergen declaration reveals them. That is regulatorily coherent — they are not in the wine — but it means the label still cannot answer an ethical rather than a safety question, and third-party certification remains necessary. The gap is worth naming; it is not evidence that the regulation is defective.

**Calories will matter more than the ingredient list.** An energy figure on the front of pack makes wine directly comparable with beer, spirits and food in a market where calorie-tracking is mainstream. Over time this pushes producers toward lower-alcohol and lighter styles far more than any ingredient disclosure will.

Two practical implications close the assessment. The e-label can be updated vintage by vintage and batch by batch without reprinting, so the information a consumer sees is more current and more accurate than a printed panel could be — a genuine benefit, provided links are maintained and not allowed to die. And compliance costs fall hardest on the smallest producers; permitting calculated rather than analysed nutrition values is what keeps the burden tolerable, at some cost in precision.

## Conclusion

The distinction is functional and the law respects it, with allergens as a deliberate exception. The changes make far more information available than before, but availability behind a QR code is not the same as informing anyone. Of the two innovations, the energy declaration is the one that will change consumer behaviour; the ingredient list will change producer behaviour, by making formulation visible to anyone who cares to look.
