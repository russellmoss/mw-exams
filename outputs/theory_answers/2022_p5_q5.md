## Definitions

By **wine writer** I mean anyone whose work about wine is, at root, written words intended for publication: critics who assign scores, journalists writing news and features, technical and trade writers, authors of reference works and textbooks, public-relations and press-release writers, retail and e-commerce copywriters and the authors of back labels, newsletter and blog writers, and the scripted text behind most video and social content. I include press releases and commercial copy deliberately. Excluding them would exclude the great majority of words about wine that a consumer actually reads, and would make the question unanswerable in any honest way.

By **need** I mean a dependency rather than a preference: what would break if these people stopped writing. That has to be assessed population by population, and the useful segmentation is not demographic but by engagement and expertise — producers, trade intermediaries, engaged consumers, casual consumers, educators, and the industry's own governance.

## Where the need came from

Wine writing began as a commercial instrument. The merchant's list was the first wine writing, and its descendants — the retailer's website, the importer's tech sheet — still are. Cyrus Redding and later André Simon turned it into a subject with a literature. The decisive shift was Hugh Johnson's World Atlas of Wine, first published in 1971, which created a mass consumer readership and established that wine could be learned rather than merely bought, and Jancis Robinson's Oxford Companion to Wine, first published in 1994, which gave the trade a shared reference layer it still runs on.

Robert Parker's Wine Advocate, founded in 1978, and its hundred-point scale did something different again: it converted judgement into a number that could travel, be quoted in an advertisement and be lent against. That moved purchasing authority from the merchant, who had held it for two centuries, to the independent critic, and made en primeur a market in scores.

None of this is anglophone-specific, and it is a mistake to treat it that way. La Revue du vin de France, Gambero Rosso's Vini d'Italia, Guía Peñín in Spain and Falstaff in Austria and Germany all built domestic markets on the same mechanism. The most striking demonstration of a writer's market power anywhere is not a critic at all: the manga Kami no Shizuku, serialised from 2004, moved demand for named wines across Japan, Korea and Taiwan on a scale no Western publication has matched. Writers made markets, in several languages, and that is the baseline against which "still" has to be measured.

## What the last fifteen years changed

Three things, and they are separable.

The **economic base** collapsed. Print advertising fell away, wine columns were cut, and freelance rates have barely moved in twenty years. The writer's employer is disappearing, which is not at all the same thing as the reader disappearing, and the two are constantly confused.

**Crowd data** replaced two of the critic's functions outright. Vivino supplies a popularity signal from millions of users and Wine-Searcher supplies the price check. Both do those jobs better than any individual ever could. What crowd data cannot supply is a considered judgement of quality, which is a different thing from an average of preferences.

**Social and online channels** moved discovery to visual, personality-led content distributed by algorithm — Instagram and TikTok in the West, WeChat and Xiaohongshu in China. Reach is now decoupled from institutional authority, which is the genuine opportunity as well as the genuine threat: a writer no longer needs a masthead, but neither does anyone else. The counterpart is that independence has become the scarce asset, because a great deal of platform content is paid and undisclosed. The clearest opportunity created by the same shift is direct payment: the subscription models at JancisRobinson.com, Vinous and the Wine Advocate show that willingness to pay survived the collapse of advertising, and simply changed hands.

## Who still needs them, and for what

**The trade — the strongest dependency, and it has grown.** A buyer listing several hundred wines cannot taste them all; a monopoly tender or a multiple's range review is decided partly on documented independent assessment. The trade's need is twofold: informational — what is this, is it sound, what should it cost — and narrative, because a salesperson needs something to say. The much-derided press release is where most of that narrative originates, which is precisely why it belongs inside the definition.

**New and rebuilding regions.** Ningxia, Georgia, Uruguay, Greece, England, the Portuguese interior. A distant buyer will not find these through an algorithm optimised for what already sells, and these regions have no advertising budget. Writers remain the mechanism by which reputation crosses a border, and for them this is a dependency, not a convenience.

**The engaged consumer.** A minority, but the one that trades up and buys widely. The need is orientation and a filter against a repertoire no individual can navigate, and it is increasingly paid for directly.

**Educators and students.** The reference layer — companions, ampelographies, regional monographs, textbooks — is written by wine writers and is the substrate for every qualification, every sommelier list and, increasingly, every machine-learning wine tool. It is the least visible dependency and the hardest to reconstruct if it lapses.

**The industry's own accountability.** The Rudy Kurniawan counterfeiting case, the recurring counterfeit-Bordeaux prosecutions, labour conditions in several producing countries, and the 2020 New York Times investigation into harassment in the Court of Master Sommeliers: none of that came from creators whose model depends on access and goodwill. An industry trading on trust needs reporters, not only reviewers.

## Who does not

The majority of consumers, who buy on price, brand, occasion and shelf position, and who never read wine writing in the first place — the honest historical point is that the mass market never needed writers, it needed retailers. Large branded producers, for whom a review changes nothing at a supermarket price point. Fine-wine buyers who once needed a single number, now that scores have multiplied and the currency has inflated. And younger consumers in most markets, whose discovery is visual and social first; they may still need the underlying work, but not in that form and not from that byline.

## What happens next

The payer changes: from advertisers to subscribers and to institutional and trade clients, producing fewer, better-paid specialists and a hollowed-out middle. The surviving functions are those that cannot be crowdsourced or automated — access, verification, reporting, judgement and accountability — while the descriptive tasting note, the commodity layer, passes to crowds and to machines, and I do not think its loss should be mourned. And the growth is not in English: the expansion of wine writing over the next two decades will be in Mandarin, Portuguese, Spanish and Japanese, authored locally rather than translated.

## Conclusion

Yes — but fewer of them, for a narrower set of purposes, paid by different people. The trade needs wine writers more than ever and will increasingly pay them directly; new regions and engaged consumers need them for discovery and orientation; educators depend on them silently and completely. The casual consumer does not need them and never did.

The nuance that matters is which kind is at risk. The reviewer is being replaced from three directions at once and will survive only in specialist, subscription form. The reporter — the person who checks, verifies and publishes what the industry would rather not read — has the weakest business model and is the one the wine world can least afford to lose.
