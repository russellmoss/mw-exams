## What counts as microbial spoilage

Microbial spoilage is the alteration of a wine by any micro-organism unwanted at that stage — chemically, sensorially or visually. The same organism can be a tool or a fault depending on timing: *Oenococcus oeni* is a resource during malolactic conversion and a spoilage organism if it acts in bottle. The realistic list at bottling is refermentation by *Saccharomyces*; osmotolerant, SO2-tolerant *Zygosaccharomyces bailii* in wines with sugar; *Brettanomyces* producing 4-ethylphenol and 4-ethylguaiacol; lactic acid bacteria causing in-bottle malolactic conversion, spritz, haze, mousiness, ropiness or geranium taint where sorbate is present; and acetic acid bacteria raising volatile acidity and ethyl acetate.

Two clarifications, because both are routinely muddled. Volatile acidity is a measurement — acetic acid plus its esters — not an organism; acetic acid bacteria are one route to it, but yeast and lactic acid bacteria produce acetic acid too, so a high VA is a symptom to be diagnosed, not a diagnosis. And cork taint is not microbial spoilage: TCA is a contaminant carried into the wine on the closure, not the product of a wine organism growing in the wine.

## Why the risk differs for reds and whites

The primary difference is substrate. A red is almost always fermented dry and taken through malolactic conversion, so it arrives at bottling with essentially no fermentable sugar and no malic acid — the two things that most readily feed a bottle-side population. But reds run at higher pH, commonly 3.5 to 3.9, where SO2 is far less effective, and they carry the hydroxycinnamic acid precursors *Brettanomyces* converts to volatile phenols. Reds are also frequently filtered less tightly to protect texture and colour. The red risk is therefore *Brettanomyces*, *Pediococcus* and acetic acid bacteria, at a pH where chemical protection is weak.

A white is the mirror image. Lower pH, commonly 3.0 to 3.4, makes SO2 do more work for less; but whites frequently retain malic acid because malolactic conversion was blocked to keep freshness, and many carry residual sugar. Both are food. The white risk is refermentation and unwanted malolactic conversion in bottle, showing as a hazy, gassy, buttery wine on shelf. So reds need pH-aware protection and phenolic vigilance; whites need absolute microbial removal.

## SO2: the numbers, stated correctly

Free SO2 at bottling should sit between 25 and 35 mg/L, equivalent to roughly 0.8 mg/L molecular; up to 40 mg/L is defensible for special cases such as bulk wines or dessert wines. Molecular SO2 is the antimicrobial fraction and it is a function of pH, so the free figure alone is meaningless without the pH: at pH 3.2 around 20 mg/L free gives close to 0.8 mg/L molecular, while at pH 3.6 it takes nearer 50 mg/L to reach the same protection. Total SO2 includes the bound fraction and is a legal and labelling number, not a preservation target — quoting total as the microbial defence is simply wrong. Acidification to lower pH is therefore often a better microbial lever than more SO2.

## Preparation for bottling: hygiene first

Prevention is the strategy; at this stage cure is largely impossible. Hygiene is the first rule and it applies to any winery regardless of size — the determinant is the risk profile of the equipment and the product, not the scale of the operation, and a small manual line with old gaskets is more dangerous than a large automated one. In the weeks before bottling: clean and sanitise tanks, hoses, pumps and the filler to a written procedure — a caustic wash, rinse, acid rinse and a hot water or peracetic sanitation — with dead legs, valves, gaskets and sample taps treated as the highest-risk points. Verify by ATP swabbing and rinse-water plating rather than by inspection. Keep tanks full and sealed, top up regularly, and plate every lot for viable yeast and bacteria so a problem is found in tank, where it can still be filtered out, rather than in bottle.

## Filtration

Filtration is the decisive removal step. A depth or cross-flow clarification stage, then a graded prefilter, then a 0.45 micron absolute membrane for a sterile bottling; 0.65 micron is used on reds where the tighter grade strips too much, but it is a compromise and only acceptable when the wine is dry and the sugar risk is nil. Integrity-test the membrane by bubble point before and after every run. Note that *Brettanomyces* will work on trace sugars of a few hundred milligrams per litre, so "dry" is not the same as "safe".

## Gas and temperature management

Oxygen feeds acetic acid bacteria and *Brettanomyces*. Inert every transfer, sparge the line, purge the bottle and the headspace, and target dissolved oxygen below about 1 mg/L at the filler. Bottle cool, and store the finished stock cool and stable rather than in a hot warehouse, since warmth accelerates any population that survived.

## Additives are the backstop, not the plan

DMDC, sorbic acid, lysozyme and chitosan exist and each has a legitimate narrow use, but reaching for them at the filler means the preventive system has already failed. I would treat them as remedial, not routine, and fix hygiene, filtration and pH instead.
