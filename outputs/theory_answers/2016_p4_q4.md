## What "strong" has to mean before it can be measured

A strong brand is one that can command a price premium over the category and be repurchased without being bought — that is, without discount, without trade loading and without paying for the distribution. Everything measurable follows from that definition, and I structure the answer around it: four families of metric (price, distribution and rate of sale, consumer behaviour, and financial), then the three I would trust above all the others, then what the numbers cannot tell you. I am answering with measurements, not with a list of brands I consider strong, because a name proves nothing without the data behind it.

## 1. Price metrics

The purest evidence of strength is price achieved without support.

- **Price index against the category** — average selling price divided by the category average in the same channel. A brand consistently above 100 is capturing a premium for its name.
- **Percentage of volume sold on deal, and average depth of discount.** This is the single most revealing number on a scan-data report. Two brands can show identical rate of sale, but the one selling 60 per cent of its volume at a promotional price has bought that volume; the one selling 15 per cent on deal owns it.
- **Price achieved as a percentage of recommended retail.**
- **Price elasticity** derived from promotional response. A strong brand loses less volume to a price rise and gains less from a cut — its buyers are not shopping the price.
- **Successive annual price increases taken without share loss.** This is the hardest test a brand can pass.

## 2. Distribution and rate of sale

- **Numeric and weighted distribution** (percentage of stores, and percentage of category sales those stores represent).
- **Rate of sale per point of distribution per week** — the metric that separates a brand that is *bought* from one that is merely *stocked*. Distribution can be purchased with listing fees; rate of sale cannot.
- **Share of category value against share of shelf space.** A brand delivering more of the value than the facings is earning its place.
- **Listings retained through range review**, and the number of markets showing sustained repeat orders rather than one-off fills.

## 3. Consumer behaviour

- **Spontaneous and prompted awareness**, tracked over time — spontaneous is the meaningful one.
- **Household penetration** and **repeat purchase rate**, measured by Nielsen or IRI scan data off-trade and by Kantar Worldpanel or an equivalent household panel.
- **Share of requirements** — of the category volume a buying household purchases, how much is yours.
- **Conversion through the funnel**: awareness to trial to repeat. Wide awareness with poor conversion indicates advertising, not a brand.
- **On-premise by-the-glass listings and reorder rate**, where the brand is chosen by a professional rather than a shopper.

## 4. Financial and market metrics

- **Gross margin per case against the category**, and contribution after advertising and promotion.
- **Marketing spend as a percentage of net sales required to hold share.** A strong brand needs less; a rising ratio at flat share is an early warning that the equity is being rented.
- **Transaction value.** What an acquirer pays is the market's own quantification of brand equity: Constellation Brands paid of the order of $315 million for Meiomi in 2015 for a brand carrying essentially no vineyards or winery — the money bought consumer demand and nothing else.
- For fine wine, the secondary market is an unusually clean instrument: **secondary price against release price, Liv-ex trade volume, bid-offer spread**, auction sell-through rate, and the ratio of allocation demand to allocation offered.

## The three I would weight most heavily

Rate of sale per point of distribution; price realisation with low promotional dependency; and repeat-purchase rate. Each is chosen because it is hard to fake. A brand can buy awareness, buy distribution and load the trade to inflate depletions, but it cannot make a consumer take it off the shelf at full price and then come back. Together these three answer the only question that matters: does demand exist independently of what we are spending to create it?

## Limits of the numbers

Two caveats keep the answer honest. First, coverage is uneven — scan data is good off-trade and poor on-trade, DTC and travel retail sit outside most panels, and emerging markets have thin data, so a brand can look weak where it is simply unmeasured. Second, these metrics are lagging. They confirm strength that already exists; the leading indicators (search behaviour, sommelier advocacy, unprompted mention) are the least quantifiable of all.

## Conclusion

The most quantifiable signs of a strong wine brand are a sustained price premium taken with little promotional support, a high rate of sale per point of distribution, and a high repeat-purchase rate — corroborated by penetration, by the marketing ratio needed to hold share, and ultimately by what a buyer will pay for the name alone.
