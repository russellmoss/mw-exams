## The criterion

I judge all three against a single standard: do they deliver fruit of full physiological ripeness, with balanced sugar, acid and pH, ripe seed and skin tannin, and intact aroma precursors, evenly across the block and reliably across vintages? Each is a way of manipulating the two currencies of a vineyard — light intercepted and water available — and each is worth most at the margin, where a site is otherwise struggling to ripen or struggling to stay fresh. I give the three equal space, as the question does.

## Aspect

Aspect is the orientation and inclination of the slope, and it works by changing the angle at which radiation strikes the ground. At high latitude it is decisive, because the sun is low: a south-facing slope in the northern hemisphere intercepts far more energy per unit area than flat land, and steepening the slope amplifies the effect. The Graacher Himmelreich on the Mosel is the clean illustration — a steep south to south-west facing slate slope at a latitude where Riesling would not ripen on the valley floor. The relationship between slope and sun exposure there is intimate and inseparable: the incline exists to buy the ripeness the latitude denies, and the dark slate re-radiating stored heat at night extends it further. The same logic explains the Côte d'Or's east-facing scarp, the Rangen de Thann and the Côte-Rôtie.

The corollaries matter as much as the rule. Slope drains cold air, so mid-slope sites sit above the frost pocket and below the exposed, wind-scoured crest — which is why the grand crus of Burgundy sit in the middle. Slope drains water too, which restrains vigour and reduces disease pressure but risks drought stress on thin soils, and it erodes. And in a hot region the sign flips: in the Douro, Priorat, Sicily or inland Australia, a north-facing or east-facing aspect is the quality aspect, because it moderates afternoon temperature, protects acidity and prevents the shutdown of anthocyanin synthesis and the sunburn that ruins skins. Aspect is therefore not "south-facing is better"; it is the tool by which a grower sets the site's heat budget to the variety's requirement, and its value is highest at the climatic extremes.

## Vine density

Density — vines per hectare, a function of row width and in-row spacing — governs how the site's resources are divided. High density, as in the Médoc, Burgundy and Champagne at around ten thousand vines per hectare, allocates a small volume of soil to each plant. On a poor, well-drained, low-vigour soil that is exactly right: each vine carries few bunches, competition restrains shoot growth so the canopy stays open and the fruit exposed, root systems are forced deep, and the small crop per vine ripens easily. The result is concentration and even ripening — but only where the soil is genuinely poor. Plant at that density on fertile alluvium and the vines compete for water, shut down in a dry summer, and the block collapses.

Low density with wide rows suits fertile or dry sites: a large volume of soil per vine supports a larger vine, whose leaf area can be spread over a divided canopy — Scott Henry, Smart-Dyson, the lyre — so that a substantial crop still ripens fully. The Australian and Californian inland blocks are built this way for water reasons, and the best New Zealand and Australian premium sites use divided canopies to reconcile vigour with exposure.

The quality logic is therefore vine balance, not density per se: density should be chosen so that each vine's leaf area matches the crop it carries on that particular soil. High density also raises establishment and farming cost sharply and dictates narrow tractors, so it must be justified by fruit value. Its secondary benefits — better wind resistance, quicker canopy closure, more uniform ripening across the row — are real but consequential.

## Row orientation

Orientation determines when in the day the fruiting zone is lit. North-south rows give balanced exposure to both faces, morning on one and afternoon on the other, and are the default in cool regions because total interception is maximised. East-west rows expose one face for most of the day and shade the other, which suits a hot region where the intention is to keep fruit out of afternoon radiation — the choice increasingly made in southern Spain, inland Australia and parts of Argentina. In a warming climate the specific problem is the west-facing side of a north-south row, which takes maximum radiation at the hottest hour: rotating the rows, or simply retaining leaves on that side, prevents sunburn, acid loss and shrivel.

Beyond sunlight, orientation must answer wind, and this is where good analysis separates itself. Rows aligned across a strong prevailing wind take shoot breakage, poor fruit set and stalled photosynthesis from closed stomata: the afternoon marine winds of the Salinas Valley, the mistral in the southern Rhône, the Cape Doctor in South Africa and the Zonda in Mendoza all dictate orientation more strongly than sun angle does. Aligning with the wind reduces damage but increases drying and cooling of the canopy — useful for disease control in a humid region, damaging where it stops ripening. Orientation also interacts with slope, since rows across the fall line control erosion but complicate machinery, and with density, since row spacing sets how much one row shades the next.

## Conclusion

None of the three is a quality factor on its own. Aspect sets the heat budget, density sets the resource share, and orientation sets the timing of exposure and the exposure to wind; together they decide whether the fruit ripens evenly, keeps its acid and finishes its tannins. They are settled at planting and are effectively permanent, which is why they deserve more analysis than they usually receive.
