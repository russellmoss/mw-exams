## Definitions and the commercial frame

By "inexpensive" I mean a shelf price under about £7 in the UK or US$8 in the United States, which after duty, VAT, packaging, freight and retail margin leaves of the order of 50p a litre for the wine — a figure that decides everything below. "Off-dry" I take in the EU and OIV sense of medium dry: sugar above 4 g/L and up to 12 g/L, or up to 18 g/L where total acidity is within 10 g/L of the sugar figure. In perception terms sweetness is detectable from around 5-6 g/L in a high-acid wine and much higher in a soft one, but the legal band is what governs labelling.

This is a technically demanding category, not a lesser one. The producer is making a microbiologically fragile wine, at very large volume, on a margin that permits no reworking, for a consumer who will notice inconsistency between bottles. The critical considerations follow from that.

## Fruit ripeness and intake

The style needs moderate ripeness, not high ripeness. Fruit picked at 11-12% potential alcohol gives a finished wine of 10.5-11.5% abv where residual sugar reads as balance rather than as weight, keeps duty low in banded markets, and retains the natural acidity the sugar has to be hung on. Aromatic varieties suit the style because sweetness carries aroma. Fruit health matters more than at any other price: machine harvesting at volume permits no sorting, and botrytis brings laccase, glucans that blind filters, and an oxidation risk the winery cannot afford to chase.

## Juice preparation and fermentation

Clarify fast — flotation rather than static settling, because tank turnaround is a real cost — with bentonite in juice to fix protein stability early. Ferment cool, at 13-16°C, on a selected aromatic yeast with adequate nitrogen, to protect thiols and esters and, critically, to give a predictable and reproducible ferment. Blend across parcels to a specification, since consistency of the finished sugar and acid is what the customer is buying.

## Concentrating sugar in the winery

Where more sugar is wanted than the fruit supplies, the options are reverse osmosis or vacuum evaporation of a portion of the juice, or cryoconcentration; all remove water and raise sugar, acid and any fault character together, and all carry a volume loss that at this price is hard to justify. Enrichment with rectified concentrated grape must raises alcohol, not residual sugar, and is a different decision. In practice the economics favour buying sweetness in as a separate stream rather than concentrating the whole batch.

## Stopping the ferment short

Arresting fermentation at the target sugar is the most direct route and the most demanding. The options are chilling the tank to 2-4°C to drop the yeast out, followed immediately by racking and centrifugation; sterile membrane filtration at 0.45 microns to remove the population; fermenting under pressure so accumulating CO2 inhibits the yeast, which is how low-alcohol lightly sweet styles are made; or a heavy SO2 addition, which works but leaves a bound-SO2 burden the wine will carry to the shelf. The risk is that arrest is never complete: viable cells and residual nutrient in the same tank is the definition of an unstable wine.

## Adding sweetness back

The lower-risk and more common route is to ferment to dryness — a clean, stable, low-nutrient base — and sweeten before bottling. The options are unfermented grape juice held sterile under refrigeration and SO2, in the German Süssreserve tradition; rectified concentrated grape must, which is neutral, storable and dosable to a specification; or blending in a separate sweet wine component. In the EU sweetening may only be done with grape-derived products; sucrose is not permitted for this purpose, and chaptalisation is a permitted alcohol adjustment, not a sweetening one. This route lets one dry base wine be split into several sugar specifications for different markets, which is a decisive commercial advantage at scale.

## Hygiene, which is not negotiable

A wine with fermentable sugar, modest alcohol and a warm supply chain will referment if a single viable cell reaches it. That demands full CIP on every tank, line and valve — caustic wash, rinse, acid, sanitiser such as peracetic acid — sanitary fittings with no dead legs, replacement of perished gaskets, and swab or ATP verification rather than assumption. The filler, the filler bowl and the capper are the highest-risk points in the plant because they sit downstream of the last filter. Hygiene here is a bottling-line discipline as much as a cellar one.

## Preventing refermentation in bottle

Belt and braces, and all three levers are used together. Sorbic acid inhibits yeast but not bacteria and is capped at 200 mg/L and used at 150-200 mg/L; it must be accompanied by SO2, or lactic bacteria metabolise it to the geranium taint. SO2 is dosed to a molecular SO2 target of 0.8 mg/L, which at pH 3.2 needs roughly 25-30 mg/L free — higher than a dry wine of the same pH would need. Dimethyl dicarbonate, capped at 200 mg/L in the EU, kills yeast on contact at the filler and leaves no residue, but has no persistence, so it only secures a wine that is already close to sterile. Sterile filtration into an aseptic filler is the most reliable of all and increasingly the industry standard.

Finish with a low-oxygen-transmission screwcap and a warehouse specification, because the last critical consideration is that this wine must survive a shipping container, not a cellar.
