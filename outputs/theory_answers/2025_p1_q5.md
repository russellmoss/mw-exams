## Scope: what is actually changing

I set temperature aside deliberately: this is not a general warming question, and rainfall and wind behave differently from temperature because they are far less predictable and far less well modelled. Three things are changing about rainfall — the **annual total** in some regions, more importantly its **seasonal distribution** (winter recharge versus growing-season rain), and most importantly its **intensity**, with the same total arriving in fewer, heavier events separated by longer dry spells. Wind is changing in prevailing direction and mean speed, in gustiness and the frequency of severe events, and in the strength of the nocturnal inversions on which frost protection depends. I take rainfall through the season from dormancy to harvest, then wind, then the equipment and trellis consequences, and I treat both sides — some of these changes are useful.

## Rainfall, by timing in the season

**Dormancy and winter.** Reduced winter recharge is the change with the longest reach, because it sets both soil moisture at budburst and the year's irrigation allocation. The Western Cape drought of roughly 2016 to 2018 cut Cape harvests and forced allocation restrictions; central Chile has been in drought for well over a decade; in Australia's Murray–Darling and California's Central Valley, water price and groundwater regulation now shape planting decisions more than any vineyard practice. Where winter rain increases instead, the effect is the opposite and welcome — a full profile is the cheapest irrigation there is.

**Budburst and spring.** Wet springs delay every operation that needs a tractor, so pruning finishes late and the first sprays are missed. Wet soils, however, buffer radiative frost — a real, under-acknowledged benefit — while a dry spring raises frost risk on the same night. Heavier convective activity brings hail, and repeated hail years in Burgundy in the 2010s showed how a single afternoon removes a crop.

**Flowering.** This is the point of maximum yield sensitivity. Rain, cold and strong wind during flowering disrupt pollination and cause coulure and millerandage, and the yield lost then cannot be recovered. Wind at flowering can equally help, by drying the canopy and reducing infection.

**Set to veraison.** The heavy-event pattern is at its most damaging here. Successive wet periods drive downy mildew epidemics — the 2018 downy mildew year in Bordeaux is the reference case — and heavy rain on slopes erodes topsoil irreversibly, leaches nitrogen and waterlogs roots, causing the transient stress that shows up later as uneven ripening.

**Veraison to harvest.** Rain now costs quality directly: berry splitting, dilution, botrytis and sour rot, and the pressure to pick before phenolic ripeness. Wet La Niña harvests in eastern Australia have repeatedly produced this pattern. The compensating change is that longer dry spells between events mean more regions can now finish the season cleanly than could a generation ago.

## Wind

Wind is the more genuinely two-sided of the pair.

**Where it helps.** Reliable afternoon wind dries the canopy and suppresses fungal disease, which is why the south-easter in the Cape and the maritime afternoon wind on the Chilean and Californian coasts underpin low-spray viticulture; the mistral does the same for the southern Rhône. Wind also cools, moderating heat spikes, and by inducing partial stomatal closure it slows sugar accumulation — useful where ripening has become too fast.

**Where it harms.** The same mechanism becomes a cost when it goes further: sustained wind closes stomata for long enough to arrest photosynthesis, delaying ripening and reducing sugar, and it raises evapotranspiration precisely when water is scarce. Spring gales snap young shoots; persistent wind abrades leaves and scars berries; strong wind at flowering compounds the set problem above. Operationally, wind removes spraying hours — the narrowing of the window between wet soils and windy afternoons is a genuine and often overlooked change — and creates drift and buffer-zone liability. Most seriously, wind drives fire and transports smoke: smoke carried far from the fire front in the Australian 2019-20 season and in California in 2020 tainted fruit in vineyards that never saw flame.

## Trellis, equipment and capital

These changes are expensive because they are absorbed as infrastructure rather than practice. Wind pressure on a full canopy means heavier and deeper-set end assemblies, stronger anchoring, more catch wires, and lower cordons on exposed sites; shelter belts are planted for exactly this in Marlborough and Mendoza, at the cost of land, water and a frost-trapping still zone behind them. Rainfall change is answered by drainage, contour work and permanent cover to hold slopes, by storage dams and a shift from overhead to drip, by hail netting, and by lighter or tracked machinery that can work wet soils without compacting them. Sprayers get larger and faster to fit shorter weather windows. Wind machines deserve a specific note: they depend on a temperature inversion, and where inversions are weakening or wind at dawn is more common, an expensive installed asset is losing effectiveness.

## Conclusion

Changing rainfall affects viticulture chiefly through timing and intensity rather than annual total, and it is the more serious of the two, because it strikes both yield — at flowering and through hail and flood — and fruit health at harvest, in the same season and often in the same week. Wind is more local and more evenly balanced: it remains a genuine asset for disease control and heat moderation in the regions built around it, and a growing liability everywhere through spray windows, fire and infrastructure load. Both push viticulture towards resilience investment — drainage, storage, netting, stronger trellis, insurance — and the significant secondary effect is distributional: those investments are affordable to large and well-capitalised growers, and are pricing smaller ones out of marginal sites.
