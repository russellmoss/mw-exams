## Scoping the decision

Picking date is not a single judgement about ripeness but a compromise between four competing pressures: the composition the winemaker wants, the risk the weather imposes, the disease already present in the fruit, and the capacity of the labour force and the winery to take the crop. I discuss the four in that order, since the target must be set before the constraints can be traded against it.

## 1. Which ripeness? The competing standards

**Sugar** is the oldest measure and the easiest — Brix, Baume or Oechsle, converted to potential alcohol — and it remains the legal measure in much of Europe, where appellations set minimum must weights and German Prädikat levels are defined by minimum must weight in degrees Oechsle. Its weakness is that it tracks accumulation and dehydration together: in a hot September, a rising Brix reading can mean shrivel rather than ripening.

**Acidity** is two measurements with different meanings. Titratable acidity tracks the loss of malic acid to respiration and falls fastest in warm weather; pH rises with potassium uptake and matters more operationally, because a high-pH must is microbially unstable, binds less free sulphur dioxide and gives poorer colour in reds, so in warm regions it commonly becomes the limiting factor before sugar does.

**Phenolic or physiological maturity** — seed colour and tannin, skin tannin polymerisation, anthocyanin accumulation, ease of berry detachment, loss of green herbaceous character — is what most red winemakers actually pick on, assessed by tasting berries in the block rather than by analysis. The core problem is that in warm climates sugar runs ahead of tannin ripeness, so picking on phenolics gives high alcohol; in marginal climates the reverse.

**Aromatic maturity** is variety-specific and often peaks and then declines. Methoxypyrazines in Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet degrade with sun exposure and time; terpenes in Muscat, Riesling and Gewurztraminer accumulate and then fall; thiol precursors respond to canopy exposure. Picking Riesling in Alsace or the Mosel is a judgement about where aromatic intensity sits against acidity, not about sugar.

## 2. Style sets the target

The intended wine determines which standard governs. Traditional-method sparkling base is picked at low ripeness and high acidity — Champagne base wine is generally picked at roughly 9-10.5 per cent potential alcohol, and Tasmania and English growers work to a similar brief — often several weeks before still wine from the same fruit would be picked. Sherry base from Palomino in Jerez is picked at moderate ripeness with the knowledge that fortification will supply the alcohol. Botrytised sweet wines invert the exercise entirely: Sauternes and Tokaji are harvested in successive tries over weeks, selecting for infection rather than for a date. Eiswein and icewine must be picked with the fruit frozen on the vine, in Germany and Canada at around -7 to -8C, which can mean waiting until midwinter and accepting the loss of the crop to birds and rot. Amarone requires intact, thick-skinned, disease-free bunches that will survive months of appassimento, so growers in Valpolicella pick a little early and select rigorously. Fortified Douro reds are picked for extractable colour and tannin, since fermentation will be arrested early.

## 3. Weather and disease: the risk side

Once the target is set, the weather decides whether it can be reached. An approaching front is the commonest reason a producer picks earlier than planned: rain dilutes, splits berries and triggers botrytis and sour rot within days, and thin-skinned tight-clustered varieties such as Pinot Noir, Chenin Blanc and Zinfandel are least able to wait. In Bordeaux and Burgundy the autumn rain window frequently forces the decision; on the US east coast the risk is tropical storms; in Central Otago and Ontario an early autumn frost can end the season. A heat spike does the same in reverse, forcing a pick before shrivel concentrates sugar and strips acid.

Existing disease pressure removes the option to wait at all. A block already carrying botrytis or downy mildew defoliation must come in, because both the crop and the quality deteriorate faster than the fruit ripens. This is where the harvest decision most often diverges block by block rather than estate-wide.

## 4. Labour, machinery and capacity

The most underrated constraints are logistical. Hand-harvesting requires a crew assembled and housed on a date fixed weeks earlier, and seasonal labour is now scarce and expensive in most producing countries; a contract machine harvester is shared across many growers and booked in advance. Warm regions harvest at night to bring fruit in cold, which halves the effective daily window and requires lit machinery and reception staff.

Winery capacity is the hard ceiling. A producer cannot pick faster than the press and the tanks allow: whole-bunch pressing for sparkling is slow, so Champagne's dispersed pressing centres exist precisely to spread that load, and any estate with a single press must stagger picking across days it might prefer to compress. Tank space, fermentation temperature control, chilling capacity and even barrel availability all set a maximum daily intake, and a co-operative member picks to a delivery slot he does not set.

## Conclusion

The major factors are, in order of authority: the wine style, which sets the compositional target; the several and often conflicting definitions of ripeness, among which the winemaker must choose one to govern; the weather and disease pressure, which decide how much of the intended waiting is actually available; and the labour and winery capacity, which convert an ideal date into a feasible sequence. The best growers pick block by block on tasted phenolic and aromatic maturity, using analysis to confirm rather than to decide, and hold enough reserve capacity to act on a forecast.
