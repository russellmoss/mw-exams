### Back-to-back F1 flights — breadth logic
When P1 runs two single-variety flights in the same paper, the design logic favours breadth across the white canon over repetition — the same variety twice in one paper would waste half the paper's identification marks re-testing ground already covered. Q1 (wines 1-6) resolved to Chardonnay; Q2 drawing a different variety (Riesling) confirms this breadth principle rather than contradicting it, matching the pattern seen in 2015 (Q2 Chenin Blanc / Q3 six-wine spread ending in aromatic varieties) and 2025 (Q2 Chardonnay / Q3 three different varieties from one country).

### Riesling quality-hierarchy + maturity fit
Riesling is uniquely suited to a "quality in context of origin" + "maturity" double-ask: it has an unusually legible internal quality hierarchy in every major producing country (Kabinett/Spätlese/GG in Germany, Smaragd/Federspiel in Austria, single-vineyard Clare/Eden Valley bottlings in Australia, Grand Cru/lieu-dit in Alsace) AND ages with unmistakable, easy-to-articulate markers — deepening gold, softening perceived acidity, and eventually petrol/kerosene development. Chardonnay would have been the equally plausible alternate on both grounds, but was the more probable choice for the larger Q1 flight, leaving Riesling as the natural Q2 variety.

### Deliberate maturity spread
The three wines span a textbook 9/6/2-year age gradient at the point of tasting (2026 sitting): Trimbach Cuvée Frédéric Emile 2017 (9 years, Alsace) is oldest and structurally built for decades of ageing; Domäne Wachau Federspiel Bruck 2020 (6 years, Austria) sits mid-maturity; Pewsey Vale 2024 (2 years, Australia) is youngest and most overtly primary. All three are dry (RS 1.6-2.4 g/L or unpublished-but-dry), removing sweetness as a confounding variable and isolating maturity and quality-in-region as the pure variables under test.
