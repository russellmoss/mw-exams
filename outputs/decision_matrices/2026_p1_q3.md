# Decision matrix — 2026 Paper 1 Question 3

## Question (verbatim)
```
Wines 10-12 are wines from regions influenced by the Mediterranean Sea.

For each wine:

a) Identify the primary grape variety and origin as closely as possible. (3 x 10 marks)

b) Discuss how winemaking has impacted the freshness of the wine. (3 x 10 marks)

c) Comment on the commercial position. (3 x 5 marks)
```

## Paper context
Paper 1 = white still wines. Flight of 3 (wines 10–12), the last flight of the paper — historically the IMW uses the closing flight of P1 to test breadth across lesser-known/indigenous varieties rather than the "big five" internationals that anchor earlier flights (see 2025 P1 Q4 pattern: "grab bag… should definitely be able to identify [the obvious one]… the third may be lesser able to ID"). 75 marks total, evenly weighted a)/b) at 30 each, c) lighter at 15 — origin ID and the freshness/winemaking narrative are the examiner's real interest; commercial position is a shorter add-on.

## Stem signals
- "**regions influenced by the Mediterranean Sea**" — a literal geographic framing, not the looser "Mediterranean climate" language the IMW uses when it wants to include California/Chile/South Africa. This phrasing is deliberately chosen and (per corpus check) does not recur verbatim in any prior year — it is a fresh framing for 2026, so there's no direct precedent to pattern-match against, only adjacent flights (2025 P1 Q4's "important white wine styles across three European countries").
- Reading backed: **literal sea, not climate analogue.** If the examiner meant "warm/dry climate style" they would have written "Mediterranean-climate regions," which is the standard term used to sweep in the New World. Restricting to "influenced by the Mediterranean Sea" points to actual coastal/near-coastal appellations of the Mediterranean basin: southern France, Italy (incl. islands), Spain's east coast, Greece, Croatia/Slovenia, Cyprus, Malta, Lebanon/Israel, North Africa, Turkey's Aegean coast.
- "**primary grape variety**" (not just "grape variety") — deliberate wording implying at least one wine is a **blend** where a dominant component needs to be named, or a field blend/co-fermented indigenous wine.
- "**origin as closely as possible**" — reward for drilling to sub-region/DOC/PDO, not just country.
- b) asks specifically how **winemaking** (not climate alone) preserved **freshness** — this presumes the base material (Mediterranean sun, warm nights) works against acid retention, so the examiner wants technique: early/night harvest, whole-bunch pressing, reductive handling, low/no malo, cool ferment, minimal skin contact, inert vessel, screwcap or early bottling. This is the technical core of the question.
- c) commercial position at only 5 marks/wine — likely testing recognition that indigenous Mediterranean whites span from cheap-and-cheerful bar wines to prestige, volcanic-terroir icons.

## Universe
Given P1 (white, still) + literal Mediterranean Sea framing:
**In-universe**: Italy (Liguria, Sardinia, Sicily, Campania, Puglia, Tuscan coast, Marche via Adriatic), Southern France (Languedoc, Provence, Corsica, southern Rhône), Spain (Catalonia/Penedès, Valencia, Balearics — NOT Atlantic Galicia/Rías Baixas), Greece (Santorini/Cyclades, Peloponnese near-coast, Macedonia), Croatia/Slovenia (Istria, Dalmatia), Cyprus, Lebanon (Bekaa Valley, marketed as Mediterranean-climate-adjacent despite inland altitude), Malta, and more marginal Turkey/North Africa/Israel.
**Out-of-universe** (ruled out by the literal reading): Rías Baixas Albariño (Atlantic), Vinho Verde (Atlantic), Loire, Alsace, Germany/Austria, Marlborough/Central Otago, California/Chile/South Africa (Mediterranean *climate* but not *sea*) — all should be treated as decoys the stem is specifically steering candidates away from.

## Candidate varieties (confidence tiers)
- **Vermentino / Rolle**: STRONG SIGNAL — the single most widely planted "Mediterranean Sea" white, spanning Liguria, Sardinia, Tuscany, Corsica and Provence; textbook for a and b (naturally high acid retained via early picking, reductive stainless steel).
- **Assyrtiko**: STRONG SIGNAL — the poster child for "how winemaking/terroir preserves freshness in a hot Mediterranean site" (Santorini volcanic soils, kouloura training, naturally searing acidity despite heat); very likely to anchor one slot given how directly it answers part b).
- **Fiano / Falanghina / Greco (Campania)**: PLAUSIBLE — genuine Mediterranean coastal Italy, good origin-ID test (Fiano di Avellino vs. coastal Falanghina).
- **Grillo / Catarratto / Carricante (Sicily/Etna)**: PLAUSIBLE — Sicily is unambiguously Mediterranean, Etna Bianco (Carricante) offers a strong "volcanic freshness" story parallel to Assyrtiko; examiner may prefer only one volcanic-freshness wine, so this could be traded off against Santorini.
- **Southern Rhône/Provence white blend (Grenache Blanc, Roussanne, Marsanne, Clairette, Bourboulenc, Vermentino)** or **Picpoul de Pinet**: PLAUSIBLE — directly satisfies "primary grape variety" (a blend with a dominant component) and is textbook Mediterranean France.
- **Château Musar White (Obaideh/Merwah, Bekaa Valley, Lebanon)**: CURVEBALL — indigenous field blend, oxidative/skin-contact winemaking that actually works *against* the "freshness" question (an interesting trap: candidate must recognize this wine is deliberately NOT fresh, testing whether they can argue the opposite case in part b).
- **Istrian Malvazija (Croatia) or Xynisteri (Cyprus)**: CURVEBALL — genuinely Mediterranean, rarely tested, would be the "IMW breadth trap" third wine.

- **Wine 11**: most likely Greece — Assyrtiko, Santorini or Peloponnese. STRONG SIGNAL — best fit for the "how winemaking preserved freshness in a hot climate" prompt.

## Rule-outs
- Albariño/Rías Baixas and Vinho Verde: geographically Atlantic, not Mediterranean — a classic decoy the literal stem wording is designed to filter out.
- Riesling, Grüner Veltliner, Alsace whites: continental/cool-climate, wrong sea entirely.
- Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc, California Chardonnay, South African Chenin: "Mediterranean climate" analogues that fail the literal "Mediterranean Sea" test — including one of these would be a trap for candidates who default to climate-type reasoning rather than reading the stem precisely.
- Full-blown Sauternes/German sweet styles: wrong question family (this is about freshness/table wine, not botrytis/dessert wines) and no signal of residual sugar in the stem.

## The IMW's likely intent
This tests (1) precise geographic reasoning from an unusual stem — rewarding candidates who read "Mediterranean Sea" literally and resist the pull toward generic warm-climate answers; (2) origin ID to sub-region/DOC/PDO level across an underrepresented but coherent cluster of white regions; (3) technical winemaking vocabulary specific to acid preservation in warm coastal viticulture (harvest timing, reductive handling, malo suppression, inert vessels); and (4) commercial fluency across a spread from everyday coastal whites to prestige volcanic bottlings — testing whether the candidate can place niche Mediterranean whites at realistic price points rather than defaulting to "premium because unusual."

## What to focus on in the glass
- **Salinity / minerality and pithy citrus** → Vermentino, Assyrtiko, coastal Italian whites generally.
- **High, searing acidity despite evident ripeness/alcohol** → hot-climate variety with deliberately early picking or volcanic-soil freshness (Santorini, Etna) — this is the direct evidence for part b).
- **Reductive, flinty, non-oaked, stainless-steel character** vs **any oxidative, nutty, amber tint** → distinguishes the "preserved freshness" wines from a possible Musar-style trap wine that is intentionally NOT fresh.
- **Texture/weight**: fuller, phenolic, skin-contact grip may signal Sicilian/Grillo or a field-blend indigenous wine handled with some skin maceration.
- **Herbal/garrigue, fennel, white pepper notes** → southern Rhône/Provence blend components (Grenache Blanc, Clairette, Bourboulenc).
- Confirm "primary grape variety" wording by checking for a blend signature (layered aromatics that don't resolve to one clean varietal marker) versus a clean single-variety wine.
