# Decision matrix — 2026 Paper 2 Question 3

## Question (verbatim)
```
Wines 7-8, 9-10, and 11-12 form three pairs. Within each pair, the wines are from the same country and region.
Each pair is from a different country.

For each pair:

a) Identify the origin as closely as possible. (3 x 20 marks)

b) Compare the style and quality in the context of the region of origin. (3 x 20 marks)

c) Compare the maturity of the two wines, considering the likely vintage. (3 x 10 marks)
```

## Paper context
Paper 2 = red still wines only. This question is the centrepiece of the paper: 150 of 300 marks, half the flight, six of the twelve wines.

## Stem signals
- Wines run as **three fixed pairs** (7-8 / 9-10 / 11-12), not a loose flight of six
- Within a pair: **same country AND same region** — the two wines share an origin exactly
- Across pairs: **three different countries** — full geographic spread is guaranteed
- **No explicit grape-variety sub-question.** Origin (a) is worth as much as style/quality (b) — 20 marks each — which strongly implies the examiners expect variety to be self-evident from (or folded into) a correctly identified region, i.e. classic "region = variety" territory
- (b) explicitly frames the task as **compare, in context of origin** — the skill being tested is discriminating two wines that share terroir, not distinguishing terroir itself
- (c) says "considering the **likely vintage**" — a strong signal that at least some pairs differ in vintage, and the candidate is expected to reason about which vintage is which from style/maturity cues, not just describe development

## Universe
Given P2 + "same country and region" pairs across three different countries, the universe narrows to red regions that are (1) famous enough to source reliably, (2) large/diverse enough to yield two genuinely different but still-recognizable bottlings (quality tier, sub-zone, or vintage), and (3) distinctive enough that "region = variety" holds without a separate variety question. That excludes obscure single-producer appellations and points at the classic global benchmarks: Bordeaux, Burgundy, Rhône (France); Piedmont, Tuscany (Italy); Rioja, Ribera del Duero, Priorat (Spain); Douro (Portugal); Napa/Sonoma (USA); Barossa, McLaren Vale, Coonawarra (Australia); Central Otago/Martinborough (NZ); Stellenbosch (South Africa); Maipo/Colchagua (Chile); Mendoza (Argentina).

## Rule-outs
- Unlikely to repeat a country/style already implied elsewhere in the paper (P2 Q1/Q2 likely already cover single-variety and blend/quality territory) — the IMW rarely stacks three Old World pairs; expect at least one New World anchor
- Unlikely to be a minor or single-producer appellation (e.g. Priorat, a tiny Loire red AOC) — sourcing two contrastable examples reliably at this mark weight (20+20+10 per pair) favors regions with deep bench strength
- Unlikely South Africa or Argentina to headline a pair here — historically they appear more as "curveball" single wines in mixed flights (per corpus pattern) than as a full same-region pair, though not impossible
- Unlikely a blend-driven GSM/Rhône pairing to be the "no variety sub-question" pair unless it's clearly Châteauneuf-du-Pape (identifiable by region alone) rather than a generic Rhône wine

## The IMW's likely intent
This question deliberately removes variety ID as an independent hurdle (no separate marks for it) so it can spend maximum marks (40 of 50 per pair) on the harder skill: **discriminating quality, style and maturity between two wines from the identical origin.** That is a sophisticated, examiner-favorite test — it punishes candidates who can name a region but can't articulate *why* two wines from that same region differ (producer ambition, vineyard/cru hierarchy, oak regime, vintage). The vintage clause in (c) additionally tests whether candidates can read development/structure cues to infer which vintage is riper/more advanced, not just describe "young vs. old."

## What to focus on in the glass
- **Within-pair confirmation first:** do the two wines in a pair genuinely share terroir signature (soil-driven minerality, regional tannin structure, oak style) despite differing in weight/development? If yes, origin call is likely correct
- **Structural cues for quality tier:** concentration, oak integration/newness, ripeness of tannin, length — the classic quality ladder within a single region (e.g. village vs. cru Burgundy/Barolo, Crianza vs. Gran Reserva Rioja, classified vs. generic Bordeaux)
- **Maturity/vintage cues:** color rim development (garnet/brick), tertiary aromas (leather, dried fruit, forest floor) vs. primary/secondary fruit and oak spice, acid and tannin resolution — use these to place each wine's likely vintage, then reconcile with the region's known vintage variation
- **Cross-pair spread:** once each pair's country is fixed, sanity-check the three together represent a genuinely different climate/style each (e.g. don't call all three "cool-climate elegant" — the stem guarantees geographic diversity)
