# Decision matrix — 2026 Paper 1 Question 2

## Question (verbatim)
```
Wines 7-9 are from the same single variety and come from three different countries.

With reference to all three wines:

a) Identify the grape variety. (15 marks)

For each wine:

b) Discuss the quality in the context of its region of origin. (3 x 15 marks)

c) Comment on the wine's maturity. (3 x 5 marks)
```

## Paper context
Paper 1 = white still wines. This is the **second** single-variety flight in the paper (Q1, wines 1–6, is also single-variety). That structural fact matters: the examiners are deliberately running two back-to-back "name the grape" flights in one paper, which means they are almost certainly using **two different varieties** to test breadth across the white canon rather than depth on one. Precedent for this exact shape exists — 2025 P1 ran Q2 as a 4-wine single-variety flight (Chardonnay) immediately followed by Q3, a 3-wine flight from one country but three *different* varieties. 2026 inverts that: Q2 here is one variety across three *countries*. The closest structural precedent is 2015 P1 Q2 (5–6, "same variety but different countries," resolved to Chenin Blanc/SA+France) and 2015 P1 Q3 (7–12, same variety across six wines/countries, resolved to a spread from Riesling to Gewürztraminer/Viognier by aromatic intensity).

## Stem signals
- Three wines, **one single grape variety**, **three different countries** — an explicit origin-discrimination test, not a variety-ID-only test
- (a) is worth 15 marks total for variety — a single flat mark for correct ID across the flight, not per-wine
- (b) is heavily weighted: 15 marks **per wine** (45 of 75 total) for "quality in the context of its region of origin" — this is the heart of the question. It demands appellation/quality-hierarchy fluency (e.g., where does this wine sit within its own region's pecking order), not just "this is good/bad"
- (c) asks specifically about **maturity**, 5 marks per wine (15 total) — implies a deliberate vintage spread designed to be tasted and articulated, and a variety whose aging markers are legible (color, aromatic evolution, textural softening)
- No mention of blends, oak, or sweetness level — a straightforward dry-style, single-variety, single-style flight is the default read

## Universe
Same logic as the user's own recurring heuristic (2015 P1 Q2, 2015 P1 Q3, 2025 P1 Q2): when the stem says "same single variety, different countries" in Paper 1, the IMW reaches for one of the **handful of white varieties grown at serious quality in three or more countries simultaneously** — because you need three countries that each have a genuinely defensible, examinable "region of origin" quality story. That shortlist is narrow: Chardonnay, Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Gris/Grigio, Chenin Blanc, and (less often) Semillon or Muscat.

Because Q1 (wines 1–6) already burns one of these varieties on a single-country/single-style deep dive, Q2 is very likely drawing from the *remaining* pool of internationally-planted varieties — reinforcing rather than duplicating the paper's breadth.

## Candidate varieties (confidence tiers)
- **Riesling — STRONG SIGNAL.** The single best-suited variety for a "quality in context of origin" + "maturity" double-ask: it has an unusually legible quality hierarchy (Kabinett/Spätlese/GG in Germany, Smaragd/Federspiel in Austria, single-vineyard Clare/Eden Valley in Australia) AND ages with unmistakable, easy-to-articulate markers (petrol/kerosene, deepening gold, softening acid perception). Three-country spread is trivial to construct: Germany, Austria, Australia (or NZ/Alsace/US Finger Lakes as alternates).
- **Chardonnay — STRONG SIGNAL.** Equally plausible on variety grounds (Burgundy/California/Australia/Chile/South Africa all have distinct, examinable quality tiers) and ages well (oxidative, honeyed, nutty development). Slightly less likely here *only* because Chardonnay is the more probable choice for a 6-wine flight like Q1 (per 2025 P1 Q2 precedent), which would make it the "used" variety rather than the Q2 variety — but this is an inference, not a certainty, since Q1's actual variety is unknown to this analysis.
- **Sauvignon Blanc — PLAUSIBLE.** Loire, Marlborough, and South Africa (or Chile) give three legitimate, distinct countries with real quality tiers (Sancerre/Pouilly-Fumé vs. commercial Marlborough vs. old-vine Stellenbosch). Weaker on the maturity ask — most SB is drunk young, though top Loire and top Marlborough (e.g., barrel-aged, lees-aged) can show real development, so this remains viable but slightly less natural for a heavy maturity sub-question.
- **Chenin Blanc — PLAUSIBLE.** Ages beautifully and has real quality-hierarchy depth in both South Africa (old-vine Swartland/Stellenbosch) and the Loire (Vouvray/Savennières), but a genuine *third* country at serious quality (California, Australia) is a stretch — this is the user's own recurring caveat ("consider Chenin and Semillon every time... though not most voluminous").
- **Pinot Gris/Grigio — CURVEBALL.** Alsace, Italy (Alto Adige/Friuli), Oregon or New Zealand would supply three countries with real stylistic range (dense/sweet Alsace vs. lean commercial Italian vs. fruit-forward New World), but Pinot Gris is less classically used to test *maturity* — most bottlings are made for early consumption, weakening the fit with sub-question (c).
- **Semillon / Muscat — CURVEBALL.** Both have real multi-country credentials (Semillon: Australia/Bordeaux/South Africa; Muscat: France/Italy/Greece/Austria) but are less frequently the IMW's choice for a flat "identify the variety" 15-mark ask, since they're less commonly bottled as a widely-recognized international varietal trio at consistent quality.

## Rule-outs
- Unlikely to be an aromatic curveball like Gewürztraminer or Torrontés for this slot — Alsace so thoroughly dominates quality Gewürztraminer that constructing three *distinct, examinable* countries is hard, and 15 marks/wine on "quality in context of origin" needs real regional depth in all three places
- Unlikely to be Albariño or Grüner Veltliner as the flight variety — both are essentially single-country stories (Spain/Portugal for Albariño, Austria for Grüner) with only thin quality-tier presence elsewhere, which undercuts a genuine three-country design
- Unlikely to duplicate whatever variety anchors Q1 (wines 1–6) — the paper's design logic (breadth across two flights) argues against repetition, even though this cannot be verified without reading Q1's actual wines

## The IMW's likely intent
This question is testing **origin discrimination and quality-hierarchy fluency within one variety**, not variety ID (which is worth only 15 of 75 marks and applies to the whole flight). The heavy weighting on (b) — 45 marks total — signals the examiners want candidates to articulate *where each wine sits within its own region's internal quality ladder* (e.g., "this Mosel Riesling reads as GG-level dry, not simple Kabinett" or "this reads as commercial Marlborough SB, not a top Sancerre-rivaling cuvée"). The maturity sub-question (c) is a secondary but deliberate test of whether the candidate can read bottle age accurately — likely to catch out anyone who over- or under-estimates development, especially if one wine is deceptively youthful-looking or prematurely developed for its stated origin.

## What to focus on in the glass
- Aromatic signature first to lock variety: petrol/kerosene = aged Riesling; hazelnut/toast/butter from malo and oak = Chardonnay; blackcurrant leaf/gooseberry = Sauvignon Blanc; wet wool/honey = Chenin Blanc
- Acid structure and its integration — high, racy, unresolved acid vs. acid that has "knitted" into the wine over time is the primary maturity tell
- Colour depth and hue — pale green-gold to deep gold signals both variety (Riesling stays pale far longer than Chardonnay) and age
- For (b), immediately triage each wine into a quality tier within its region (entry-level / village-commercial / premier-cru-equivalent / grand-cru-equivalent) rather than describing it in the abstract — this is what the 15-mark weighting is rewarding
- Palate weight and texture — lees-derived breadth (Chardonnay, top Chenin) vs. linear/precise structure (Riesling, most SB) as a secondary variety confirmation
- Note any oak signature (vanilla, toast, spice) carefully — its presence or absence will help separate Chardonnay/Chenin candidates from Riesling/Sauvignon Blanc, which are far more rarely oaked at the top level (though Austrian and some Alsace/Australian Riesling and top Loire/Fumé-style Sauvignon can complicate this)
