# Decision matrix — 2026 Paper 2 Question 2

## Question (verbatim)
```
Wines 4-6 are from three different countries.

For each wine:

a) Identify the grape variety(ies) and origin as closely as possible. (3 x 10 marks)

b) Comment on the method of production. (3 x 5 marks)

c) Comment on the quality and commercial position. (3 x 10 marks)
```

## Paper context
Paper 2 = red still wines. This is the second of three questions in the paper. Q1 typically constrains by country (three varieties, one origin); Q3 typically constrains by region in pairs. Q2 sitting between them is deliberately the **open, breadth-testing flight** — the only hard constraint is "three different countries," so the examiner is free to range across the entire red wine world.

## Stem signals
- **Only constraint: three different countries.** No shared variety, no shared region, no stated relationship between the wines.
- **"grape variety(ies)"** — the parenthetical plural is a real signal (confirmed pattern from 2024 P2 Q2 annotation: "the term of variety/ies used will almost always indicate that one or more of these wines is a blend"). Expect at least one — possibly more — of the three wines to be a blend, not a varietal wine.
- **b) "method of production," 5 marks each, asked of every wine** — low weight per wine but structurally guaranteed, meaning the examiner expects each wine to yield a specific, nameable answer (oak type/regime, maceration technique, whole-bunch, carbonic, extended skin contact, appassimento, etc.), not just "aged in oak, went through malo."
- **c) "quality and commercial position," 10 marks each = 30 marks total** — tied with (a) as the largest component of the question. This is not just variety/origin ID; the examiner wants explicit judgment of where each wine sits in the market and why.
- No mention of vintage, maturity, or ageability — unusual for a P2 flight, suggesting the focus is squarely on identity + production + commercial read, not development.

## Universe
Given P2 (red, still) + "three different countries" + explicit allowance for blends, the universe is effectively the full red wine world, but structurally the IMW tends to build this exact slot (an open three-country flight with a production-method sub-question) from wines whose **production method is genuinely distinctive and teachable** — i.e., wines chosen because tasting for method (not just variety) is a fair, gradable exercise. The closest structural precedent in the corpus is **2015 P2 Q2** (also wines 4–6, also "three different countries," also a 5-mark "method of production" sub-question), which paired: Rioja Reserva (Spain, Tempranillo-dominant blend, American oak), Barossa Shiraz (Australia, single variety, new French/American oak, warm-climate ripeness), and Fleurie (France, Gamay, carbonic/semi-carbonic maceration). That triangulation — one wine with a signature oak regime, one clean New World varietal, one wine with a signature non-oak vinification technique — is a strong template for how this slot gets built.

## Candidate varieties (confidence tiers)
- **Tempranillo (Rioja or Ribera del Duero blend), Spain — STRONG SIGNAL.** American oak aging is the single most "nameable" production method in red wine and a gift for a 5-mark sub-question; the "(ies)" plural in the stem fits a Tempranillo/Garnacha/Graciano blend.
- **Gamay, Beaujolais (Cru), France — STRONG SIGNAL.** Carbonic or semi-carbonic maceration is equally nameable and instantly recognizable in the glass (banana/bubblegum ester, low tannin, bright purple color) — a classic "method of production" answer.
- **Nebbiolo, Barolo/Barbaresco, Italy — PLAUSIBLE.** Extended maceration and large old-oak (botte) aging is another textbook nameable method; high tannin/acid structure is distinctive enough to anchor a country slot.
- **Shiraz/Syrah or a GSM blend, Australia (Barossa/McLaren Vale) or South Africa — PLAUSIBLE.** Serves as the "clean, warm-climate, commercially legible" New World anchor; new oak regime is a fair method answer.
- **Malbec, Argentina (Mendoza/Uco Valley) — PLAUSIBLE.** Increasingly common New World anchor in recent papers; French oak, high-altitude fruit purity gives a clean method + commercial-position answer (strong retail visibility = easy commercial commentary).
- **Amarone della Valpolicella blend, Italy — CURVEBALL.** Appassimento (dried-grape) is the most distinctive production method available in red wine and would make (b) very gradable, but it competes with Nebbiolo/Sangiovese for the "Italy" slot and the high alcohol/residual richness could read as sweet if mistaken.
- **Douro red blend, Portugal — CURVEBALL.** Field-blend, foot-treading/lagares is a strong nameable method and would be a fresh choice not yet seen in the P2 corpus at this slot; would also test blend recognition per the "(ies)" signal.
- **Pinotage, South Africa — CURVEBALL.** Distinctive production (occasionally coffee/banana note is not "method" per se, but the variety itself is nameable) — less likely because it's a single narrow origin with no real methodological hook.

## Candidate origins per wine
- **Wine 4 — PLAUSIBLE: Spain (Rioja or Ribera), Tempranillo-based blend.** American oak aging = clean 5-mark method answer (dill, coconut, sweet spice vs French oak's finer tannin/toast).
- **Wine 5 — STRONG SIGNAL: a New World warm-climate single-varietal or blend (Australia Shiraz, Argentina Malbec, or Chile Cabernet/Carmenère).** Purity of fruit, higher alcohol, softer tannin, new oak — the "commercial, recognizable, well-priced" anchor that lets candidates score cleanly on (c).
- **Wine 6 — PLAUSIBLE: France, either Beaujolais Gamay (carbonic) or a lighter Loire red (Cabernet Franc, Chinon/Bourgueil, potentially minimal oak/steel) — or alternatively Italy Nebbiolo/Sangiovese.** Whichever variety fills this slot, expect it to carry the flight's most "textbook" nameable method (carbonic maceration, extended maceration, or appassimento), since one of the three wines needs to anchor sub-question (b) with an unambiguous answer.

## Rule-outs
- **Very obscure/curveball origins (Georgia, Lebanon, Greece, Uruguay) — unlikely as the primary read**, though not impossible as a single wildcard. The 30-mark weighting on quality/commercial position argues for wines candidates can plausibly place in the market, which favors well-known origins over genuine curveballs.
- **All three wines as straightforward single-country-style Cabernet/Merlot varietals** is unlikely — the explicit "variety(ies)" plural argues against a flight where every wine is a simple varietal wine.
- **A flight built entirely from cool-climate/light reds (e.g., three different Pinot Noir origins)** is inconsistent with the stem — Pinot Noir across three countries would typically be flagged as "same variety," which isn't the constraint given here (the stem states three countries, not three varieties, and does not mention a shared variety at all).
- **Fortified or off-dry reds** are out of scope — Paper 2 is still, dry red wine.

## The IMW's likely intent
This question tests **production-method literacy across styles**, not just variety/origin recall — the 5-mark-per-wine (b) sub-question guarantees every wine must yield a nameable, gradable technique (oak origin/regime, maceration style, whole-bunch/carbonic influence). Combined with the 30-mark weighting on (c), the deeper skill being tested is **connecting production choice to market position**: can the candidate explain not just what was done in the winery, but why it results in the wine's price point and commercial appeal? This is a "breadth and market fluency" question, deliberately placed between a narrower country-constrained Q1 and a narrower region-paired Q3.

## What to focus on in the glass
- **Oak signature first**: American oak (dill, coconut, vanilla, broader grain) vs French oak (fine, cedary, integrated) vs neutral/large-format oak (subtle, textural only, no overt oak flavor) — this alone should resolve most of sub-question (b).
- **Tannin texture and color**: bright, low-tannin, confected fruit with a whole-berry/banana lift = carbonic or semi-carbonic maceration (Gamay). Firm, high, fine-grained tannin with tar/rose = extended maceration (Nebbiolo). Soft, plush, riper tannin = warm-climate New World vinification.
- **Alcohol and ripeness level**: separates Old World structural wines (12.5–14%) from New World fruit-forward wines (14–15%+) and flags any appassimento-style concentration.
- **Fruit purity vs savoury/rustic complexity**: purity of primary fruit points New World or modern winemaking; savoury, earthy, slightly reductive or rustic notes point Old World/traditional.
- **Structure-to-price read for (c)**: is the tannin/oak/concentration proportionate to what you'd expect at a commercial (£10–15) price point, or does the wine show the extra layering (dry extract, oak integration, length) that signals a premium/fine-wine tier? Be ready to name a specific commercial bracket and channel (supermarket, restaurant list, fine-wine merchant) for each wine.
