# Decision matrix — 2026 Paper 3 Question 1

## Question (verbatim)
```
Wines 1-4 are sparkling wines and come from four different countries.

For each wine:

a) Identify the origin as closely as possible. (4 x 10 marks)

b) Comment on the quality within the context of the region of origin. (4 x 8 marks)

c) Identify the key winemaking decisions that have affected the wine's style. (4 x 7 marks)
```
100 marks total (10+8+7 = 25 per wine x 4).

## Paper context
Paper 3 = mixed/special styles (sparkling, fortified, sweet, rosé, oxidative). This question is a full sparkling-only flight opening the paper — the whole first block of 4 wines (of 12) is dedicated to bubbles. That is a larger sparkling allocation than 2024 (P3 Q1 was a 2-wine Champagne-vs-not pairing) but consistent with the paper generally opening on the most "expected" style before moving to rarer categories (fortified, botrytis/ice wine, oxidative) later in the paper.

## Stem signals
- "Sparkling wines" — style is given, no ambiguity on primary category.
- "Four different countries" — the defining constraint. Not paired comparisons (unlike 2024 P3 Q1); each wine stands alone against its own regional context.
- a) origin "as closely as possible" carries the **most marks per wine (10)** — country → region → possibly appellation/sub-zone is the priority skill.
- b) quality **"within the context of the region of origin"** (8 marks) — explicitly NOT asking to rank the four wines against each other or against Champagne as an absolute benchmark. It wants the candidate to judge each wine against what "good" looks like *for that place* — a recurring IMW instruction seen in 2015 P3 Q2 (fortified quality/maturity) and 2024 P3 Q3 (commercial headwinds framing).
- c) "key winemaking decisions that have affected style" (7 marks) — this is a production-method question, not just an origin question. Four different countries strongly implies four *different production methods or stylistic drivers* (traditional method vs tank/Charmat vs transfer vs ancestral vs carbonation; base blend; dosage; lees aging; vintage vs NV).

## Universe
Given P3 + "sparkling, four countries" → the universe is the full set of commercially significant sparkling wine origins: France (Champagne or Crémant), Italy (Prosecco, Franciacorta, Asti, Lambrusco), Spain (Cava, Corpinnat), Germany (Sekt), England, Australia (Tasmania, Yarra, Adelaide Hills), New Zealand, South Africa (Cap Classique), USA (California traditional method), and outliers (Austria Sekt, Argentina).

## Candidate varieties/methods (confidence tiers)
- **Traditional method** (Champagne, England, Franciacorta, Cava, Cap Classique, Tasmania): **STRONG SIGNAL** — the default teaching method the IMW expects candidates to identify by autolytic character, mousse, dosage.
- **Tank/Charmat method** (Prosecco, Asti, some New World "fizz"): **STRONG SIGNAL** — near-certain to be represented, since (c) rewards contrasting winemaking decisions and Charmat vs traditional is the single clearest style-driving choice in sparkling wine.
- **Traditional method New World benchmark** (England, Tasmania, or Cap Classique): **PLAUSIBLE** — one slot likely goes to a non-European traditional-method wine to test whether candidates over-index on "bubbles + biscuit = Champagne."
- **Champagne itself**: **PLAUSIBLE, not certain** — with only 4 slots for 4 countries, including Champagne "spends" a slot on the most obvious answer. The examiner may instead choose to withhold Champagne entirely to force candidates to build quality judgments without the default anchor — treat this as a live possibility, not an assumption.
- **Ancestral method / pét-nat**: **CURVEBALL** — would be a striking "winemaking decision" contrast, but pét-nat rarely carries enough commercial weight to anchor a 25-mark wine at this level; possible but not the way to bet.

## Candidate origins per wine
- One slot: **Champagne or Crémant (France)** — France is near-certain to appear somewhere in a 4-country sparkling flight; whether it's Champagne (prestige benchmark) or Crémant (value/second-tier French fizz, tests "quality within region" against a humbler backdrop) is the open question.
- One slot: **Italy** — most likely Prosecco (tank method, aromatic, easy commercial positioning) or Franciacorta (traditional method, quality flex). Prosecco is the more probable pick because it maximizes the (c) contrast against a traditional-method wine elsewhere in the flight.
- One slot: **Spain (Cava)** — high-probability pick: traditional method at a value price point, useful for testing "quality within context of region" since Cava spans everything from bulk export to Corpinnat-tier.
- One slot: **New World or England** — Cap Classique (South Africa), Tasmania/Adelaide Hills (Australia), or English sparkling are all plausible; England is attractive to examiners right now given the category's rising profile and cool-climate chalk-soil parallel to Champagne, making it a strong test of origin discrimination (candidates may default to "Champagne" on structure/aroma alone and need to catch chalk/acid/fruit cues pointing to England instead).

## Rule-outs
- **Germany (Sekt)** is possible but less likely to occupy a scarce country-slot versus England or Cap Classique, which the IMW has favored more recently for testing "New World/emerging" traditional method knowledge.
- **USA California traditional method** — plausible in principle but rarely appears in P3 sparkling flights historically; more commonly used in still-wine or fortified contexts. Treat as low-probability.
- **All-Champagne-absent flights** are unusual but not unprecedented — do not assume Champagne is automatically present; if the first wine on the nose reads clearly autolytic/chalky but the palate profile doesn't match Champagne fruit character, consider England or Franciacorta before defaulting.
- **Lambrusco/Asti** as a 4th-slot pick is unlikely — too narrow/regional for a "four different countries" survey question that wants breadth, not Italian internal style diversity (that would be its own separate question).

## The IMW's likely intent
This question tests (1) breadth of geographic knowledge across the global sparkling category — can the candidate go beyond Champagne to identify Cava, Prosecco, Cap Classique/England-type wines confidently and specifically; (2) the ability to judge quality *relative to the wine's own appellation norms* rather than applying a single Champagne-centric quality bar (explicit in the b) wording); (3) fluency in the mechanics of sparkling winemaking — traditional vs tank/Charmat vs transfer method, lees contact length, dosage, base blend — since (c) is worth nearly a third of the marks and is method-specific, not variety-specific.

## What to focus on in the glass
- **Bead and mousse**: fine, persistent bead = traditional method (extended lees, higher CO2 pressure); coarser, larger bubbles = tank/Charmat or carbonation.
- **Autolytic character**: brioche, biscuit, toast = extended lees aging (traditional method, likely 15+ months); absence of autolysis with bright primary fruit = short-lees Charmat (Prosecco-type).
- **Acidity and fruit profile**: high, taut acidity with citrus/orchard fruit and chalky minerality → cool climate (Champagne, England, Tasmania); riper stone fruit/tropical notes with softer acid → warmer climate (Cava, some Cap Classique, warmer-site Australia).
- **Dosage level**: Brut Nature/Extra Brut vs Brut vs Extra Dry/Demi-Sec — a key "winemaking decision" to call out explicitly in (c), and a strong style/commercial-positioning signal.
- **Base variety cues**: Chardonnay-dominant (citrus, linear, austere) vs Pinot-dominant (red-fruit weight, richer texture) vs Glera (floral, pear, low phenolics, aromatic primary fruit) vs Macabeo/Xarel-lo/Parellada (herbal, waxy, neutral-to-earthy) — use to narrow both variety and country simultaneously.
- **Colour and phenolic grip**: deeper gold with more phenolic texture suggests longer lees/skin contact or barrel-fermented base wine (higher-quality Champagne, Franciacorta, top Cava); pale, neutral, clean fruit suggests industrial-scale tank production.
