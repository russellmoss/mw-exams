# Decision matrix — 2026 Paper 2 Question 1

## Question (verbatim)
Wines 1-3 are from the same country and different single grape varieties.

For each wine:

a) Comment on the style and commercial position. (3 x 11 marks)

b) Comment on the method of production. (3 x 6 marks)

c) Identify the grape variety and origin as closely as possible (3 x 8 marks)

## Paper context
Paper 2 = red still wines. First flight of the paper, 3 wines, 75 marks total — a headline question that sets the tone for the exam.

## Stem signals
- **Same country** across all three wines — country is effectively "given," the exam is testing what you do with that constraint, not whether you can find it.
- **Different single grape varieties** — each wine must be a genuinely varietal wine (not a blend), and the three varieties must be distinct. This rules out countries whose flagship reds are overwhelmingly blends (classic Bordeaux, Douro, Chianti Classico-style Sangiovese-plus blends unless bottled 100%, Rioja Tempranillo-Garnacha-Graciano blends unless single-varietal cuvées).
- **Mark structure is inverted from the norm**: style/commercial position leads at 11 marks per wine (33 total — the largest single component of the question), production method is 6 marks (18 total), and identification of variety + origin is asked *last* and is worth the *fewest* marks (8 each, 24 total).
- This inversion is a strong signal: the examiners are not primarily testing "can you name the grape and place" — they are testing whether the candidate can read style, infer target market/price point, and articulate winemaking choices. ID is almost a formality once style is correctly read.

## Universe
Given P2 (reds) + "same country, three distinct single varieties" + heavy weight on commercial-position discrimination →
the country must offer at least three well-known, globally recognized single-varietal reds that plausibly occupy *different* style/quality/price tiers (so there is something meaningful to say about commercial position for each). ## Candidate varieties/countries (confidence tiers)
- **Italy — STRONG SIGNAL.** Deep bench of globally-known single-varietal reds at genuinely different price/style tiers: Nebbiolo (Barolo/Barbaresco, premium, high tannin+acid), Sangiovese (Chianti Classico/Brunello, mid-to-premium, sour-cherry acid-driven), and a value/commercial tier from Primitivo (Puglia) or Nero d'Avola (Sicily), riper and rounder. This is close to an ideal fit for the "commercial position" emphasis.
- **France — STRONG SIGNAL.** Non-Bordeaux single-varietal traditions are strong enough: Pinot Noir (Burgundy, premium-to-ultra-premium), Syrah (Northern Rhône — Hermitage/Cornas/Crozes, mid-to-premium), Gamay (Beaujolais, everyday-to-cru, most accessible commercially) or Cabernet Franc (Loire — Chinon/Bourgueil, mid-tier, food wine). Gives a clean cheap→premium spread for the commercial-position marks.
- **USA (California/Oregon) — STRONG SIGNAL.** Cabernet Sauvignon (Napa, premium/status wine), Pinot Noir (Sonoma Coast or Willamette, mid-to-premium, restaurant-list wine), Zinfandel (Sonoma/Lodi, characterful, mid-market, distinctly "American" story) — three wines with very different commercial narratives, which plays directly to the 11-mark emphasis.
- **Australia — PLAUSIBLE.** Shiraz (Barossa, can span bulk to icon-wine), Cabernet Sauvignon (Coonawarra, structured, mid-premium), Pinot Noir (Yarra Valley/Mornington/Tasmania, cool-climate, premium niche). Strong stylistic spread, slightly less "three iconic varieties" than Italy/France/USA.
- **Spain — PLAUSIBLE.** Tempranillo (Rioja/Ribera del Duero, oak-aged, mid-premium), Garnacha (Priorat, old-vine, premium), Monastrell/Mencía (Jumilla/Bierzo, value-to-emerging) — defensible but leans more on regional than varietal fame versus Italy/France.
- **South Africa — PLAUSIBLE.** Pinotage (national signature, mid-market), Cabernet Sauvignon (Stellenbosch, mid-premium), Syrah/Shiraz (Swartland, increasingly premium) — coherent but a smaller stage; more likely if the exam wants a "know your emerging fine-wine countries" angle.
- **Argentina — CURVEBALL.** Malbec (Mendoza, the obvious anchor) plus Cabernet Sauvignon and/or Bonarda/Pinot Noir (Patagonia) — plausible but the second and third varieties are less globally "famous," weakening the commercial-position contrast the marks are pushing toward.
- **Chile — CURVEBALL.** Carmenère, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah/Pinot Noir (Casablanca/San Antonio) — workable but historically less favored for this exact three-varietal-showcase structure.
- **Portugal, Germany, New Zealand — RULE OUT / deep curveball.** Portugal's fine reds are still dominated by field blends even where varietal labeling exists; Germany's red bench basically starts and ends at Spätburgunder; New Zealand's non-Pinot reds (Hawke's Bay Bordeaux blends, some Syrah) don't give three distinct, equally famous single varieties.

## Rule-outs
- **Bordeaux/Rioja/Chianti as the flagship wine within its country** — these are blend-first regions; using them here would violate "single grape variety" unless the examiners specifically chose an atypical 100%-varietal bottling, which is possible but works against the grain of the question.
- **Douro/Port-producing Portugal** — field-blend culture makes "three distinct famous single varietals" hard to construct credibly.
- **Germany for reds** — essentially a one-variety country (Spätburgunder) at this level; can't supply three distinct famous reds.
- **New Zealand** — same logic as the 2025 P1 Q3 white ruling: the country can supply one iconic variety (there, Sauvignon Blanc; here, Pinot Noir) but the second/third choices are comparatively obscure globally.

## IMW's likely intent
Given the mark inversion — 11 for style/commercial, 6 for production, only 8 for ID — this question is testing **market fluency and stylistic range within a single origin**, not fine-grained origin detective work. The examiners are asking: can the candidate read three genuinely different red styles (structure, tannin, oak, ripeness) and correctly translate that into where each wine sits commercially (restaurant list vs. supermarket vs. collector's cellar) — with correct variety/origin naming almost a checkbox once the style read is right. This is the natural "breadth of one country's red wine landscape" companion to 2025 P1 Q3's white version and to 2024 P2 Q1's "one variety across styles/price points" — here inverted to "one country across varieties/price points."

## What to focus on in the glass
- **Tannin quality and acid structure** are the fastest variety differentiators: high, fine, grippy tannin + high acid points to Nebbiolo; bright, savory, sour-cherry acid with softer tannin points to Sangiovese; silky, red-fruited, lower-tannin structure points to Pinot Noir or Gamay; broad, riper, lower-acid fruit points to Primitivo/Zinfandel/warm-climate Garnacha.
- **Oak signature and extraction** for the production-method marks: new oak vanilla/toast + firm tannin management = premium, deliberate winemaking (Napa Cab, Barolo, Burgundy premier/grand cru); carbonic/semi-carbonic aromatics (banana, bubblegum, low tannin) = Beaujolais; whole-cluster/stem inclusion (green, peppery, chewy tannin) = cool-climate Syrah or Pinot Noir.
- **Concentration, alcohol, and finish length** as the direct evidence for commercial position: high concentration + long finish + refined oak = collector/on-premise pricing; simpler mid-palate, shorter finish, more overt fruit sweetness = everyday/retail pricing.
- **Colour depth and rim variation across the three glasses** — since the wines share a country but not a variety, expect visibly different colour profiles (pale garnet Pinot/Nebbiolo-in-youth vs. deep opaque Primitivo/Zinfandel/Malbec) — use this as a first-pass sort before nosing.
- Once style, structure and production read is locked in, spend minimal time on the ID marks (8 each) — confirm variety/origin as a confirmation step, not a discovery step, consistent with the mark weighting.
