# Decision matrix — 2026 Paper 1 Question 1

## Question (verbatim)
Wines 1-6 are from the same single grape variety and come from five different countries.

For each wine:

a) Identify the origin as closely as possible. (6 x 10 marks)

b) Discuss how winemaking has influenced the wine's style. (6 x 10 marks)

c) Comment on the commercial position. (6 x 5 marks)

## Paper context
Paper 1 = white still wines. This is the largest opener in the ten-year corpus by wine count (6) and by marks (150). No prior P1 Q1 has gone above 6 wines from a single variety, but the *shape* of the question — one variety, multiple countries, origin + winemaking + commercial — is a well-worn IMW template scaled up to maximum size.

## Stem signals
- **Same single grape variety, six wines** — must be a variety with genuinely global commercial plantings; a niche grape cannot support six distinct national expressions.
- **Five different countries for six wines** — exactly one country is used twice. That repeat is deliberate: it exists to let the candidate show intra-country winemaking or regional contrast rather than just spot-the-country.
- **(a) Origin, 60 marks** — full weight on placing each wine as specifically as possible (region/sub-region, not just country).
- **(b) Winemaking's influence on style, 60 marks — equal weight to origin.** This is the most important signal in the stem. When winemaking scores as much as origin, the examiner is not treating the wines as passive expressions of terroir; deliberate cellar decisions (oak regime, malolactic, lees work, skin contact, picking date/style) must be legible and worth discussing at length for every single wine.
- **(c) Commercial position, 30 marks (5 each)** — lower weight but present for all six, implying a genuine price/quality spread rather than six peers.

## Universe
A single white variety, planted at real commercial volume in at least five countries, capable of showing dramatically different styles depending on winemaking choices (not just climate). This is a short list of truly global, winemaking-malleable whites.

## Candidate varieties (confidence tiers)
- **Chardonnay: STRONG SIGNAL.** The corpus's default answer for every large-scale P1 "same variety, multiple countries" flight (2011, 2016, 2018, 2025 — always 2+ wines, scaling to 4 in the two largest prior instances). It is *the* variety defined by winemaking choice — oak type/age/toast, malolactic (full/partial/none), lees stirring, wild vs. cultured yeast — producing radically different wines from identical fruit. That fits (b)'s 60-mark emphasis better than any other white grape. Global commercial plantings comfortably cover five-plus countries (France, USA, Australia, South Africa, New Zealand, Chile, Argentina, Italy).
- **Riesling: STRONG SIGNAL, second tier.** Also proven at 4-wine P1 scale (2014: Australia/Alsace/NZ/Germany) and reaches five countries easily (Germany, Alsace, Austria, Australia, NZ, USA). Weaker fit for (b) specifically — Riesling's classic identity is site/climate-driven with minimal cellar intervention, though skin contact, oak, and old-school oxidative German winemaking are real curveball angles the IMW could be testing.
- **Sauvignon Blanc: PLAUSIBLE.** Global reach (Loire, NZ, California, South Africa, Chile) and real winemaking variance (Fumé Blanc oak, lees-aged Sancerre vs. tank-fresh Marlborough). Used only as a 2-wine pair in the corpus so far (2023 Q1), never scaled to a full multi-country flight.
- **Pinot Gris/Grigio: PLAUSIBLE.** Has appeared repeatedly at 2–3 wine scale (2012, 2016, 2017) with real stylistic range (neutral Italian vs. rich skin-contact Alsace vs. fruity Oregon/NZ), but five distinct countries at commercial volume is a stretch.
- **Chenin Blanc: CURVEBALL.** Real winemaking range (dry to sweet, oaked to unoaked) but production is concentrated in essentially two countries (France, South Africa); reaching five is implausible without reaching for marginal producers (US, Argentina, Australia).

## Candidate origins per wine
Assuming Chardonnay (lead hypothesis): expect a classic "big five" Chardonnay-producing spread — **France (Burgundy), USA (California/Oregon), Australia, New Zealand, South Africa** — with the doubled country most likely **France**, contrasting an unoaked/lean Chablis-style wine against an oaked, malo-driven Côte de Beaune-style wine (Meursault/Puligny/Corton-Charlemagne). This maximizes the payoff of the equal-weighted winemaking question: two wines, same country, same grape, opposite cellar philosophy. A doubled USA (California vs. Oregon) or doubled Australia (cool Margaret River/Adelaide Hills vs. warm inland/commercial) are the next most likely alternates.

If Riesling: expect Germany doubled (steely Mosel Kabinett vs. dry Pfalz/Rheingau GG) alongside Alsace, Austria, Australia, and New Zealand or the USA.

## Rule-outs
- Unlikely Gewürztraminer, Viognier, Muscat, Albariño, Grüner Veltliner — none has genuine commercial-scale plantings in five separate countries.
- Unlikely a blend-driven answer — the stem says "same single grape variety," ruling out Bordeaux Blanc/GSM-style multi-variety wines.
- Unlikely for the doubled country to be an obscure one (e.g., Chile or Argentina twice) — the exam favors doubling a country the candidate already expects to see, so the "trick" is in the contrast, not the discovery.

## The IMW's likely intent
Testing sustained accuracy at scale: six independent origin calls, six independent winemaking analyses, six commercial judgments, all on one variety. The equal weighting of origin and winemaking signals the examiner wants candidates to separate "where is this from" reasoning from "what did the winemaker do" reasoning — i.e., not to default to climate as the sole explanation for style, and to actively identify oak, malolactic, lees contact, and skin contact as independent variables. The doubled country is very likely the single highest-value discriminator in the whole question: nailing why two wines from the same place, same grape, taste completely different is the mark of a strong candidate.

## What to focus on in the glass
- **Oak signals first**: vanilla, toast, clove, smoke, spice — presence/absence and new vs. old, small vs. large format.
- **Malolactic markers**: buttery/creamy diacetyl texture vs. crisp, linear malic acidity.
- **Lees influence**: bready, biscuity, textural weight from batonnage/extended lees aging.
- **Reduction vs. oxidative handling**: flinty/matchstick reduction (protective winemaking) vs. nutty/honeyed oxidative notes (old-school or intentional oxidative style).
- **Fruit ripeness and alcohol** as the climate signal, separate from the above winemaking signals — use this to cross-check origin once winemaking style is accounted for.
- **Within any repeated country**, isolate exactly which winemaking lever differs — that pair is where (b)'s marks concentrate hardest.
- Colour depth and evolution cues (pale/green vs. deep gold) to support maturity and commercial tier judgments for (c).
