# Mock answer — 2023 Paper 2 Question 3

## a) Identify the grape variety (4 x 8 marks)

**Wine 9** — Pinot Noir. Translucent ruby, red-black cherry, earth and spice, and a savoury, sweet-tannined palate with only light oak point away from a thicker-skinned variety. Nebbiolo was considered for the pale colour but its tar-and-rose lift and drier tannin are absent; Gamay was ruled out by the wine's greater depth and firmer mid-palate grip than simple carbonic fruit would give. The savoury, earthy signature at 13.5% confirms Pinot Noir.

**Wine 10** — Malbec. Opaque purple-ruby, dense black plum, cassis, violet and liquorice, firm oak and a full, muscular palate at 15% alcohol indicate a thick-skinned, deeply pigmented variety built for extraction. Syrah was considered for the dark fruit and pepper spice, but the violet-liquorice signature and riper tannin are more typical of Malbec; Tannat was eliminated by its characteristically harder, more astringent tannin.

**Wine 11** — Pinot Noir. Medium ruby-garnet, fresh strawberry and cherry with subtle spice, and a light-to-medium, supple palate with gentle tannin again point to Pinot's thin-skinned, low-anthocyanin profile. Gamay was the main alternative but its simpler, one-dimensional red fruit and lower structural weight do not match the spice and light tannic grip here.

**Wine 12** — Malbec. Deep purple-ruby, black fruit and violet with well-integrated oak, and a concentrated, fine-tannined, long palate at 13.8% again suggest Malbec. Cabernet Sauvignon and Syrah were both considered given the oak treatment, but neither shows this wine's violet lift, and the fine rather than blocky tannin, together with the bright acidity, is more consistent with Malbec than Cabernet's firmer, angular tannin.

## b) Identify the origin as closely as possible (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 9** — Burgundy, Côte de Nuits, likely Nuits-Saint-Georges. The savoury, earthy, spice-driven profile with moderate rather than searing tannin points to Burgundy over any New World Pinot source. Old-vine concentration without the extraction or length of a Premier or Grand Cru site narrows this to village level, consistent with Nuits-Saint-Georges' reputation for firmer, more structured Pinot than neighbouring Chambolle-Musigny.

**Wine 10** — South West France, Cahors. The near-opaque colour, dense dark fruit and firm tannic backbone at 15% alcohol are well beyond anything Bordeaux's Right Bank Merlot-based wines would show, pointing instead to Cahors, where Malbec — locally Côt or Auxerrois — retains its deepest colour and most tannic expression, reinforced by the liquorice and violet lift typical of the appellation's cooler, more elevated plateau sites.

**Wine 11** — Argentina, Patagonia, Río Negro. Set against Wine 12's riper, oak-framed style, this wine's paler colour, red-fruited restraint and lighter body point to a cooler New World site than warm-valley Mendoza. The bright acidity and gentle tannin are consistent with Patagonia's cool, dry, high-latitude conditions along the Río Negro valley rather than the higher-altitude but warmer Uco Valley.

**Wine 12** — Argentina, Mendoza, high-altitude Uco Valley/Luján de Cuyo. The concentration, fine rather than coarse tannin and the freshness persisting despite full ripeness and oak point to elevation rather than latitude as the key explanatory lever here — altitude, not a cooler regional climate, is what checks the alcohol and preserves the acid line in a variety that can otherwise turn blowsy in warmer, lower Mendoza sites.

## c) Comment on style, quality and commercial position (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 9** — A restrained, savoury, structured style typical of Burgundian Pinot rather than an overtly fruity New World rendition. Very good quality, Village Nuits-Saint-Georges level, comparable to a well-sited Village Gevrey-Chambertin, retailing around £40–50. Sold through independent specialists and by-the-glass on serious restaurant lists, it competes with premium Côte de Nuits villages and ambitious New World Pinot from Central Otago or Sonoma Coast, with export markets in the UK, US and Japan among collectors trading up from generic Burgundy.

**Wine 10** — A powerful, oak-framed, age-worthy style showing Cahors' modern ambition rather than its historic rustic reputation. Very good quality, at the top of the appellation, retailing around £25–35 — a step above everyday Cahors and a credible rival to premium Bordeaux satellites at a similar price. Best suited to independent specialists and on-trade by the bottle, it targets Malbec-curious drinkers in the US and UK, competing directly with premium Argentine Malbec on richness while offering genuine Old World structure.

**Wine 11** — An accessible, fresh, lightly oaked style built for early drinking rather than long cellaring. Good to very good quality, estate level, retailing around £15–20. This suits premium supermarkets and casual on-trade by-the-glass listings, competing with cooler-climate New World Pinot from New Zealand and Chile, with export potential in markets seeking approachable, fruit-driven Pinot below Burgundian village prices.

**Wine 12** — A concentrated, precise, oak-integrated style with real ageing potential. Excellent quality, equivalent to a top-tier Mendoza icon bottling, retailing around £30–40, benchmarked against premium Napa Cabernet or top Coonawarra reds for scale and polish. Positioned through independent specialists and fine dining lists by the bottle, with the US and UK as key export markets, it competes with premium New World reds at the $40–50 bracket on the strength of its altitude-driven precision.
