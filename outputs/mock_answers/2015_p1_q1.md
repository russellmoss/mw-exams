# Mock answer — 2015 Paper 1 Question 1

## a) Identify the specific origin as closely as possible (4 x 12 marks)

All four wines show a neutral, non-aromatic core of green apple and citrus with no obvious floral or grapey lift, which rules out Riesling, Muscat and Sauvignon Blanc as the common thread and points instead to Chardonnay. Two show no oak at all while two show clear barrel character, and the stem confirms a single region and vintage across all four — this pattern of a lean, unoaked pair alongside a richer, barrel-worked pair is the classic split within white Burgundy rather than, say, Alsace or the Mosel, where a single estate style would be more uniform. That funnels the region firmly to Burgundy Chardonnay before considering each wine individually.

**Wine 1** — village Chablis. Pale lemon with green tints, intense green apple, lemon curd and a distinct oyster-shell, chalky minerality, no oak evident, searing acidity and a light 12.5% alcohol. This is the textbook cool, Kimmeridgian-limestone profile of Chablis; the total absence of oak and the linear, saline finish place it at village rather than any higher tier.

**Wine 2** — Chablis Grand Cru. The same flinty, oyster-shell minerality and unoaked, steely backbone confirm Chablis, but with noticeably more depth, extract and a longer, saline finish at 13.0% alcohol — weight that the village wine (Wine 1) lacks. Within Chablis this concentration without any change in winemaking (still no oak) is diagnostic of a Grand Cru climat rather than a Premier Cru, which would show slightly less mid-palate density.

**Wine 3** — a Grand Cru from the Côte de Beaune. Hazelnut, brioche, vanilla and a creamy oak frame alongside white peach set this wine apart structurally from the two Chablis examples — riper fruit, fuller body, glycerol weight and toasty barrel integration at 13.5% alcohol. This shift in style, not just intensity, rules out Chablis and confirms the warmer, richer Côte de Beaune. The sheer concentration, unctuous texture and length of oak integration are beyond village or even Premier Cru scale, pointing to Grand Cru — most plausibly Bâtard-Montrachet or a related Montrachet climat.

**Wine 4** — village Puligny-Montrachet. Barrel-fermented like Wine 3, with hazelnut, light vanilla and wet-stone character, but at 13.0% alcohol it shows noticeably less concentration, a tighter mid-palate and a medium rather than long finish. The oaked, Côte de Beaune style confirms the same commune area as Wine 3, but the lesser weight and shorter finish rule out Grand Cru or Premier Cru status and place it at village Puligny-Montrachet.

## b) Discuss quality, with specific reference to the region of production (40 marks)

Read together, these four wines map a clean 2x2 grid across Burgundy's white hierarchy: Chablis against Côte de Beaune on one axis, village against Grand Cru on the other. Wine 1, a good quality village Chablis retailing around £25, shows genuine Kimmeridgian minerality and racy acidity but a simple, linear structure with limited mid-palate density — accurate and well made but without the concentration to suggest anything above village level. Wine 2, an outstanding Chablis Grand Cru around £60, shows what the same unoaked, stainless-steel philosophy can achieve with superior fruit: greater extract, a saline, persistent finish and real intensity without added oak, fully justifying Grand Cru status and standing at the top of the Chablis quality pyramid. Wine 3, the Bâtard-Montrachet Grand Cru at a trophy price around £300, is the most complete wine of the flight — concentrated, textured, with well-judged new oak integration and a long, mineral finish that shows genuine complexity and ageing potential; this is world-class white Burgundy at the pinnacle of the appellation system. Wine 4, a very good village Puligny-Montrachet around £60, sits below Wine 3 in weight and length though sharing its oaked style — elegant and well balanced but lacking the density and persistence of a Premier or Grand Cru site. The pairing usefully shows that quality in Burgundy tracks precisely with the classification pyramid: the unoaked Chablis wines rely on soil-driven minerality and acid tension for their hierarchy, while the Côte de Beaune wines layer barrel-derived richness onto that same site-driven concentration, with Wine 3's Grand Cru weight the clear peak of the flight.

## c) Identify the vintage (12 marks)

2012. All four wines show real concentration relative to their tier — low yields from a difficult growing season, most likely from spring frost and summer hail, are consistent with fruit that is fuller and more intense than would be expected from a generous crop. Despite this, the wines retain lively, well-integrated acidity rather than the leaner, greener profile of a genuinely cool, high-yielding year, suggesting a warm, dry finish to the season that brought ripeness back into balance. The colour depth (pale lemon to gold, gaining with tier) and the primary but faintly developing fruit character across all four are consistent with wines roughly three years from harvest at the time of tasting, pointing to 2012 as the vintage.
