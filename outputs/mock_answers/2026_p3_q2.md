# Mock answer — 2026 Paper 3 Question 2

## Pair 1 — Wines 5-6

### a) Origin (8 marks)

Both wines show a fine, persistent bead and a pronounced autolytic character — biscuit, brioche and toast over green apple and citrus fruit — that signals traditional method with real lees contact rather than tank-method or short-ageing sparkling. Racy, almost searing acidity together with a chalky, saline mineral edge running through both glasses points to a cool-climate, chalk-soil origin; that combination of fine mousse, high acid and 12.5% alcohol is more consistent with Champagne than with Franciacorta (softer acid, warmer fruit) or English sparkling (typically leaner, less textural depth at this price point). Within Champagne, wine 5's flinty, white-flower purity with no dosage sweetness suggests all-Chardonnay fruit — a Blanc de Blancs — while wine 6's rounder, more peach-toned profile with broader autolytic softness fits the classic three-variety blend.

### b) Methods of production (14 marks)

Wine 5's completely dry, unsoftened finish shows no dosage liqueur was added at disgorgement — Brut Nature. The unblunted acid line and mineral purity, with no buttery gloss, suggest a spontaneous, all-stainless-steel base-wine fermentation with malolactic allowed to run its course to take the edge off raw acidity, followed by an extended lees ageing (the depth of brioche character implies close to two years) before disgorgement with no dosage. Wine 6's rounder, faintly sweeter impression indicates a conventional dosage, estimated at around 9 g/L, added to smooth a base wine with less textural precision — a large-volume, multi-vintage reserve-wine assemblage aimed at consistent house style rather than site expression, with a shorter lees regime than wine 5.

### c) Quality (16 marks)

Wine 5's precision and mineral drive, unmasked by dosage, sit at genuine grower/artisan quality — comparable to a well-regarded single-variety Champagne from a strong village, retailing around £35-45, with enough concentration to carry the demanding zero-dosage style without feeling austere. Wine 6 is a soundly made, commercially polished Champagne but shows less textural depth and a simpler, dosage-assisted fruit profile typical of the accessible, own-label tier — good value at around £25 but positioned as an entry point rather than a wine of individual character, several notches below wine 5 despite sharing the same appellation.

## Pair 2 — Wines 7-8

### a) Origin (8 marks)

Wine 7's pale tawny-amber core and orange-green rim, with dried fruit, walnut and baking spice built by slow oxidation, mark a long-aged tawny style. Wine 8's deep, glossy purple-black colour with primary black cherry, cassis and firm structured tannin marks a young, fruit-driven, essentially reductive fortified red. Both share high alcohol (19-20%) alongside real residual sweetness and spirit-derived heat, the unmistakable signature of grape-spirit fortification arresting fermentation mid-ferment; combined with a field-blend of indigenous dark-skinned varieties and schist-soil character, this points unambiguously to Portugal's Douro/Porto rather than biologically-aged Jerez or the softer, single-variety VDNs of the southern Rhône.

### b) Methods of production (14 marks)

Both wines share an identical starting point — hand-harvested, warm-fermented Douro reds arrested by grape spirit (aguardente, c.77% abv) at roughly the midpoint of fermentation, once about half the natural sugar has converted. From there they diverge entirely through elevage. Wine 7's integrated spirit and lack of fresh red-fruit vibrancy point to many years — a blended average of varied cask ages — maturing slowly in small, seasoned oak in a humid riverside cellar, where gradual oxygen ingress drives the browning, evaporative concentration and walnut/dried-fig complexity in the glass. Wine 8's retained deep colour, unresolved tannin and absence of bottle sediment point to a single, favourable vintage matured far more briefly — around four to six years — in larger, more neutral wood limiting oxidative exchange, then filtered before bottling to remove deposit and give a wine ready to drink without decanting.

### c) Quality (16 marks)

Wine 7's seamless spirit integration, layered complexity and long, nutty finish reflect a well-aged blend at the upper-middle of the IVDP's indicated-age tiers — consistent with a 20-Year-Old tawny, well above 10-Year in complexity though short of the drier concentration of 30- or 40-Year wines, retailing around £45-55 for a half-litre. Wine 8's density of fruit and ripe, present tannin point to a structurally generous underlying vintage, placing it at the top of the LBV category — a genuine "baby Vintage Port" style at around £20-25, offering much of Vintage Port's drama without the ageing requirement, though lacking the extra layer that unfiltered bottle maturation would eventually add.

## Pair 3 — Wines 9-10

### a) Origin (8 marks)

Both wines show deep gold colour, viscous legs and pronounced botrytis aromatics — dried apricot, honey, marmalade, ginger-spice — over high alcohol (13.5%) and very high residual sugar, ruling out passerillage or ice wine (neither shows cryo-concentration or unripe green-apple acidity) in favour of noble-rot-driven, Sémillon-dominant sweet Bordeaux. The citrus lift and racy underlying acid keeping both fresh rather than cloying, on a Sémillon/Sauvignon Blanc base, narrows this to Sauternes-Barsac rather than Loire (more purely Chenin, less unctuous) or Alsace SGN (aromatic, Gewürztraminer/Pinot Gris driven). Wine 9's fresher, more citrus-and-orchard register against wine 10's broader, floral acacia-and-peach profile fits the traditionally lighter Barsac style across the Ciron from the richer wines of Sauternes proper, where wine 10 sits.

### b) Methods of production (14 marks)

Both depend on the same mechanism — botrytis cinerea desiccating and concentrating sugar — but harvest and cellar routes diverge sharply. Wine 9's layered concentration, building through fresh citrus, dried fruit then spice, suggests a demanding, multi-pass hand harvest picking only botrytised berries as they reach the right stage of shrivel across several successive tries; the toasted, vanilla-spiced oak knitted into the fruit rather than sitting on top points to full barrel fermentation in a high proportion of new French oak and an extended elevage of around 18 months. Wine 10 shows none of that oak seasoning — its unclouded botrytis purity indicates fermentation and ageing entirely in stainless steel, a simpler selective-picking regime and a shorter elevage aimed at preserving primary freshness rather than building complexity through barrel contact.

### c) Quality (16 marks)

Wine 9's density, oak-derived structural spine and layering of aromatic development sit convincingly at classified-growth level — consistent with a Premier or Deuxieme Cru Classe under the 1855 Sauternes classification, retailing around £45-60 per half-bottle. Wine 10 is a sound, true expression of botrytis character but simpler and more linear, lacking wine 9's structural grip and complexity; this reads as an unclassified estate wine or a classified château's more accessible second label, offering immediate, fresh-drinking pleasure at a more modest £20-25 rather than the ageability of a classed growth — a reminder that quality in Sauternes-Barsac varies enormously beneath the 1855 hierarchy.

## Pair 4 — Wines 11-12

### a) Origin (8 marks)

Both wines show pure, high-toned lime and orchard-fruit character, a distinct wet-slate minerality and taut acidity that point to Riesling rather than Gruner Veltliner (lacks this acid drive) or Chenin Blanc (wine 11's linear, unhoneyed structure does not fit). Moderate alcohol, cool-climate acid retention and slate-derived mineral drive place this in Germany rather than riper, more phenolic Alsace or more peppery, saline Austria. Within Germany, the racy precision and pronounced site-specific grip, rather than the softer, more overtly fruity style of the Mosel or Rheingau, point to the Nahe, whose fragmented slate-and-volcanic geology gives Riesling unusual textural weight alongside its acid line. The shared aromatic and mineral fingerprint across both wines — allowing for wine 12's arrested-fermentation sweetness — is consistent with a single grower working two adjacent Einzellagen rather than two unrelated estates.

### b) Methods of production (14 marks)

Wine 11's essentially dry finish alongside full 12.5% alcohol shows fermentation ran to completion, converting almost all natural sugar to alcohol; hand-picked at moderate ripeness with no botrytis evident, fermented and matured with no oak at all to preserve mineral purity — a classic Trocken regime from a top-rated site. Wine 12's low 8% alcohol carried alongside substantial residual sweetness is the clearest possible signature of an arrested fermentation, halted — most likely by chilling and racking off the lees — once only a modest share of the naturally high sugar had converted; the intact, unshrivelled orchard-fruit character indicates ripe, healthy fruit at Spätlese must-weight rather than botrytis-affected or desiccated grapes, picked in a single, less selective pass appropriate to a straightforward Prädikat designation.

### c) Quality (16 marks)

Wine 11's concentration, textural grip and long, precise mineral finish sit well above simple village Riesling and are consistent with fruit from a top-rated Einzellage — in the VDP hierarchy this reads as Grosse Lage quality, the equivalent of a Grand Cru site, retailing around £30-35 with real ageing potential. Wine 12 shows lovely purity and a beguiling sweet-acid interplay typical of good Nahe Spätlese, but has less structural depth and site-specific minerality than wine 11, coming from a respected but lesser-ranked Einzellage sitting below Grosse Lage status — a good quality, classic Prädikatswein at Spätlese level worth perhaps £20-25, sound and true to style but a clear tier below its stablemate. Since both share a single grower, this quality gap is site-driven rather than producer-driven — precisely the distinction the pairing tests.
