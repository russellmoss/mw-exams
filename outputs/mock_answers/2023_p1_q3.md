# Mock answer — 2023 Paper 1 Question 3

## a) Name the country and the three grape varieties, referencing all four wines (40 marks)

South Africa. The flight opens with a restrained, saline, barrel-worked white (Wine 9) sitting alongside two contrasting wines sharing a waxy, textural Chenin identity (Wines 10 and 11) and a herbal, mineral, notably un-pungent Sauvignon Blanc (Wine 12). No single New World country routinely offers this specific spread — serious cool-climate Chardonnay, Chenin Blanc pitched at both boutique and commercial volume, and a restrained rather than thiol-driven Sauvignon — as convincingly as South Africa, where Chenin is the historic flagship variety and maritime sites such as Walker Bay and Hemel-en-Aarde deliver the tension seen across the flight. Australia and California were considered for the Chardonnay but ruled out by its saline, chalky reserve rather than riper New World generosity; New Zealand was eliminated for the Sauvignon given the absence of pungent gooseberry and passionfruit lift.

Wine 9 is Chardonnay: pear, lime and citrus peel over a struck-flint, saline mineral edge, with a creamy, barrel-derived breadth and a long chalky finish, are more consistent with Chardonnay's textural range than with Chenin, whose waxy, lanolin fruit character is absent here.

Wines 10 and 11 are both Chenin Blanc, and deliberately so — the pairing is the flight's built-in quality and commercial contrast, a low-yield old-vine site wine set against a large-volume entry bottling. Both show the variety's signature waxy citrus, yellow apple and quince fruit with bright acidity, but Wine 10 carries old-vine concentration and textural weight while Wine 11 is lighter and more overtly fruity; Chardonnay was eliminated for both on the absence of any creamy, malic or buttery dimension, and Sauvignon Blanc on the absence of green, herbaceous lift.

Wine 12 is Sauvignon Blanc: citrus, fresh herbs and a subtle stony, saline note, with brisk acidity but no pungent blackcurrant-leaf or passionfruit character, point to a restrained Cape style rather than a New Zealand-style thiol-driven wine; the variety is confirmed by the characteristic green herbal register that none of the other three wines show.

## b) Comment on the winemaking (4 x 5 marks)

**Wine 9** — 100% barrel fermentation, mostly in French oak with a meaningful first-fill component, explains the discreet creamy texture and long chalky finish; the restraint of oak flavour despite full barrel handling suggests careful blending of older barrels with the new wood, with extended lees contact adding further textural breadth.

**Wine 10** — whole-bunch pressing and a natural fermentation preserve the pure waxy citrus fruit; ageing in old neutral oak, with a portion in concrete, rather than new wood explains why there is texture and roundness without vanilla or spice, consistent with an old-vine, site-focused approach that lets fruit and soil speak.

**Wine 11** — essentially no oak signature; hand-harvesting and a cool, controlled fermentation followed by early bottling account for the crunchy, immediate, fruit-forward style, made for freshness rather than complexity or ageing potential.

**Wine 12** — natural yeast fermentation and a small proportion, under 10%, matured in neutral barrels give a lightly structural mid-palate without adding oak flavour; the bulk of the blend is clearly handled in stainless steel to preserve the herbal, saline purity.

## c) Comment on quality and market potential (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 9** — Outstanding quality, equivalent to a serious village or lower Premier Cru level white Burgundy in its balance of fruit purity, oak integration and length; South Africa's cool maritime Chardonnay sites are increasingly benchmarked at this level and origin bias should not discount it. Retailing around £30-35, this belongs on independent specialist and fine-dining by-the-glass lists, targeting Burgundy-curious consumers in the UK and US as an alternative to increasingly expensive white Burgundy.

**Wine 10** — Excellent quality, comparable to a serious dry Loire Chenin such as Savennières, showing genuine old-vine concentration and site expression; this is fine-wine-tier Chenin, not a commercial style. At around £25-30 it suits independent merchants and sommelier-led restaurant lists, appealing to consumers seeking terroir-driven alternatives to Chenin's Loire heartland, with strong potential in the UK, US and Scandinavian on-trade.

**Wine 11** — A good, honest entry-level commercial style, well made but built for immediate drinking rather than complexity; equivalent to a solid varietal Coastal Region bottling rather than an estate wine, and a useful counterpoint to the concentration of Wine 10 from the same variety. Retailing around £8-10, it belongs in supermarkets and chain on-trade by-the-glass pours, competing with everyday Loire and southern French whites for consumers wanting reliable, fruity, easy-drinking Chenin.

**Wine 12** — Very good quality, above generic Coastal Region Sauvignon and closer to a premium single-site Cape example, its textural restraint and length marking it out from simple varietal Sauvignon. At around £15-18 it suits independent specialists and premium supermarket ranges, positioned as a more serious, food-friendly alternative to Loire Sauvignon or restrained Marlborough for UK and Northern European drinkers.
