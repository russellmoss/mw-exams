# Mock answer — 2021 Paper 1 Question 3

## a) Identify the grape variety and the region as closely as possible (30 marks)

Riesling. Both wines share bone-dry palates, very high, racy acidity, no evidence of oak and pure, precise lime, green apple and white peach fruit lifted by a floral, slightly kerosene-tinged aromatic edge — a combination that reads as Riesling rather than Grüner Veltliner, which was considered but eliminated by the absence of the characteristic white pepper and lentil-like vegetal note and by the more piercing citrus intensity here. German or Alsace Riesling was also considered but the bone-dry finish, moderate 12.5–13.5% alcohols and the notable extract and glycerol even at the lower-alcohol wine argue against a cooler German site and instead point to Austria.

The dry, mineral, saline-finishing style with no discernible residual sugar, married to a flinty, slate-like minerality and taut acid line, points specifically to the Wachau on the Danube's terraced slopes rather than a broader Austrian appellation such as Kamptal or Kremstal. Because the two wines share producer, variety and vintage, the entire remaining evidence — the near-identical fruit spectrum but markedly different alcohol, weight and concentration — is best explained not by site or year but by Austria's own ripeness-based classification. Wine 8, at 12.5% and leaner in body, sits at the top of the Federspiel band (11.5–12.5% abv, earlier-picked, lighter style). Wine 9, at 13.5% with far greater extract, richness and a longer, layered finish, exceeds the Smaragd threshold (minimum 12.5%, typically 13–14%, the latest-picked and most concentrated tier) — reinforced by evidence of a single, named terraced Ried rather than a broader estate blend.

## b) Compare and contrast the two wines, discussing quality, maturity, winemaking and market position (20 marks)

Wine 8 is very good quality, equivalent to a village-level Wachau Federspiel retailing around $20–25; Wine 9 is outstanding, single-vineyard Smaragd quality, comparable to a Grand Cru-level Alsace Riesling or top Mosel GG, at $50–80+. Both show pale colour and youthful, primary fruit consistent with a 2018 vintage now 2–3 years old; Wine 8 is ready to drink now and will hold for a further 2–3 years, while Wine 9's greater concentration and structure suggest it will improve for 8–10 years and hold considerably longer, developing honeyed, petrol-driven tertiary notes. Both are almost certainly fermented in stainless steel with no new oak and no malolactic, preserving the taut acidity — the decisive difference is harvest timing and resulting must weight rather than cellar technique, though Wine 9's greater textural richness suggests extended lees contact. Wine 8 suits by-the-glass and independent on-trade listings for early, refreshing drinking; Wine 9 targets specialist fine-wine retailers and sommelier lists, competing with premier Alsace and Mosel Riesling among collectors seeking age-worthy dry whites.
