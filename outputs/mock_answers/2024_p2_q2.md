# Mock answer — 2024 Paper 2 Question 2

## a) Identify the origin referencing the grape variety/ies used (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 4.** The pale-to-medium garnet colour with an already orange-brick rim, despite obvious tannic power, is the classic paradox of Nebbiolo — thin-skinned but ferociously tannic and high in acid. Aromas of dried rose, tar, potpourri and sour cherry confirm this over Sangiovese, which was considered but eliminated on the more savoury, tar-and-rose top note and the searing, almost saline acidity that Sangiovese rarely shows to this degree. The firm, drying, fine-grained tannin and 14.5% alcohol are consistent with Nebbiolo from Piedmont, and the neutral, non-vanilla oak treatment together with structured power over pure perfume points to Barolo DOCG rather than the more fragrant, earlier-drinking Barbaresco.

**Wine 5.** Bright ruby-garnet, still fairly deep for a wine of this age, with violets, sour cherry, dusty herbs and iron point to Sangiovese. Nebbiolo was ruled out — the tannin here is fine and dusty rather than tarry and coarse, and the colour holds more red than orange. Grenache was also considered given the ripe 14.5% alcohol, but the racier acid spine and savoury tobacco-iron note are more Sangiovese than Grenache's softer, sweeter fruit. The concentration and ripe warmth point to Tuscany's warmest Sangiovese zone, Montalcino, and the intensity and DOCG-level extraction (rather than the lighter, earlier-release Rosso di Montalcino) confirm Brunello di Montalcino.

**Wine 6.** Deep, dense purple-ruby — markedly darker than the two Italians — with cassis, mulberry, violets and graphite, plus a plush, velvety tannin, signals a Merlot-led blend. A Cabernet Sauvignon-dominant Left Bank style was eliminated: the fruit is riper and more red/blue-fruited than the blackcurrant-leaf, graphite-dominant profile of the Médoc, and the tannin, while substantial, is rounder. A violet lift alongside the core suggests a Cabernet Franc component. The limestone-plateau precision and generous new-oak toast point to Saint-Émilion, Bordeaux.

**Wine 7.** Medium garnet with a distinctly orange rim shows more development than the others despite similar or greater age. Coconut and dill from American oak, layered with leather, tobacco, dried orange and cedar, are the signature of Tempranillo rather than Grenache, which was considered but eliminated for lacking the savoury, leathery development and firmer structure here. The velvety, integrated tannin and moderate acid at 14% alcohol point to Rioja, Spain.

## b) Comment on the quality in the context of the origin (4 x 8 marks)

**Wine 4.** Very good to outstanding quality — concentrated, structured and true to a serious Barolo estate, though the slightly savoury, less opulent fruit reflects a difficult growing season; equivalent to a well-sited straight Barolo DOCG from a quality-focused grower, retailing around £45-60, rather than a riper cru bottling.

**Wine 5.** Outstanding quality — the density, ripe tannin and length are well above generic Brunello and place this at the top end of the DOCG, comparable to a leading estate's flagship cuvée; a £70-90 retail wine that over-delivers for the category.

**Wine 6.** Outstanding quality — the depth, precision and polish of tannin, allied to a long, earthy, mineral finish, are consistent with a leading Premier Grand Cru Classé rather than a generic Saint-Émilion Grand Cru; realistically £100-150 on release.

**Wine 7.** Outstanding quality — the seamless integration of oak and fruit, and the layered tertiary complexity without any dilution, mark this at the pinnacle of the Gran Reserva category from a historic, quality-obsessed bodega; a £80-120 wine that rivals many Riserva-level reds globally.

## c) Identify the vintage and suggest an ideal drinking window (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 4.** Primary red fruit still dominates, with only the first hints of dried fruit and liquorice emerging, and the fruit shows slightly less ripeness and mid-palate flesh than a warm Barolo vintage — consistent with the challenging, rain-affected 2018 rather than a riper year like 2015 or 2016. Needs a further 3-5 years for the tannins to soften, then holds for 15+ years, gaining tar and dried-rose complexity.

**Wine 5.** Ripe, concentrated fruit with slightly lower acid drive than a classically cool vintage points to the hot, dry 2017 growing season. Still youthful with primary black cherry dominant. Will improve for 2-4 years and hold for a further 8-10, developing more leather and spice.

**Wine 6.** Deep colour, ripe but present tannin and freshness preserved despite high alcohol are consistent with 2018's late-season heat following a wet start — a great modern Right Bank vintage. Needs 5-7 years to fully integrate and will hold for 25-30 years, evolving toward cedar and truffle.

**Wine 7.** The pronounced tertiary spice, leather and orange-rimmed colour reflect an extended traditional ageing regime — many years in cask followed by further bottle age before release — rather than simply an old vintage; this points to 2010, itself a benchmark year. Drink now through the next 6-8 years; unlikely to gain further complexity but will hold gracefully before the fruit fades.
