# Mock answer — 2022 Paper 3 Question 3

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 10.** Deep ruby-garnet, concentrated dried-cherry, fig and dark-chocolate aromas, sweet fruit and firm but ripe tannin at only 15% alcohol point to dried-grape (appassimento) concentration rather than botrytis, which would show an apricot/marmalade marker, or fortification, which would show spirit heat. The ripe, sweet tannin indicates a red grape base, funnelling toward Corvina-led Valpolicella. Retained sweetness against fresh acidity, rather than the dry, bitter-almond finish of the same drying process taken to completion, marks this as the sweet sibling of that style — Recioto rather than Amarone. The precision alongside the richness points to the historic Classico zone: Recioto della Valpolicella Classico DOCG, Veneto, Italy.

**Wine 11.** Tawny-amber colour with an orange-brick rim and a nutty, oxidative nose of dried fig, walnut and toffee rules out Ruby or Vintage Port, which would show deeper colour and primary black fruit, and rules out Sherry, which lacks this sweetness and spirit warmth. High alcohol at 19.5%, medium-sweet RS and an elegant, moderate finish are consistent with wood-aged fortified wine from a temperate, terroir-precise origin rather than a hot one — Tawny Port, Douro Valley, Portugal.

**Wine 12.** The same oxidative, tawny-amber family is evident, but alcohol reaches nearly 21%, riper, raisined dried-fruit and Christmas-cake intensity dominate, and a pronounced rancio note emerges — indicating a hotter, more concentrated fortified base than Wine 11. This heat signature, with broader, more caramelised richness than Douro tawny typically shows, points away from Europe (Port is a protected name) toward a warm New World tawny tradition: South Australia, most likely Barossa Valley.

## b) Comment on the key winemaking techniques (3 x 5 marks)

**Wine 10.** Grapes were dried on racks for three to four months, concentrating sugar; unlike Amarone, fermentation was halted before dryness — likely by chilling or filtration — to retain sweetness, then matured in old, neutral oak that preserves fruit purity over adding oak flavour.

**Wine 11.** Fortification with neutral grape spirit mid-fermentation arrested sugar conversion, fixing sweetness and lifting alcohol; maturation in small, seasoned oak pipes under oxidative conditions built the nutty profile, with fractional blending across vintages meaning "10 Year" denotes average age, not a single harvest.

**Wine 12.** The same fortify-then-oxidise principle applies, but longer, warmer barrel maturation and fractional blending account for the deeper rancio; the hotter climate likely gave a riper, higher-sugar base wine before fortification than in the Douro example.

## c) Comment on the style, quality, and commercial position (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 10.** A rich, sweet red table wine, not a fortified one — style depends on dried-grape concentration, not spirit or botrytis. The balance of sweetness, fresh acidity and structured tannin against 15% alcohol suggests very good quality, above basic Recioto and toward the top of Classico producers, retailing around $45–60. Suited to independent specialists and fine-dining lists, it competes with Banyuls or premium sweet reds and finds favour in North American and Northern European markets seeking a meal-friendly alternative to Port.

**Wine 11.** Classically elegant, medium-sweet, nutty and oxidative — refined rather than powerful. Purity of dried fruit, integration of spirit and length indicate very good quality, above entry 10-year blends, at around $30–40 retail. Positioned through independent merchants and premium supermarkets, it competes with aged Amontillado and travels well in UK, US and Benelux markets as an after-dinner, by-the-glass proposition.

**Wine 12.** Bigger, riper and more overtly rancio than Wine 11, reflecting Barossa heat rather than Douro precision — a legitimate but different, very good quality expression within the Australian tawny category, similarly priced around $30–40. It suits domestic Australian premium retail and UK/US export niches among consumers who enjoy the richer, more raisined style, competing with other Australian and South African fortified tawnies rather than Port itself.
