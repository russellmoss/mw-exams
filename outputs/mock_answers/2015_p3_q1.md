# Mock answer — 2015 Paper 3 Question 1

## a) Method of production, with particular reference to the role of yeast (4 x 15 marks)

Across the flight yeast performs four distinct jobs: creating bubbles, then dying and enriching the wine through autolysis, then living on as a protective biological veil over a fortified wine, and finally living on — more tentatively — as a veil over an unfortified one. That progression is the thread linking all four glasses.

**Wine 1.** The pale gold colour, fine persistent mousse and pronounced toasted brioche, fresh pastry and hazelnut character point to traditional method sparkling wine with substantial time on lees. The base wine, partly barrel-fermented, undergoes a second fermentation in bottle where yeast converts added sugar into alcohol and CO2, trapped under crown cap to build the mousse. The dead yeast cells then autolyse over several years, releasing mannoproteins that soften the acid line and give the creamy, biscuity depth here — the intensity of that character suggests seven-plus years on lees, consistent with a vintage wine disgorged not long before tasting. The very low, Extra Brut dosage leaves almost nothing to mask that autolytic complexity, so it reads as the dominant flavour driver rather than fruit or sweetness.

**Wine 2.** The pale salmon hue, delicate mousse and predominantly primary redcurrant and strawberry fruit, with only a whisper of yeasty bread character, point to the same biological process — bottle-fermented, yeast-driven mousse — but arrested much earlier. A non-vintage blend aged on lees for closer to the statutory minimum than a decade explains the far lighter autolytic imprint. The high, searching acidity and modest 11.5% alcohol are consistent with a marginal, cool maritime growing season rather than a warm Champenois one.

**Wine 3.** The pale gold-green colour, pungent aromas of fresh dough, green apple, camomile and saline tang, and the bone-dry, low-glycerol palate indicate biological rather than oxidative ageing — a living veil of yeast, or flor, covering the wine in cask. Fortification to around 15% is the critical production decision: it is judged precisely to sit at the ceiling flor can tolerate, keeping the yeast alive rather than killing it as higher fortification would. The flor consumes oxygen and glycerol and produces acetaldehyde, which explains the green-apple pungency and the wine's lean, saline drive; it also shields the wine from air, so there is no browning. Continuous refreshment through a criadera-solera system keeps that yeast culture active indefinitely.

**Wine 4.** Deep gold colour with waxy, honeyed quince and white peach sitting alongside a discreet walnut and curry-spice edge suggests barrel ageing with only partial, rather than continuous, exposure to a flor-type veil — retained fruit purity argues against a full oxidative regime. A true vin jaune requires a minimum of six years three months entirely un-topped under voile, developing far more pungent walnut, curry and aldehydic intensity than shown here; the fresher fruit and more moderate acidity point instead to a barrel regime that limits or interrupts that veil, giving complexity without full oxidative transformation.

## b) Quality and style (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1** is outstanding quality, above the house's standard NV Brut, comparable to a benchmark vintage/grower Champagne retailing around $70–90; bone dry, precise and drinking in a beautiful window now.

**Wine 2** is good, well-made commercial quality — an entry-level English sparkling rosé around $25–35 — fresh, light and fruit-driven, built for immediate drinking rather than complexity.

**Wine 3** is very good quality, a benchmark single-vineyard fino, around $20–25; bone dry, saline and intensely refreshing, but a style demanding immediate consumption once opened.

**Wine 4** is good to very good quality, a serious Jura Savagnin around $30–45 with real complexity and enough structure to improve further over the medium term.
