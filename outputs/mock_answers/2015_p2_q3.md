# Mock answer — 2015 Paper 2 Question 3

## a) Identify the origin and grape variety(ies) as closely as possible (4 x 10 marks)

The four wines split cleanly into two pairs on colour and structure alone. Wines 7 and 8 share a medium ruby-garnet core, sour red cherry and dried herb fruit, tomato-leaf and leather savouriness, and a firm, slightly rustic tannin alongside searing acidity — the high-acid, high-tannin combination that is Sangiovese's calling card, rather than the softer tannin of Merlot or the more red-fruited, lower-acid profile of Grenache, both considered and set aside. Wines 9 and 10 are strikingly different in colour for their tannic weight: pale-to-medium garnet with an orange-brick rim developing early, carrying tar, dried rose, anise and bitter dried cherry over ferociously drying tannin and equally piercing acidity. That mismatch between a light, translucent colour and a genuinely tannic palate is the flight's most distinctive marker and points directly to Nebbiolo — Pinot Noir was briefly considered for the pale colour but its tannin is far softer and its acidity less searing than what these two show. Old World handling (savoury, non-fruit-forward character, moderate 13.5-14% alcohols, no pyrazine or eucalyptus lift of the New World) confirms both pairs are European, and two Sangiovese wines sitting alongside two Nebbiolo wines lands the whole flight in Italy — no other country pairs these two varieties.

Within the Sangiovese pair, Wine 7's more open fruit and accessible tannin against Wine 8's greater structure, savoury depth and firmer grip point to a junior/senior split: Wine 7 as Chianti Classico, Tuscany, and Wine 8, with its concentrated fruit, longer finish and evident bottle development, as Brunello di Montalcino, Tuscany — Sangiovese's most demanding single-vineyard-driven DOCG. Within the Nebbiolo pair, Wine 9's lighter body and immediate drinkability sit below Wine 10's fuller body, polished but substantial tannin and greater complexity, pointing to Wine 9 as Langhe Nebbiolo and Wine 10 as Barolo, both Piedmont — the hierarchy repeating exactly the pattern seen in Tuscany.

## b) Discuss the method of production (4 x 5 marks)

Wine 7's fruit purity and moderate tannin refinement suggest destemmed fruit fermented in temperature-controlled steel with a two-week maceration; the savoury, non-fruit-driven oak signature and lack of vanilla or coconut point to ageing in large, neutral Slavonian botti rather than new barriques — a traditional approach releasing the wine within about four years of harvest. Wine 8 shows a comparable large-format, neutral oak signature but with markedly more concentration and structural extraction, consistent with longer maceration and the extended ageing Brunello's DOCG regulations require — a minimum of two years in wood and four years total before release, explaining why this 2009 shows more development than the 2011 Chianti Classico despite the earlier vintage. Wine 9's lighter body and softer, more immediate tannin suggest a shorter maceration and lighter extraction than the Barolo pair, aged briefly in large neutral oak or steel and released young under the declassified Langhe DOC, which carries no lengthy mandatory ageing. Wine 10's more polished, less rustic tannin against genuine concentration suggests shorter, temperature-controlled maceration (possibly rotary fermentation) followed by ageing that includes a portion of small French oak alongside larger barrels, consistent with a more modernist hand — reflected in its greater aromatic lift than the Langhe Nebbiolo, and with Barolo's minimum three years' ageing, eighteen months of which must be in wood.

## c) Comment on quality and maturity (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 7** — very good quality, upper Chianti Classico Annata level, comparable to a well-regarded single-estate bottling around £20. Good concentration and typical Sangiovese grip give above-average intensity for the tier, though it lacks a Riserva's depth. The medium ruby colour and still-primary fruit suggest a wine of four to five years old, approaching but not at its peak; it will hold for another two to three years with limited further improvement, best drunk over the next three to four years.

**Wine 8** — outstanding quality, comparable to a serious estate-level Brunello at around £55, showing the concentration, savoury complexity and long finish that justify DOCG's most demanding Sangiovese requirements. The garnet colour with early rim development and secondary tobacco and forest-floor notes indicate a wine of around six years old, only recently released and still tightly wound; it has real capacity to improve over the next five to eight years and will hold for a decade or more, gaining dried-fruit and truffle complexity.

**Wine 9** — good, commercial quality at Langhe DOC level, around £18, offering typicity and immediate appeal rather than profundity — Nebbiolo's entry point, not its statement. The pale, already browning colour and forward fruit indicate a wine for early drinking at two to three years old; it has little to gain from further ageing and should be enjoyed over the next one to two years before its fruit fades.

**Wine 10** — outstanding quality, equivalent to a serious, prestige-adjacent Barolo DOCG at around £60, from what tastes like an exceptional, structured vintage. The still-firm tannin and concentrated, complex fruit indicate a wine of only five years old, youthful and unresolved for the category; it needs a further eight to ten years to soften and develop the classic tar, rose and truffle complexity of mature Barolo, and will then hold for fifteen years or more.
