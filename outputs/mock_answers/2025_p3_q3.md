# Mock answer — 2025 Paper 3 Question 3

## a) Comment on the quality and commercial position with specific reference to its classification (6 x 15 marks)

**Wine 7.** A pale salmon-pink, direct-pressed rosé with real weight and a saline, textured finish rather than the thin, one-dimensional style of commodity pink — this points to a Cru Classé Côtes de Provence, one of only eighteen estates carrying the 1955 classification. The concentration, the layered red-fruit-through-exotic aromatics and the full body for the style (14% alcohol) place this above generic appellation level, at genuinely outstanding quality within the rosé category — comparable to the top handful of Cru Classé bottlings rather than mid-market Côtes de Provence. Commercially this belongs in Specialist independents and premium on-trade by-the-glass lists, retailing around $35–45, competing with other Cru Classé and prestige Provence blends, aimed at consumers who want gastronomic, food-friendly pink rather than poolside pink; the category ceiling on Provence rosé pricing remains a real commercial constraint even at this quality.

**Wine 8.** Golden colour, honeyed quince and mirabelle with a rich, semi-liquorous mid-palate signal a serious, concentrated white — the Grand Cru designation (restricted to four noble varieties on 51 named sites) is the classification to invoke here. Yields at half the appellation maximum and the depth of fruit confirm outstanding quality, on a par with the finest Grand Cru sites for the variety. Commercial position is a premium Specialist and fine-dining by-the-glass wine at $40–55, competing with top dry-leaning Austrian Smaragd Grüner Veltliner or rich Rheingau Spätlese; the main commercial challenge is that Alsace Grand Cru carries no mandatory sweetness statement, so the off-dry impression risks confusing a consumer expecting bone-dry Pinot Gris.

**Wine 9.** Deep gold, dense and sweet with dried apricot, marmalade and honey, but with the vivid acidity that separates fine Tokaj from simple dessert wine — the classification here is Édes Szamorodni, not Aszú, made from whole bunches carrying both healthy and botrytised berries rather than individually selected aszú berries. Very high sugar (190 g/L), full botrytis intensity and lifted acid put this at outstanding quality for the category, rivalling a serious 5–6 puttonyos Aszú in richness. It is a niche fine-wine proposition for Specialist merchants and sommelier-led restaurant lists, at $50–70 for a half bottle, competing with mid-tier Sauternes, aimed at collectors who understand Tokaj beyond the Aszú category.

**Wine 10.** Amber-gold, bone-dry and savoury with citrus peel, walnut and searing acidity — this is a 10 Years Old Sercial, Madeira's official age-indicated category, and clearly the driest of the noble grapes rather than a Bual or Malmsey. The precision, length and oxidative complexity mark it as very good to outstanding within dry Madeira, equivalent to a Reserva Velha tier for the variety. It sits as a Specialist and sommelier niche, priced $35–45, competing directly with aged dry Amontillado or dry Oloroso Sherry, and offers real growth potential in the food-pairing and mixology trade given its acid-driven, savoury profile.

**Wine 11.** Bright, youthful ruby with primary cherry and plum, soft sweetness and light tannin — a basic Ruby designation, the entry rung of the Port hierarchy, sitting below Reserve Ruby, LBV and Vintage. It is honest, commercial, good-quality Ruby but must not be over-called: it lacks the concentration and structure of Reserve or LBV and is nowhere near Vintage Port. Commercially this is a high-volume, broad-market wine retailing $12–18 through supermarkets and chain on-trade, sold by the glass or in long cocktails, competing with other basic Rubies and inexpensive VDN, aimed at the casual, entry-level fortified drinker.

**Wine 12.** Deep amber with a green rim, walnut, fig and caramelised dried fruit carrying real freshness and length rather than flat sweetness — an age-indicated 40 Year Old Tawny, the top of the IVDP average-age ladder. This is genuinely outstanding: the layered complexity and lifted acidity after decades in cask make it one of the most compelling wines in the flight and a pleasure to taste. It is a luxury, collector-driven wine for Specialist fine-wine merchants and top restaurant by-the-glass lists at $150–250, competing with fine aged Oloroso/Pedro Ximénez blends or a benchmark 20 Year Tawny, aimed at connoisseurs seeking a contemplative digestif, with strong export potential into UK, US and Asian luxury channels.

## b) Comment on the winemaking techniques (6 x 10 marks)

**Wine 7.** The pale hue and fresh red-fruit purity point to direct pressing or very brief skin contact of a multi-variety blend rather than saignée; the six components were likely vinified separately in temperature-controlled stainless steel to protect primary aromatics, then assembled post-fermentation. The creamy, saline texture without any oak spice suggests lees contact and battonage rather than barrel ageing as the source of mid-palate weight.

**Wine 8.** Ripe, botrytis-tinged aromatics and residual sweetness indicate late, selective hand-picking rather than an early, dry-style harvest; the naturally cold, high-altitude site checked fermentation before full dryness, leaving the off-dry impression. Ageing in large, neutral old-oak foudres — no vanilla or toast present — rounds texture without adding overt wood flavour, consistent with the traditional Alsatian large-cask approach.

**Wine 9.** Marmalade and honeyed depth confirm botrytis, but the textural density rather than pure concentration points to whole bunches of mixed healthy and botrytised fruit macerated for a day or so before pressing, rather than individually selected aszú berries. Slow, naturally arrested fermentation left the high residual sugar; two and a half years in small Hungarian oak added the light oxidative, spiced complexity while preserving racy acidity.

**Wine 10.** Bone-dry structure and high acidity indicate fruit picked at lower ripeness from a cooler, higher site, with fortification timed early to halt fermentation before much sugar accumulated. The walnut, smoke and volatile lift are signatures of slow, natural heating in cask over a decade — canteiro rather than the tank-heated estufagem used for younger commercial Madeira — followed by cask-blending to a consistent house style before bottling.

**Wine 11.** Deep, unbricked colour and soft tannin suggest a short, warm maceration in lagares or autovinifiers to extract colour and fruit quickly, followed by fortification with grape spirit part-way through fermentation to arrest it and retain sweetness. Only two to three years in large, neutral wood limits oxidative change, and the wine was likely cold-stabilised and filtered before bottling for immediate release rather than further ageing.

**Wine 12.** The amber colour, green rim and dried-fruit, nutty complexity confirm decades of slow oxidative ageing in small, seasoned casks rather than reductive development. Grapes were fortified with grape spirit during fermentation in the traditional way, then racked repeatedly over the decades, concentrating flavour through evaporation. The defining production decision is the final blending of multiple vintages and casks to the stated average age, with bottling only shortly before release to preserve stability.
