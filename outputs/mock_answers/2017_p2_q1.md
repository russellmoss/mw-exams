# Mock answer — 2017 Paper 2 Question 1

## a) Identify the common dominant grape variety (16 marks)

Cabernet Sauvignon. All four wines share a core of blackcurrant/cassis fruit with firm, fine-grained tannin and acidity that outweighs the fruit — the classic Cabernet architecture. Wine 1 shows it in fully resolved, savoury cedar-and-tobacco form after long age; Wines 3 and 4 show ripe cassis under firm tannin behind contrasting oak; Wine 2 shows a softer, diluted version of the same core. Merlot dominance was considered for the softer Wine 2, but the persistent firmness argues against it. Shiraz dominance was considered given its presence there too, but the absence of pepper, smoked meat and violet lift across the flight rules it out as the common thread. The consistent cassis-and-tannin backbone, present through very different climates and oak regimes, points to Cabernet Sauvignon.

## b) Identify the origin as closely as possible (4 x 8 marks)

**Wine 1** — St-Estèphe, Bordeaux, France. The brick-orange rim and savoury nose of cedar, cigar box, graphite and dried fruit point to a mature Old World Cabernet blend. Firm tannin, racy acidity and a restrained 12.5% alcohol indicate a cool, classic Left Bank vintage rather than a modern release. The particularly firm, almost austere grip — more clay-driven than Pauillac's gravelly finesse — points to St-Estèphe.

**Wine 2** — South Australia, Australia. Lifted eucalyptus, soft ripe tannin and a juicy, fruit-forward palate are hallmarks of warm Australian Cabernet, and the Shiraz component confirms a blending convention essentially unique to Australia. The absence of a defining single-site character — no Barossa richness or Coonawarra terra rossa — points to a broader, multi-regional South Australian blend rather than a named GI.

**Wine 3** — Alexander Valley, Sonoma, California. The unmistakable dill, coconut and vanilla of American oak, with 14.2% alcohol and a plush, generous texture, place this in California. Riper, rounder fruit than a leaner Napa benchmark, which was considered and eliminated, suggests the warmer Sonoma sub-region of Alexander Valley.

**Wine 4** — Stellenbosch, South Africa. Deep colour, cassis, pencil shavings and cedar suggest a Bordeaux-style blend, while riper fruit and higher alcohol than Wine 1 rule out Bordeaux itself. Fine, firm tannin and herbal, mineral lift — cooler and more structured than a Barossa or Napa comparator — are consistent with maritime Stellenbosch, cooled by False Bay.

## c) Identify the key winemaking techniques, with reference to other grape varieties and oak (4 x 6 marks)

**Wine 1** — The softened Cabernet core and plummy mid-palate weight suggest a meaningful proportion of Merlot, with a little Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot for lift and colour. The cedar and sweet spice, still perceptible after two decades, indicate maturation in French oak barriques, a significant proportion new, before extended bottle age.

**Wine 2** — The added plum and dark-berry richness confirms Shiraz as the blending partner, a distinctly Australian pairing rarely seen elsewhere. The light, slightly disjointed vanillin note suggests brief, inexpensive oak contact — staves, chips or large format rather than barrique — for a wine made for immediate release.

**Wine 3** — Purity of cassis fruit with minimal secondary complexity suggests largely varietal Cabernet Sauvignon, perhaps with a little Cabernet Franc. The pronounced dill, coconut and vanilla point to extended maturation in new American oak barriques, likely around two years, giving the wine its signature sweet-spice fingerprint.

**Wine 4** — Cassis lifted by violets and dried herbs, with a savoury, structured finish, is consistent with a classic blend of Merlot, Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot alongside dominant Cabernet. Integrated cedar and pencil-shaving spice suggest French oak barriques, moderate new wood, around 18 months, giving structure without masking the cooler, herbal fruit.

## d) Comment on quality and maturity (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 1** — Outstanding quality, equivalent to a classified growth St-Estèphe retailing upwards of $150. Layered savoury complexity, persistent length and still-present tannic grip after roughly twenty years reflect a great vintage. At its peak now, with little further improvement likely; will hold 3-5 years before fruit fades and structure dries.

**Wine 2** — Good quality, a well-made commercial wine, entry-level in style, around $12-15. Soft tannin and forward fruit deliver clean immediate pleasure but lack the length of a regional benchmark. No development potential; best drunk within 1-2 years before the fresh fruit fades.

**Wine 3** — Very good to excellent quality, premium AVA-designated Cabernet around $90-110. Concentration, ripe tannin and well-judged oak integration reflect a strong vintage. Still primary at around five years old, with depth to improve a further 5-8 years and hold 12-15 years beyond that.

**Wine 4** — Outstanding quality, the Cape's top-tier Bordeaux-style blend, comparable to Premier Cru-level Bordeaux, around $60-80. Fine tannin, savoury complexity and structured length reflect serious site selection. At around five years old and entering its secondary phase, with capacity to improve a further 5-10 years and hold 15-20 years, developing leather and dried-herb complexity.
