# Mock answer — 2026 Paper 2 Question 3

## Pair 1: Wines 7–8

### a) Origin (20 marks)

Both wines share a savoury, red-plum and dried-herb signature with firm but ripe tannin and racy acidity that points to Tempranillo before anything else — Garnacha alone would be paler and softer, Nebbiolo would show far higher acid and tar-rose intensity, neither fits. Wine 7's clove, vanilla and coconut-tinged spice confirm ageing in American oak, the house signature of a classic Rioja bodega blending Tempranillo across sub-zones with a splash of Garnacha for mid-palate flesh. Wine 8 is leaner on oak spice, more chalky and saline, with a single, precise fruit signature rather than a blended one — evidence of a single-parcel Tempranillo from limestone-clay soil rather than a multi-village assemblage. Both land in Rioja, DOCa Spain; Wine 7's blended, cellar-driven profile is consistent with Rioja Alta/Rioja Oriental fruit sourcing, while Wine 8's stonier, higher-altitude freshness points specifically to Rioja Alavesa.

### b) Style and quality (20 marks)

This pair is a traditional-versus-modern contrast, not one of quality per se. Wine 7 is the classicist: extended American-oak ageing, blended fruit and a plush, toasty, opulent frame place it squarely at Reserva level — very good quality, a benchmark house-style Reserva retailing around $35–45. Wine 8 rejects that template altogether: minimal new oak in large French foudre, a single named parcel and a fresher, more mineral, saline profile reflect the "vino de pueblo" movement's deliberate move away from the Crianza/Reserva ladder toward site transparency. Its absence of an official ageing classification is itself a style statement, not a quality shortfall — this reads as an excellent quality, boutique single-vineyard Tempranillo, comparable in seriousness to a fine Crianza or Reserva despite sidestepping the category, at a similar $30–40 price point.

### c) Maturity and vintage (10 marks)

Wine 7 shows moderate development against still-vibrant fruit and polished, integrated tannin — consistent with a concentrated, ripe vintage such as 2019, following a cool, uneven flowering that cut yields ahead of a dry, even summer; it is drinking well now but has real upside over the next 5–8 years. Wine 8's brighter, more youthful, almost effervescent freshness and less resolved oak point to a markedly younger wine, likely only two to three years from harvest, built by design for earlier, fresher drinking rather than long cellaring.

## Pair 2: Wines 9–10

### a) Origin (20 marks)

Sour cherry, dried herb and a dusty, fine-grained tannin over racy, mouth-watering acidity are the calling card of Sangiovese in both glasses — riper, softer Grenache and denser, tar-scented Nebbiolo are both eliminated on structure alone. The galestro-driven stoniness and balsamic lift place these in central Tuscany; Wine 10's greater fruit concentration, dried rose petal complexity and fine, integrated barrique spice confirm a top, single-cru site within the zone rather than the diluted, everyday Chianti found outside it. Both are Chianti Classico DOCG.

### b) Style and quality (20 marks)

Same vintage, same appellation — the contrast here is purely one of classification tier, not weather. Wine 9 is straightforward, fruit-forward and softly structured, aged in large, neutral oak with no vanilla or toast lift: a good quality, commercially styled Chianti Classico annata, entry-level within the DOCG and realistically priced around $12–15. Wine 10 shows markedly greater concentration and precision — firmer, finer tannin, a longer saline finish and a discreet dusting of barrique spice from a minority of new French oak — consistent with a single-vineyard Gran Selezione, the top rung of the Chianti Classico pyramid: outstanding quality, comparable to the region's leading estate crus, retailing around $70–75.

### c) Maturity (10 marks)

Despite sharing a vintage, the two show quite different developmental profiles because of structure, not age. Wine 9's soft tannin and open fruit suggest it is already at or near its peak, built for a short 5–7 year window from vintage. Wine 10 is tighter and more primary for its age, its greater extract and oak integration still resolving — it will improve over the next 4–6 years and hold considerably longer, into the mid-2030s.

## Pair 3: Wines 11–12

### a) Origin (20 marks)

Cassis, violet and graphite over firm, fine tannin identify a Cabernet Sauvignon-led Left Bank Bordeaux blend in both glasses — the perfumed, floral lift rather than plummier, rounder Merlot-driven fruit rules out the Right Bank. Within the Left Bank, the aromatic, violet-scented perfume allied to elegant rather than blunt tannin — more refined than the denser cassis and tobacco typical of Pauillac or St Julien — points specifically to Margaux, the most aromatically lifted and structurally polished of the Left Bank communes. Wine 11's lighter colour, softer tannic grip and slightly earlier-drinking feel are consistent with a Third Growth (3ème Cru Classé); Wine 12's denser purple-black hue, greater new-oak integration and structural depth point to a Second Growth (2ème Cru Classé).

### b) Style and quality (20 marks)

Both are classified Margaux, but the classification gap is audible in the glass. Wine 11 is elegant and already approachable, with polished but lighter tannin and moderate concentration — very good quality, true to Third Growth level, retailing around $70–90. Wine 12 shows noticeably greater depth of fruit, a more complete tannic structure and finer oak integration (a higher proportion of new barriques is evident from the more pronounced sandalwood-spice frame) — outstanding quality, comparable to the finest Second Growths, at a materially higher $150–200 price point that reflects both site and ambition.

### c) Maturity and vintage (10 marks)

This pair is the vintage tell. Wine 11's already-developing garnet rim, moderate 13% alcohol and softer, more resolved tannin point to a lighter, frost-reduced year such as 2017, where an April frost cut yields ahead of a dry summer, giving healthy but less concentrated fruit; it is already drinking well and best suited to a 6–8 year window rather than decades of cellaring. Wine 12's deep, youthful purple-black colour and dense, still-primary tannin despite similar bottle age argue for an outstanding, structurally complete vintage such as 2016, where a wet winter recharged reserves ahead of a warm, dry summer and a cool September that preserved acidity — this is a wine still assembling itself, needing several more years before its peak and capable of holding for decades. The under-the-skin tell is that both wines share an identical Margaux perfume signature yet diverge entirely on density and freshness — the surest sign that origin is constant and vintage is doing all the work.
