# Mock answer — 2025 Paper 1 Question 4

## a) Style: relative importance of human inputs versus natural factors (3 x 15 marks)

**Wine 10.** Pale lemon-green with a green-tinged rim, aromas of green apple, white peach and grapefruit zest sit over a distinct wet-slate, mineral undertone. The palate is off-dry, with racy, almost searing acidity and a light body at only 11.5% alcohol. This profile is overwhelmingly natural-factor driven: a steep, south/east-facing slope and a cool, continental river-valley mesoclimate govern a slow, marginal ripening that delivers low sugar accumulation and retained malic acidity, while slate soils impart the stony, mineral tang. Human input is confined to picking-date selection and arresting fermentation to leave a little sugar, with a neutral stainless-steel vessel doing nothing to mask the site. Natural factors clearly dominate here.

**Wine 11.** Deep golden colour signals significant age and oxidative handling. Baked pear, quince, cigar-box spice, nutmeg and toasted vanilla sit over a creamy, structured palate with vibrant, salty acidity and real length. The variety and site supply the raw material, but the dominant sensory signature — oxidative development, spice, toast, polish — is the product of a long, deliberate élevage in mixed-age barrel followed by extended bottle age before release. This is a human-input-dominant style: a naturally robust, moderate-acid grape gave the winemaker licence to push oxidative maturation this far without the wine falling apart, but it is intervention, not terroir, that defines what is in the glass.

**Wine 12.** Amber to tawny colour with a concentrated, almost vermouth-like nose of dried fig, apricot jam, walnut and orange peel, full-bodied and lusciously sweet yet carried by remarkable, cleansing acidity at 13% alcohol. This is the most human-input-driven style of the three: post-harvest drying of whole bunches concentrates sugar and dehydrates the fruit long before fermentation begins, and years of oxidative maturation in small, part-filled casks under a resident yeast culture build the nutty, dried-fruit complexity. A warm, ripe vintage supplied healthy sugar levels and retained acidity, but the finished character is essentially artisanal and labour-built rather than site-expressive.

## b) Grape variety and origin (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 10.** Riesling. The green apple, peach and grapefruit aromatics, racy acidity, off-dry balance and low 11.5% alcohol point directly to Riesling; Sauvignon Blanc was considered but ruled out by the absence of pyrazine-driven herbaceousness and its typically drier, less sweetened style. The slate-derived minerality and delicate, low-alcohol structure indicate a cool German site, and the combination of grey-slate character with a feinherb (off-dry) balance narrows this specifically to the Mittelmosel, around the Mosel's mid-river slopes.

**Wine 11.** A Viura-led white blend, likely with a small proportion of Malvasia and Garnacha Blanca, given white Burgundy was considered and eliminated — the register here is more overtly oxidative and spice-driven, with a higher acid-to-fruit ratio than Meursault or Puligny typically show. The structured, traditional style with extensive oak and bottle age before release, combined with the golden colour and spice complexity, points to Rioja, specifically Rioja Alta, consistent with a long-aged Gran Reserva white.

**Wine 12.** The dried-fruit intensity, amber colour and oxidative, nutty complexity point to a dried-grape, cask-aged sweet style rather than botrytis (no honeyed, glycerol-rich unctuousness) or a fortified sweet wine (alcohol too low for a Vin Doux Naturel). This funnels to a passerillage-based, oxidatively aged style from central Italy — Trebbiano-based, from Tuscany, most specifically Chianti Rufina.
