# Mock answer — 2017 Paper 1 Question 5

Both wines share a pure citrus core and no tropical or confected fruit, marking cool-climate Chardonnay rather than a warm New World source and confirming Burgundy as the shared family.

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible (2 x 10 marks)

**Wine 11.** Medium lemon-gold colour, hazelnut, butter and toasted notes, and a full, broad, textured palate at 13.5% alcohol point south on the Côte d'Or to Meursault rather than Puligny or Chassagne-Montrachet, whose flintier, more citrus-driven register this wine lacks. The concentration and persistent finish sit above village Meursault, indicating Premier Cru — around £75-90 retail.

**Wine 12.** Pale lemon colour, oyster-shell, chalk and green-apple aromas, and a taut, racy palate at 12.5% are classic Kimmeridgian-limestone markers, confirming Chablis over Chardonnay grown further south in the Côte de Beaune or Chalonnaise. Mid-palate concentration, likely from old vines, sits above Petit Chablis, but the finish lacks the extended chalky drive of Premier or Grand Cru, pointing to village Chablis, around £25-30.

## b) Identify the vintage and consider the capacity to improve in the bottle (2 x 8 marks)

**Wine 11.** The still-fresh acid line against a hint of early golden development suggests a wine of around four years old rather than older, consistent with 2013 — a small, late, hail-affected Côte de Beaune vintage that produced fresher, more backward, higher-acid whites than the riper 2012. Not yet at its peak, it has the structure to improve for a further 4-5 years, gaining nutty, honeyed complexity, and should hold for a decade from vintage.

**Wine 12.** The pristine primary citrus fruit and precise, uncluttered acid line show no oxidative development, consistent with a wine of two to three years old from 2014, one of Chablis' outstanding recent vintages, combining ripe fruit with real tension. It will improve for a further 3-4 years, developing more flinty, saline complexity, and hold for 5-6 years beyond that.

## c) Discuss the method of production with particular reference to the use of oak (2 x 7 marks)

**Wine 11.** The toasted, buttery, creamy texture and integrated vanilla-spice notes point to fermentation and maturation in French oak *pièces*, with a meaningful proportion new — around 25-30% — sufficient to season without masking fruit. The soft, rounded mid-palate and lack of any green, applely edge indicate full malolactic fermentation, with lees stirring adding to the creamy weight.

**Wine 12.** The complete absence of toast, vanilla or oak spice, alongside the wine's precise, saline drive, points to fermentation and ageing in stainless steel or large neutral wood rather than barrique, a choice made specifically to protect the mineral character. The concentration here comes from old-vine fruit rather than oak-derived richness, and malolactic fermentation appears only partial, preserving the racy, appley acidity that defines the style.
