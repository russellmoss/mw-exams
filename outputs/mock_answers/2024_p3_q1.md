# Mock answer — 2024 Paper 3 Question 1

## a) Discuss the quality, winemaking, and style

**Wine 1.** The fine, persistent bead together with pronounced brioche, marzipan and baked-apple autolytic notes point unambiguously to traditional method rather than tank-fermented fizz. The deep gold colour, broad richness and integration of toasty, biscuity complexity into the fruit rather than sitting on top of it indicate an extended second fermentation and lees-ageing period well beyond the two-year minimum — closer to a decade — with a portion of the base wine barrel-fermented, judging by the layered vanilla and custard notes alongside the citrus and tropical fruit. The stem excludes Champagne; the riper, more generous fruit core and fuller body than a comparable Champenois blend suggest a warmer New World source, most plausibly California. This is outstanding quality, on a par with a top Champagne prestige cuvée, retailing at $90–150.

**Wine 2.** Straw-yellow with a fine, persistent mousse again confirms traditional method, but the lighter, more citrus- and orchard-fruit-driven profile with only modest brioche and almond development points to a shorter lees regime than Wine 1 — around 24 months, consistent with a Reserva-level ageing statement. Bone-dry with no perceptible sweetness confirms a Brut Nature, zero-dosage style; the racy acidity, saline, earthy mineral finish and non-Chardonnay orchard-fruit character (rather than the riper, richer profile of Wine 1) point away from Franciacorta toward Spain's native varieties, i.e. Cava from Penedès. Very good quality, Reserva Brut Nature level, retailing around $18–25.

## b) Compare and contrast the commercial opportunities and challenges

Wine 1 sits at a luxury price point, best placed through fine-dining on-trade lists and independent fine-wine specialists rather than supermarkets; its target consumer is the affluent enthusiast seeking a non-Champagne trophy fizz, competing directly with prestige Champagne cuvées and top Franciacorta. Its challenge is brand recognition — without Champagne's regional cachet it must rely on cellar-door and critic scores to justify the price, and export beyond the US domestic market (UK, Japan) will be a slower build.

Wine 2 occupies the value-with-quality niche, well suited to premium supermarkets and independent specialists at $18–25, appealing to consumers who want a genuinely bone-dry, food-friendly sparkling alternative to entry Champagne or Crémant. Its opportunity is riding Cava's improving quality reputation in the UK, US and Germany; its challenge is shaking off Cava's historic bulk, discount-driven image and differentiating this estate-grown, zero-dosage style from mass-market Cava on the same shelf.
