# Mock answer — 2019 Paper 2 Question 1

## a) Origin and grape variety (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** Medium ruby with a garnet rim showing some development. Ripe red plum, dried cherry, cedar, tobacco and earthy undergrowth on the nose, with soft, approachable tannins, moderate acidity and 13.5% alcohol. The plummy fruit and gentle tannin structure point to Merlot as the lead variety rather than Cabernet Sauvignon, which is ruled out by the absence of firm, dense tannin and cassis/graphite lift. The savoury, earthy register and moderate rather than searing acidity indicate an Old World, cool-influenced site — Bordeaux's Right Bank. The wine lacks the depth and grip of classified Saint-Émilion, pointing instead to one of the satellite appellations north of the commune: Lussac-Saint-Émilion.

**Wine 2.** Deep ruby-purple with minimal development. Blackcurrant, dark cherry, cassis, violets, cigar box and graphite, with dense, fine-grained tannin, good acidity and 13.5% alcohol. The cassis-and-graphite profile, firm structure and 100% new-oak spice point to Cabernet Sauvignon as the dominant variety — eliminating Merlot, whose softer tannin and plummier fruit this wine does not show. The savoury, herbal, cigar-box lift and racier acid line than a warm New World Cabernet suggest an Old World, Mediterranean-influenced origin rather than Bordeaux itself, whose typical structural restraint this wine's density and extraction exceed. This points to Tuscany, made outside DOCG rules given the Cabernet-dominant, non-Sangiovese composition — an IGT Toscana "Super Tuscan."

**Wine 3.** Medium ruby-purple with a violet, youthful edge. Bright raspberry, redcurrant and Morello cherry with pronounced green pepper, pencil shavings and flinty herbal lift; medium body, fine grippy tannin, lively acidity and 13% alcohol. The pyrazine-driven, leafy, red-fruited profile is textbook Cabernet Franc, ruling out both Merlot and Cabernet Sauvignon on aromatic and structural grounds. The freshness, lighter body and pronounced acidity point to a cool climate rather than Bordeaux's warmer Right Bank — the Loire Valley, and specifically Saumur-Champigny, the appellation built on varietal Cabernet Franc from tuffeau limestone.

**Wine 4.** Deep ruby-purple with early brick hints. Blackcurrant, black cherry, cedar, tobacco leaf, dark chocolate and graphite, full-bodied with firm ripe tannin, good acidity and 14.5% alcohol, with well-integrated new oak. The cassis-and-cedar core again signals Cabernet Sauvignon-led, but riper tannin and higher alcohol than Wine 2 rule out a cool Mediterranean site. A fynbos-adjacent herbal lift alongside the cassis, combined with the ripeness and structure, points to a warm New World origin rather than Bordeaux — Stellenbosch, South Africa, on granite-derived soils.

## b) Quality in context, maturity and development (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** Good quality, typical of the value-driven satellite tier — well below a classified Saint-Émilion, retailing around £12–15. The garnet rim and soft tannin at seven years show early secondary development; this is a drink-now wine with little further upside, holding for perhaps 1–2 more years before the fruit fades.

**Wine 2.** Outstanding quality, on a par with a leading Super Tuscan or a classed-growth Bordeaux equivalent — around £80–120 retail. At seven years the wine is still primary-to-secondary, with the density and structure to improve for a further 5–8 years and hold for 10–15, developing more leather, dried herb and cedar complexity.

**Wine 3.** Very good quality, at the top of Saumur-Champigny's range — around £15–20, above generic village Cabernet Franc. The vibrant primary fruit at five years shows this is approaching its peak; it will hold rather than improve much further, best drunk over the next 2–3 years before the freshness that defines its typicity fades.

**Wine 4.** Outstanding quality, an icon-tier Cape red comparable to a Bordeaux classed growth — around £50–70 retail. At seven years the wine shows only the first hints of brick at the rim; the concentration and firm, ripe tannin give it 5–10 more years of improvement and a holding plateau of 15–20 years, developing dried fruit, leather and tobacco.

## c) Key winemaking techniques (4 x 5 marks)

**Wine 1.** Soft, gentle tannin and modest concentration suggest a short cuvaison with restrained extraction, fermented in stainless steel or concrete; the absence of overt toast or vanilla points to neutral, older-barrel or minimal oak ageing at this commercial tier.

**Wine 2.** Dense, fine-grained tannin with well-integrated vanilla and spice indicate careful, gentle extraction — likely gravity-fed, with punch-downs rather than harsh pump-overs — followed by extended ageing in a high proportion of new French barriques.

**Wine 3.** The purity of primary red fruit and pyrazine freshness point to whole-berry or lightly crushed fruit, cool fermentation in stainless steel to protect aromatics, and minimal-to-no oak ageing, preserving the varietal, mineral-edged style.

**Wine 4.** Firm, ripe tannin with polished cedar and spice suggest careful berry sorting and small-lot fermentation, followed by extended maturation in predominantly new French oak barriques to match the wine's fruit concentration and structure.
