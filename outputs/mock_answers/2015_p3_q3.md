# Mock answer — 2015 Paper 3 Question 3

## a) Alcohol level and b) residual sugar band (4 x 2 + 4 x 2 marks)

**Wine 9** — 8.5% abv; RS roughly 60–90 g/L. **Wine 10** — 9.5% abv; RS roughly 200–250 g/L, by far the sweetest of the four. **Wine 11** — 11.5% abv; RS roughly 120–150 g/L. **Wine 12** — 13.0% abv; RS roughly 150–200 g/L. The four figures already tell a story before origin is considered: a low-alcohol, moderately sweet wine (9); an extremely low-alcohol but searingly sweet wine (10); and two higher-alcohol, richly sweet wines (11 and 12) where fermentation ran further before sugar arrested it.

## c) Origin (4 x 5 marks)

**Wine 9** — Mosel, Germany. Pale green-gold, a delicate floral, orchard-fruit nose and the first kerosene hints point to Riesling; the off-dry, low-alcohol, piercingly high-acid structure rules out a dry Alsace or Austrian style. Steep, slate-driven minerality over a light body is textbook Mittelmosel rather than the broader, riper Pfalz.

**Wine 10** — Ontario, Canada. Very low alcohol with extreme, syrupy sweetness and pure, un-botrytised tropical fruit rules out a noble-rot style (no honey, saffron or marmalade) and points to freeze-concentration. Riesling's petrol undercurrent beneath the mango and lychee, with New World purity of fruit, is consistent with Canadian icewine rather than Austrian or German Eiswein, which usually shows more restraint at this RS level.

**Wine 11** — Tokaj, Hungary. Deep amber-gold, dried-apricot, marmalade and saffron aromatics against a searing streak of acidity are the calling card of botrytised Furmint. Moderate-high alcohol with oxidative, aged-oak character eliminates Sauternes, whose Sémillon base is waxier and less acid-driven, and points specifically to Tokaji Aszú.

**Wine 12** — Tuscany, Italy. Deep amber colour and nutty-oxidative aromas of roasted almond, toffee and dried fig, with a moderate 13% alcohol too high for botrytis or icewine yet clearly unfortified, point to a dried-grape wine matured oxidatively. The bitter-orange, walnut finish from long ageing in small sealed wood identifies a Tuscan Vin Santo rather than Recioto di Soave, whose fresher fruit and lower alcohol would not fit.

## d) Method of production, vineyard to bottle (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 9.** Moderate ripeness, above Kabinett but well short of a botrytised style, points to a late-picked, fully ripe but clean harvest — a Prädikat level defined purely by must weight at picking. The low 8.5% alcohol with unbotrytised orchard fruit indicates fermentation was arrested naturally by chilling, leaving the residual sugar rather than any addition; the absence of oak or buttery character points to fermentation in a neutral vessel with no malolactic — the wine's precision comes from site and picking date alone.

**Wine 10.** Pure, un-botrytised fruit alongside intense sweetness at very low alcohol indicates the grapes were left on the vine into winter and pressed while still frozen — ice crystals excluded from the must, concentrating sugar and acid in a tiny juice yield. The slow, cold fermentation that follows naturally stalls at low alcohol given the extreme starting sugar, explaining the sweetness without any deliberate arrest. No oak, no botrytis — the concentration here is purely physical, from freezing.

**Wine 11.** Intense botrytis character (apricot jam, saffron, beeswax) alongside high sweetness and lifted acidity points to individually selected, botrytis-affected berries macerated in must or base wine before pressing — a labour-intensive, hand-picked process rather than a single harvest pass. The oxidative, nutty overlay and moderate alcohol suggest slow fermentation followed by ageing in old, neutral oak for several years. The RS level here sits at a legally defined minimum tier rather than a free winemaking choice.

**Wine 12.** Dried-fruit concentration, elevated alcohol relative to the other three wines, and pronounced nutty-oxidative character together suggest grapes dried after harvest rather than picked overripe or frozen — bunches laid out to raisin before a slow fermentation of the concentrated must. The caramelised, toffee-almond profile indicates extended ageing in small, sealed old wooden vessels subject to temperature swings, encouraging oxidation and Maillard development; retained character across vintages is consistent with a continuous starter culture. Unlike Wine 11's biological concentration, this is raisining — physical dehydration, not fungal.

## e) Quality and maturity (4 x 6 marks)

**Wine 9** — very good quality, top single-vineyard Spätlese level, retailing around £20–25. At seven years it is entering its drinking window, with early kerosene notes signalling the start of tertiary development; the acid spine allows improvement for a further 5–8 years and holding for a decade beyond that.

**Wine 10** — outstanding quality, benchmark-level Canadian icewine retailing around £40–50 for a half-bottle. Still youthful and primary at three years, it will hold its fruit intensity for a decade or more given the preservative effect of its acid and sugar.

**Wine 11** — outstanding quality, a well-aged 5 Puttonyos retailing around £35–45 for a half-bottle. Already showing early-maturity complexity at seven years, it has ample sugar and acid to continue developing for 15–20 years.

**Wine 12** — very good to outstanding quality for the appellation, retailing around £30–40. A full decade of cask ageing has brought it to full maturity now, with little further to gain, though the oxidative style will hold this plateau for several more years without decline.
