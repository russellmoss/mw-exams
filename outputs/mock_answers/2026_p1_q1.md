# Mock answer — 2026 Paper 1 Question 1

Across the flight, the common thread of citrus-to-stone-fruit fruit, a green-apple/pear core, retained natural acidity even in the riper examples, and a consistent affinity for barrel fermentation, lees contact and malolactic softening points to a single, internationally adaptable variety capable of both lean, mineral expression and rich, oak-driven texture: Chardonnay. Alternatives such as Chenin Blanc or Semillon were considered for the riper wines but ruled out by the absence of lanolin/wax or waxy-herbaceous notes respectively, and by the consistent buttery-toasty complexing seen across several wines that is far more typical of barrel-worked Chardonnay.

## a) Origin (6 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** Pale gold; citrus and white peach over a saline, flinty minerality with a creamy but taut, limestone-driven texture and a long, layered finish at 13.5% abv. This is unmistakably Old World and, given the chalky-saline minerality married to real textural richness rather than pure linearity, Burgundy's Côte de Beaune rather than Chablis (contrast Wine 5, below). The weight, integrated rather than dominant oak, and gently creamy sur-lie texture without excess butter point specifically to Meursault; the depth and length suggest a well-sited village or premier climat lieu-dit rather than a simple Bourgogne Blanc, but it lacks the extra layering and grip of Grand Cru intensity.

**Wine 2.** Bright lemon and pink grapefruit interwoven with tropical top notes, a pronounced flinty, struck-match reduction, and a long, dry, mineral finish at 14% abv and racy acidity. The reductive signature alongside genuine fruit sweetness rules out a warmer, less reductive New World read; South Africa was considered but its Chardonnays typically show a more granitic, less overtly flinty tension. The combination of tropical lift against taut acid is most consistent with Marlborough, New Zealand.

**Wine 3.** Lemon-straw colour; nectarine, peach and persimmon fruit layered with vanillin spice, cedar and nougat, carried by bright, well-retained acid despite generous ripeness, at 14% abv. The riper fruit spectrum signals a warm New World site, but the acid retention and restraint (rather than blowsy over-ripeness) argue for a maritime-tempered rather than continental warm climate — pointing to Margaret River, Western Australia, in contrast to the more overt, simpler style of Wine 6.

**Wine 4.** Pale straw-green with peach, melon, citrus and floral lift over a mineral undertow; clean, precise, medium-bodied, 12% abv with lively acid and no oak. The low alcohol, floral top note and stainless-steel purity point to a cool, high-altitude Old World site; the more floral, peach-led register (rather than saline/oyster-shell, cf. Wine 5) and elevated freshness are consistent with mountain vineyards in Alto Adige, northern Italy.

**Wine 5.** Green apple, lemon zest, oyster shell and crushed stone; bone-dry, saline and crystalline with piercing acidity, no oak, at 12.5% abv. Set beside Wine 1's riper, creamier profile, this leaner, more linear expression with an intensely saline, high-toned edge points to Chablis rather than the Côte de Beaune. The concentration relative to a light-medium frame suggests a low-yielding vintage; the precision without the extra layering of a top climat places this at village level.

**Wine 6.** Deep gold; ripe stone fruit and baked lemon under pronounced vanilla, butter and caramel, smooth and off-dry at 13.5% abv. The overtly sweet-oak-driven, soft style with no discernible mineral or site signature is a New World commercial marker; the generic ripeness without a defined cool-climate identity points to a broad Californian sourcing rather than a specific AVA — Central Coast, USA.

## b) Winemaking (6 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** The mixed fermentation across tank and barrel (roughly 20% new oak) explains why oak reads as integrated rather than dominant. Wild-yeast fermentation and sur-lie ageing with light lees stirring build the creamy texture without overt butter, while the retained limestone-driven tension suggests a disciplined, not heavy-handed, approach to malolactic softening — a restrained winemaking choice that lets the Narvaux climat's stony, limestone character speak rather than being masked by wood.

**Wine 2.** Hand-picked, whole-bunch pressing with minimal maceration explains the fruit's purity; spontaneous fermentation in barrel is the clear source of the pronounced reductive, struck-match character. Full malolactic fermentation converts the naturally high acid into a rounder, textural mid-palate, and extended lees ageing with batonnage (around 11 months) reinforces this weight; screwcap closure preserves both the primary fruit and the reductive edge rather than allowing it to blow off in bottle.

**Wine 3.** A multi-vineyard blend explains the breadth of fruit; a split fermentation across stainless steel and seasoned French oak, with only partial malolactic conversion, accounts for the wine retaining real acid tension alongside barrel-derived spice and nutty complexity rather than becoming fully broad and soft. Regular batonnage over roughly eight to ten months builds the nougat-like richness, while moderate, largely non-new oak keeps the wood register as a complexing spice rather than a dominant flavour.

**Wine 4.** Soft, whole-cluster pressing preserves the floral lift and fruit purity. Fermentation entirely in temperature-controlled stainless steel explains the total absence of any oak signature. The undimmed, lively acidity — rather than a softened, creamy mouthfeel — suggests malolactic fermentation was blocked or only partial, a deliberate choice to protect freshness at altitude; short lees contact before early bottling and filtration explains the clean, precise profile with no bready or creamy weight.

**Wine 5.** The total absence of oak or buttery texture and the crystalline, linear finish point to a neutral-vessel fermentation with no new wood. The bright, undimmed, malic-edged acidity suggests malolactic fermentation was blocked or only partial, preserving precision; any underlying creamy note implies modest, non-batonnage lees contact. The intensity relative to the wine's light-medium frame is consistent with concentrated fruit from a low-yielding, frost-affected vintage.

**Wine 6.** The dominant, front-to-back vanilla, butter and caramel register signals winemaking built to a house style rather than to reveal a site. Full malolactic fermentation explains the smooth, low-acid roundness; oak ageing, likely with a significant new component and a sweeter, more overtly toasted character than the French-oak wines in this flight, drives the dominant flavour. A touch of perceptible sweetness rounds the palate further, consistent with large-scale blending engineered for immediate, consistent richness rather than terroir transparency.

## c) Commercial position (6 x 5 marks)

**Wine 1.** Fine-wine independent specialists and premium restaurant lists, retailing around $60-90; targets Burgundy collectors and by-the-glass premium diners, competing with village-level Puligny-Montrachet, exported chiefly to the US, UK and Japan.

**Wine 2.** Independent specialists and premium on-trade by-the-glass, around $35-45; appeals to New Zealand enthusiasts seeking serious barrel-fermented Chardonnay beyond Sauvignon Blanc, competing with premium Sonoma Coast or Margaret River Chardonnay across UK, US and domestic markets.

**Wine 3.** Premium supermarkets and casual on-trade by-the-glass, around $28-35; a step-up purchase for consumers trading up from commodity Chardonnay, competing with premium Californian or Chilean examples at a similar price across Australian, UK and US channels.

**Wine 4.** Independent specialists and casual on-trade by-the-glass, around $16-20; suits food-friendly, unoaked-style drinkers as an alternative to Chablis or Sauvignon Blanc, with strong US importer distribution alongside its domestic Italian market.

**Wine 5.** Independent specialists and seafood-focused restaurant lists, around $40-50 given the reduced 2021 yields; targets Chablis specialists, competing with village Chablis from larger négociant houses across the US, UK and French domestic trade.

**Wine 6.** National chain and supermarket off-trade, around $16-20; a mass-market, recognisably "buttery" style for consumers seeking easy, oak-forward richness, competing with other large-brand US Chardonnays at the same price point, with growing export volume.
