# Mock answer — 2025 Paper 3 Question 1

## a) Identify the grape variety with reference to all three wines (12 marks)

Riesling. A searing acid line unifies all three glasses despite very different sugar and alcohol levels — present under Wine 1's dosage at 11.5%, carrying Wine 2's dry style at 12%, and cutting through Wine 3's 155 g/L residual sugar without cloying. Each sits on a shared citrus-blossom axis: tangerine and nectarine (Wine 1), mandarin pith and honeysuckle (Wine 2), marmalade and jasmine with an emerging kerosene topnote (Wine 3). Muscat was considered for Wine 3's honeyed lift, but its blousier, grapier intensity and softer acid don't fit; the unbroken acid spine across every sugar level is the decisive proof of Riesling.

## b) Identify the origin as closely as possible (3 x 7 marks)

**Wine 1** — Germany, Mosel, Middle Mosel. The fine, persistent mousse, citrus-pith and blossom purity, moderate 11.5% alcohol and noticeably off-dry dosage point to German Sekt rather than Champagne-method sparkling elsewhere; Alsace Crémant was considered but is rarely single-varietal Riesling at this sweetness. The racy, slate-toned acid narrows the call to the Mosel.

**Wine 2** — New Zealand, Marlborough. The riper, mouth-filling citrus and stone fruit, textural creaminess and generous 12% alcohol point away from cool-climate Europe toward a sunnier New World site. Clare or Eden Valley Australia was considered, but their bone-dry, lime-cordial style doesn't match this wine's off-dry finish and beeswax-honeysuckle intensity, signatures of Marlborough's long sunshine and wide diurnal range.

**Wine 3** — Germany, Mosel. Deep golden colour and marmalade-honey botrytis concentration balanced by an electric, high-toned lime finish and low 7.5% alcohol point to a German Prädikat sweet wine. Austrian Burgenland Beerenauslese was considered, but the piercing, saline slate minerality cutting through such intense sugar is the Mosel's calling card, distinct from riper Austrian botrytis wines.

## c) Comment on the winemaking techniques (3 x 5 marks)

**Wine 1** — The persistent bead with a light bready note suggests bottle fermentation via the transfer method with brief lees contact. The palate sweetness confirms Extra Dry dosage — a level sweeter than Brut, not drier as the name might mislead the unwary taster.

**Wine 2** — No phenolic bitterness points to whole-bunch pressing and predominantly stainless-steel fermentation preserving primary aromatics; the subtle textural creaminess suggests a small barrel-fermented portion and extended lees contact.

**Wine 3** — The marmalade concentration required individually selected, late-harvested botrytised berries; a slow, cool fermentation was arrested at 7.5% alcohol, leaving most of the natural sugar unfermented and the aromatics intact.

## d) Comment on the style and quality (3 x 9 marks)

**Wine 1** — A sparkling, off-dry, entry-level commercial style. Good quality, equivalent to a well-made Qualitätswein-level Sekt rather than a prestige cuvée: correct effervescence and clean fruit give reasonable length, though it lacks the toasted depth of a longer lees-aged wine. Retailing around $15-18, it suits by-the-glass and off-trade sale rather than a serious aperitif list.

**Wine 2** — A still, off-dry style built for texture and aromatic intensity rather than austerity. Very good quality, comparable to a top single-vineyard estate bottling rather than generic commercial New Zealand Riesling — layered honeyed fruit and creamy mid-palate weight give real complexity and length. Retailing around $25-30, it competes with premium Clare Valley or Alsace Riesling.

**Wine 3** — An intensely sweet, botrytis-driven dessert style. Naming the tier precisely — Beerenauslese, above Auslese and below Trockenbeerenauslese — this is outstanding quality, a benchmark whose concentration, balance and long finish sit near the top of its tier. Twenty-plus years of ageing potential; likely retailing $60-80 for a half bottle given the minute yields botrytis demands.
