# Mock answer — 2019 Paper 1 Question 1

## a) Identify the common grape variety (20 marks)

Sauvignon Blanc runs through all four glasses, though it appears alone in three of them and in a blend in the fourth. The recurring evidence is a pyrazine-driven herbaceous top note — cut grass, elderflower, gooseberry, snow pea — sitting over citrus peel and stone fruit, carried by a consistently high, zesty acid line and a pale-to-mid lemon colour with no more than moderate alcohol. That combination of green aromatics and racy acidity is far more consistent with Sauvignon Blanc than with Riesling, which would show more floral lift and a steelier, often petrolly profile even at this level of maturity, or Chenin Blanc, which would carry more waxy lanolin without needing oak to get there. Wine 3 is the trickier case: riper texture, a lanolin and light toast character initially suggest Sémillon or an oaked Chardonnay working alone, but the retained grapefruit-pith and herbaceous edge underneath the barrel treatment argues for Sauvignon Blanc blended with Sémillon rather than either variety on its own — exactly the possibility the stem's warning that these "may be blends or single varieties" is designed to test.

## b) Identify the origin as closely as possible (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1 — Sancerre, Loire, France.** The pale lemon colour, restrained citrus-and-chalk nose and racy, mineral-edged acidity point to a cool European site rather than the New World. Flinty, wet-stone notes layered over crisp gooseberry and herbs, rather than any tropical fruit, are the specific Loire signature; the precision and reserve of the fruit — citrus and chalk rather than passion fruit — funnel this past generic Touraine Sauvignon to Sancerre itself.

**Wine 2 — Awatere Valley, Marlborough, New Zealand.** The pungent passion fruit, lime and snow-pea aromatics, dry finish and searing acidity are unmistakably New World Sauvignon Blanc, and specifically Marlborough given the intensity of pyrazine lift no other southern-hemisphere source matches so consistently. Its slightly leaner, saltier, greener edge compared with the riper, more tropical fruit typical of Wairau plantings narrows the call to the cooler, drier Awatere sub-region.

**Wine 3 — Pessac-Léognan, Bordeaux.** Barrel-derived toast and a creamy, lees-worked texture layered over grapefruit and preserved lemon place this firmly in Bordeaux's dry white tradition rather than Loire or New World Sauvignon. The structured, savoury finish and moderate rather than piercing acidity are consistent with Graves; the purity of fruit alongside well-integrated, polished oak narrows this from generic Graves to Pessac-Léognan.

**Wine 4 — Marlborough, New Zealand.** Elderflower, gooseberry and lime again point unmistakably to Marlborough, but this glass lacks the saline, structural precision of Wine 2 needed to pin a specific sub-zone, so origin rests at Marlborough generically — distinguished from Wine 2 above all by its markedly lower alcohol and softer acid line.

## c) Comment on quality and style with reference to winemaking (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1** — Very good quality, comparable to a well-regarded single-vineyard Sancerre retailing around $30-35; length, chalky minerality and precision place it above generic village-level AOC Sancerre. The textural complexity without any buttery softness suggests indigenous-yeast fermentation and minimal cellar intervention, while the tightly wound acid line confirms malolactic was avoided or kept partial, consistent with a house style built on freshness and site transparency rather than roundness.

**Wine 2** — Very good, reserve-tier commercial quality, around $20-25; riper and more concentrated than entry-level Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc, built for early, exuberant drinking rather than development. The pristine, unoaked fruit purity with no toast or butter indicates cool stainless-steel fermentation and deliberate exclusion of both oak and MLF, protecting the pyrazine-driven varietal character that underpins this style's commercial appeal.

**Wine 3** — Very good to outstanding quality, comparable to a leading classified Pessac-Léognan white retailing $45-60; complexity, texture and length go well beyond a simple dry Bordeaux blanc. Toast, lees-derived creaminess and integrated spice point to barrel fermentation with regular bâtonnage, and the absence of any green, reductive edge suggests a measured proportion of new oak; six years in bottle have folded fruit and wood into a savoury, harmonious whole, still with capacity to hold.

**Wine 4** — Good, well-made commercial quality for its category, likely retailing $12-15; a deliberately styled, lower-alcohol Sauvignon Blanc rather than a flawed one. The 9.5% alcohol alongside a touch of perceptible sweetness softening the acid points to an arrested fermentation — early picking and/or chilling to halt yeast activity before full dryness — a winemaking choice aimed at a lighter, more sessionable style rather than any failure to ripen.
