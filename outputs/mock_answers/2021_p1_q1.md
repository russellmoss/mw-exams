# Mock answer — 2021 Paper 1 Question 1

## a) Identify the origin and variety/ies used (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** Deep gold to amber with a greeny rim; a searingly oxidative nose of walnut, curry spice, dried apricot, beeswax and a saline, umami edge, with high, racy acidity and 14.5% alcohol on a bone-dry, very long finish. This register recalls Fino Sherry, but the absence of fortification-level strength and a more citric, racy acid line than Palomino's rule that out. A biologically aged, non-fortified dry white with this intensity of walnut and curry can only be Savagnin under voile — Vin Jaune. The style is unmistakable and confirms Jura; the searing precision is consistent with Arbois, the largest of the four Vin Jaune appellations, rather than the more austere Château-Chalon.

**Wine 2.** Pale-to-medium gold with toasty new oak, green apple, white peach and a flinty, cinnamon-spiced reduction; full-bodied, high acid and 13.5% alcohol with a long, chalky-mineral finish. The taut acid line and integrated toast rule out a rich Chenin Blanc from Savennières, which would show more waxy quince and wet-wool character; this is Chardonnay. The new-oak handling, textural richness and power point to white Burgundy, and chalky minerality with cinnamon spice — a recognised correlate of the Corton hill — narrows this to Grand Cru level on Corton-Charlemagne rather than Village or Premier Cru Meursault or Puligny.

**Wine 3.** Deep gold, aromatic and generous, with peach, apricot, garrigue herbs and white blossom; broad and full-bodied at 14% alcohol, moderate acidity and a textured, medium-long finish. The generosity and herbal lift suggest a warm southern French blend rather than a single Alsace or Loire variety. The fruit-driven, less layered structure eliminates white Châteauneuf-du-Pape, which shows greater spice complexity, and the blossom alone is not singular enough for varietal Condrieu; this points instead to a warm southern Rhône blend of Grenache Blanc, Clairette and/or Roussanne at Côtes du Rhône-Villages level.

**Wine 4.** Medium-to-deep gold with an amber tinge; ripe pear, quince, smoked bacon, gingerbread and honey, with a viscous, medium-to-full texture, moderate acidity and an off-dry finish at 13% alcohol. The smoky, waxy weight without overt lychee and rose petal rules out Gewürztraminer, and the softer acid and absent lime/petrol lift excludes Riesling. Smoked bacon and gingerbread are classic Pinot Gris markers, and the off-dry balance with waxy texture is typical of Alsace rather than the leaner, drier Pinot Grigio style — confirming Alsace Pinot Gris.

## b) Comment on quality and maturity (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** Outstanding quality — the concentration, saline persistence and structural precision place this at the top of Vin Jaune production, retailing around $50-70 for the 62cl clavelin given the laborious six-year ouillé method. Already fully developed by the mandatory ageing requirement, the 2013 is at or near peak on release; its oxidative style is essentially static and will hold for decades in bottle without meaningful further change.

**Wine 2.** Outstanding quality, Grand Cru level — layered spice, chalky depth and long finish confirm top-tier Burgundy, though the accessible, fruit-forward register places it below the very greatest sites; retailing around $150-250. At four years old the wine is youthful, with primary fruit still dominant and oak yet to fully integrate; it will improve over 5-8 years, developing honeyed, hazelnut complexity, and hold for a further decade.

**Wine 3.** Good quality, Villages level — riper and more textured than a basic Côtes du Rhône Blanc but lacking Châteauneuf's layered complexity; retailing around $18-25. Open and already drinking well at three years old, its moderate acidity means it is not built for extended ageing and is best drunk over the next 1-2 years before the generous fruit fades.

**Wine 4.** Very good quality, upper-end AOC Pinot Gris from cooperative rather than Grand Cru fruit; retailing around $15-20. Youthful and aromatically pure at three years old, it has limited scope to improve further and should be drunk over the next 2-3 years while freshness balances the off-dry sweetness, before the fruit fades and the residual sugar becomes cloying.

## c) Comment on the method of production (4 x 5 marks)

**Wine 1.** The nutty, saline intensity with no fresh fruit points to fermentation to dryness followed by transfer to a small, deliberately un-topped barrel, where a flor-like voile forms and drives oxidative ageing for over six years, concentrating flavour and reducing volume — explaining both the intensity and the distinctive small clavelin format.

**Wine 2.** The integrated toast alongside pure fruit suggests barrel fermentation in a meaningful proportion of new French oak with indigenous yeast and lees stirring for texture; the rounded edge to otherwise high acidity indicates malolactic fermentation, with roughly 12-18 months' elevage before bottling.

**Wine 3.** The broad, moderately acidic mid-palate suggests partial fermentation and ageing in neutral vessels alongside stainless steel, preserving stone-fruit purity while adding weight; malolactic was likely partial at most, protecting freshness given the warm vintage's alcohol.

**Wine 4.** The pure varietal fruit, absence of oak and off-dry finish point to cool stainless-steel fermentation halted early to retain residual sugar, with malolactic blocked to preserve soft natural acidity; early bottling locks in the primary pear and spice character typical of cooperative-scale Alsace production.
