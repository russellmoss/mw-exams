# Mock answer — 2017 Paper 2 Question 2

## a) Identify the origin and variety(ies) as closely as possible (4 x 12 marks)

**Wine 5.** Deep, dense ruby with a purple rim points to a thick-skinned, deeply pigmented variety, and the soft, low tannin, moderate acidity and open black cherry and plum fruit rule out anything austere. This combination of generous colour with a soft, early-drinking frame is the signature of Montepulciano rather than Sangiovese, whose acid and tannin would be far more assertive at this depth of colour, or Nero d'Avola, which typically shows riper, more baked fruit. The moderate 13% alcohol and simple, fruit-forward finish confirm an everyday commercial style rather than a single-vineyard wine, pointing straight to Montepulciano d'Abruzzo DOC on Italy's Adriatic coast — the variety's heartland and its highest-volume application.

**Wine 6.** Medium-deep ruby moving to garnet at the rim, high acidity, firm, fine-grained tannin and a nose of sour cherry, dried herbs and a savoury, iron-like edge are textbook Sangiovese; the vivid acid line rules out Montepulciano (wine 5) and the tannin is finer-grained than Nebbiolo's (wine 7). The 14.5% alcohol and evident sweet, toasty French oak point to a serious, barrique-aged Riserva rather than a basic Chianti, and the concentration and structure suggest fruit from a single, higher-quality site. This reads as Chianti Classico Riserva DOCG, Tuscany — likely a single-vineyard bottling given the depth and oak integration.

**Wine 7.** Pale-to-medium garnet already showing orange at the rim, searingly high acidity and among the highest, most drying tannin in the flight, with a perfumed nose of tar, dried rose petal, liquorice and truffle, is the unmistakable signature of Nebbiolo. Etna's Nerello Mascalese (wine 8) was seriously considered — both varieties share pale colour, high acid and fine but persistent tannin — but the tar-rose-truffle perfume here, together with the 14% alcohol and structured, savoury power, is more classically Piemontese than Etna's saline, ash-inflected profile. This points to Barolo DOCG, Piemonte.

**Wine 8.** A deceptively pale, translucent ruby-garnet carrying full body, searing acidity and firm, angular tannin at 15% alcohol is the paradox that defines Nerello Mascalese. Nebbiolo was again the first alternative given the shared pale colour and structure, but the volcanic ash, smoke, saline and blood-orange lift here, together with the notably higher alcohol for such a pale wine, are a specific volcanic-terroir signature that Barolo does not show. This is Etna Rosso DOC, on the north face of Mount Etna, Sicily.

## b) Assess the quality within the context of the region of origin (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 5** is an acceptable, commercial quality wine for Montepulciano d'Abruzzo DOC — soft, correctly made and pleasant but simple, one-dimensional and short on the finish, equivalent to an entry-level bottling retailing around $10-12; it is not made to reflect a specific site.

**Wine 6** is outstanding quality, comparable to a top single-vineyard Chianti Classico Riserva — the concentration, ripe but well-integrated tannin, well-judged new oak and long, savoury finish show real typicity and complexity, retailing in the $50-70 range.

**Wine 7** is outstanding quality for Barolo DOCG, showing the intensity, structure and layered complexity expected of the appellation's classic level, with the fine balance of power and perfume that separates a good producer's Barolo from a merely correct one; a $60-90 retail wine.

**Wine 8** is very good to outstanding quality for Etna Rosso, the single-contrada precision and mineral length placing it at the top of the DOC, comparable to a premier single-vineyard Etna bottling rather than a blended regional wine, at a $40-60 price point.

## c) Comment on the maturity (4 x 6 marks)

**Wine 5**, at two to three years old, shows a fully primary fruit profile with no development; it is made for immediate drinking and has neither the structure nor the intention to improve — drink now, holding at best another year or two before the fruit fades.

**Wine 6**, at around five years old, is youthful but developing, showing the first hints of dried fruit and leather alongside primary cherry; the ripe, fine tannin and depth of fruit give it real capacity to improve for a further five to eight years and hold for a decade or more, gaining savoury, tertiary complexity.

**Wine 7**, also around five years old, already shows orange at the rim and emerging tertiary tar and truffle notes, but the still-drying tannin and structured acidity confirm it is far from its peak; it needs another five to ten years to soften and will hold for fifteen to twenty years, developing classic leather, dried rose and forest-floor character.

**Wine 8**, at three to four years old, is youthful with primary red-fruit and volcanic mineral notes just beginning to knit together; the firm tannin and high acid give it the frame to improve over the next four to six years and hold for a further eight to ten, gaining smokier, more savoury complexity without losing its saline freshness.
