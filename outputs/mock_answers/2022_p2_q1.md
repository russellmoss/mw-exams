# Mock answer — 2022 Paper 2 Question 1

## a) Identify the grape variety and origin for Wines 1-3 (3 x 15 marks)

**Wine 1.** Medium ruby with a bright purple edge, red-currant and raspberry fruit lifted by violet, graphite and a leafy, herbal edge, on a medium-bodied, high-acid palate with fine but persistent tannin at 13.5% alcohol. The pyrazine-tinged aromatics with fine-grained rather than dense tannin point to Cabernet Franc; Cabernet Sauvignon is eliminated on tannin weight and the absence of true blackcurrant density. The moderate alcohol, racy acidity and herbaceous edge point to a cool European origin, and the red-fruit purity and firmer, more structured frame than the lighter Bourgueil or Saumur-Champigny style narrow this to Chinon, Loire Valley. A serious village-level Chinon, likely $25-35 retail.

**Wine 2.** Deep, dense purple-ruby, cassis, blackcurrant, mocha and toasty vanilla oak over a full-bodied, high-alcohol (14.5%) palate with dense, ripe, well-integrated tannin. The blackcurrant core, tannic density and generous new oak point to Cabernet Sauvignon; Merlot is eliminated by tannin weight, Syrah by the absence of pepper and savoriness. The ripeness, polished new oak and structural density without rusticity point to California; the precision and layering rather than jamminess suggest a benchmark sub-appellation such as Oakville, Napa Valley. A premium Napa price point, likely $70-90.

**Wine 3.** Medium-deep ruby, plum and blueberry fruit with a soft eucalyptus lift, medium body, notably softer, rounder tannin and moderate acidity at 13.0% alcohol. The plum/blueberry fruit and pliant tannin, softer than Wines 1 and 2, point to Merlot; Cabernet Sauvignon is eliminated by tannin softness, Grenache by the absence of higher alcohol. The riper fruit with a faint mint lift and commercially soft styling point to Chile over Argentina, with the accessible frame suggesting a warm Central Valley source such as Colchagua. An entry-to-mid-tier commercial Merlot, likely $12-18.

Having identified Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot across the three wines, this trio is the classic claret palette — it anticipates a Bordeaux blend in Wine 4, and, read the other way, Wine 4's own structure will confirm the reading of these three varieties.

## b) Style and reasons for not blending (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** Dry, medium-bodied, high-acid and finely tannic, built on savory red fruit rather than power — a structural, food-styled red for now through 4-5 years, not an immediate crowd-pleaser. Chinon is one of the few places Cabernet Franc ripens fully as the dominant variety, and the appellation's identity rests on its varietal expression of leafy freshness and red-fruit purity; there is little tradition or need for blending partners, and doing so would dilute the very regional typicity that gives the wine its value.

**Wine 2.** Full-bodied, high in alcohol and tannin, generously oaked and built for both immediate pleasure and further cellaring. Cabernet Sauvignon is Napa's flagship variety and carries the prestige and price recognition in the US market; bottling it varietally showcases the ripeness Oakville delivers and protects the commercial value of the varietal name, where blending in Merlot or Cabernet Franc would only dilute brand identity.

**Wine 3.** Medium-bodied, soft-tanned, fruit-forward and ready to drink now — an easy, commercial style rather than one built for ageing. Chilean Merlot is bottled varietally chiefly for export shelf recognition: the grape name alone sells at supermarket and independent level, and Colchagua's warm, consistent climate lets Merlot ripen fully on its own without needing Cabernet Sauvignon for backbone, so there is little incentive to blend.

## c) Purpose of blending in Wine 4 (15 marks)

Deep ruby, cassis, cedar, tobacco leaf and graphite over firm, ripe tannin and a long, savory finish — riper than a typical claret, in keeping with a warm, successful vintage, yet more restrained than either New World wine in this flight. This reads as a considered blend: Cabernet Sauvignon supplies the tannic backbone and ageing potential; Merlot fills the mid-palate with flesh and earlier approachability; Cabernet Franc lifts the aromatics with violet and graphite freshness. No single variety could deliver all three elements alone, and blending also functions as vintage insurance — the three ripen at different times and to different degrees each year, letting a producer adjust proportions annually to hold a consistent house style regardless of seasonal variation. This complementary, balance-driven approach, rather than a pursuit of varietal purity, is the defining logic of Bordeaux.

## d) Identify the origin of Wine 4 (10 marks)

The Cabernet Sauvignon/Merlot/Cabernet Franc blend, 14% alcohol, cedar and tobacco-leaf savoriness and firm, structured tannin confirm Bordeaux — the same trio identified varietally in Wines 1-3, now recombined as they would be on the Left Bank. The gravel-driven cedar and graphite character, and a Cabernet-led rather than Merlot-dominant profile, point to the Graves side of the appellation rather than the softer, plumper Right Bank; the depth of fruit and structural seriousness without first-growth concentration suggest a well-regarded but unclassified or Cru Bourgeois-equivalent estate rather than a Grand Cru Classé, narrowing the call to Pessac-Léognan. Likely retail around $50-70, a serious mid-tier claret rather than a trophy bottling.
