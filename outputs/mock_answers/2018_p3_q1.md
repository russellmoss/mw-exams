# Mock answer — 2018 Paper 3 Question 1

## a) Identify the origin, as closely as possible (3 x 10 marks)

**Pair 1 (Wines 1–2).** Fine, persistent bead and toasty autolytic depth on both glasses, set against a chalky, racing acid spine, point to traditional-method sparkling wine from a cool northern origin — Champagne, not Franciacorta or English sparkling, neither of which shows this fresh-bread layering at this intensity. The precision and multi-year complexity, well above NV level, argue for a prestige cuvée built from Grand Cru fruit, most likely blending Côte des Blancs Chardonnay with Montagne de Reims Pinot Noir.

**Pair 2 (Wines 3–4).** Botrytis-driven apricot, marmalade and honeyed richness, married to searing acidity and 13.5–14% alcohol with clearly perceptible residual sugar, place these firmly in botrytised Semillon-based Bordeaux sweet wine rather than late-harvest Loire Chenin, which would show more orchard-fruit purity and less unctuous weight. The freshness and comparatively lighter frame relative to the concentration argue for Barsac over the richer, more opulent Sauternes communes proper — a Premier Cru Classé level estate.

**Pair 3 (Wines 5–6).** Deep colour, grippy spirit-derived tannin and fixed sweetness alongside 20.5% alcohol confirm a fortified wine whose fermentation was arrested by grape spirit — Port, not a French vin doux naturel, which would show softer, more delicate spirit character. The firm tannic backbone and dark-fruit intensity, rather than a nutty, oxidative rancio character, point to Vintage Port style specifically, sourced from schistous Douro Valley vineyards planted to the traditional field blend of Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca and Tinta Roriz.

## b) Comment on the key winemaking techniques used (3 x 12 marks)

**Pair 1.** The pinpoint mousse and deep autolytic layering point to traditional-method production: base wines from Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, blended across sites, undergo a second fermentation in bottle followed by extended lees ageing — around eight to ten years, given the biscuit depth — before disgorgement to a low, brut dosage that lets fruit and autolysis dominate rather than sweetness. The extra bottle age accumulated since disgorgement on the older wine explains its more advanced toasty, dried-fruit register against the fresher brioche lift of the younger.

**Pair 2.** The pure botrytis character, with no green or under-ripe notes, suggests multiple selective picking passes (tries) through the vineyard, taking only fully noble-rotted berries. Fermentation and ageing in oak, evident from the vanilla and toasted spice framing the fruit, with a meaningful proportion of new wood, points to barrel fermentation followed by an extended élevage with regular topping. The Semillon-dominant blend, lifted by a smaller share of Sauvignon Blanc for acid line and a touch of Muscadelle for aromatic lift, is consistent with the classic Barsac house style.

**Pair 3.** The saturated colour and firm, grippy tannin in the younger wine indicate colour and tannin extraction by treading or mechanical lagares, with fermentation halted early by the addition of neutral grape spirit at around 77% potential alcohol, arresting the yeast while retaining high residual sugar. Maturation in large, neutral oak pipes for a maximum of around two years — rather than small new barriques — explains the absence of overt oak character in either wine; all subsequent development happens in bottle. Both would have been bottled unfiltered, meaning either wine will have thrown a heavy sediment and needs careful decanting.

## c) Identify the vintage(s) and consider its/their key characteristics (3 x 10 marks)

**Pair 1.** The deeper gold colour, softened mousse and dominant secondary/tertiary aromatics of the first wine point to 1999 — a generous, large-volume Champagne vintage that ripened well but without the greatest acid retention, now fully mature at around nineteen years old. The brighter colour, more primary stone-fruit and hazelnut lift and riper, rounder texture of the second wine point to 2009, a warm, opulent, high-potential-alcohol vintage that is broader and more approachable young but still developing at around nine years old.

**Pair 2.** The amber hue, marmalade, saffron and barley-sugar complexity of the first wine, with acidity now resolving into the sweetness, point to 2002 — a classic Sauternes-Barsac year in which a wet autumn following a dry summer gave ideal, even botrytis spread. The brighter gold colour, fresher apricot and citrus fruit and taut, acid-driven structure of the second wine point to 2013, a difficult, cool and late Bordeaux vintage overall in which only estates selecting through several tries achieved this level of concentration.

**Pair 3.** Both wines confirm declared Vintage Port years. The tawny-rimmed colour, resolved tannin and dried fig, walnut and spice complexity of the first wine point to 1985, a hot, dry, widely declared vintage now fully mature at over thirty years. The near-opaque purple colour, primary blackberry and violet fruit and unresolved, grippy tannin of the second wine point to 2009, another widely declared, ripe, concentrated year still at the very start of its development arc.

## d) Discuss the quality and maturity of the wines (3 x 18 marks)

**Pair 1.** Wine 1 is outstanding quality, prestige-cuvée level equivalent to a Grand Cru-declared vintage Champagne retailing around $250–300 — the layered autolytic complexity and long, saline finish go well beyond standard NV or vintage brut. At around nineteen years old it is fully mature and drinking at or just past its peak; it will not improve further but should hold for a further five years before the fruit fades. Wine 2 is of the same outstanding, prestige-cuvée quality but, at around nine years old, is only just entering its early plateau; the riper, more generous 2009 fruit gives it the structure to improve for a further eight to ten years and hold considerably longer than the 1999.

**Pair 2.** Wine 3 is outstanding quality, Premier Cru Classé Barsac, comparable in intensity to the finest Sauternes communes and retailing around $150–200 for a half bottle; at sixteen years old it is fully mature, its marmalade and dried-fruit complexity fully resolved, and should be enjoyed over the next five to eight years rather than held further. Wine 4 is of the same outstanding Premier Cru Classé quality but, at only five years old, remains primary and taut; its high acidity and oak give it the structure to improve for a further ten to fifteen years and then hold for a decade beyond that.

**Pair 3.** Wine 5 is very good to excellent quality, on a par with the finest declared vintages from a leading Vintage Port shipper, retailing around $200–250; at thirty-three years old it is fully mature, its tannin resolved and its dried-fruit-and-spice complexity at its most rewarding, meaning it should be drunk over the next five to ten years before it fades. Wine 6 is of the same very high quality but, at nine years old, is a wine to lay down rather than judge as ready: the dense, unresolved tannin and primary fruit need a further fifteen to twenty years to soften before the wine even approaches its plateau, after which it should hold for decades.
