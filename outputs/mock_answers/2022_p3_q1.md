# Mock answer — 2022 Paper 3 Question 1

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible and the grape variety(ies) used (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** Very pale salmon colour, a coarse, quickly-dissipating mousse and a fresh, primary nose of white peach, strawberry and rose petal, with no bread or brioche character, point to tank (Charmat) rather than traditional method. Low 11% alcohol and a soft, low-toned acidity are consistent with Glera as the base variety, with a pink-copper tint and light red-fruit lift indicating a minority Pinot Nero addition for colour. Tank freshness plus mandated Pinot Nero is diagnostic of Prosecco Rosé DOC, Veneto — a category only legalised in 2020, fitting the youthful style. Lambrusco was considered given the shared country and method but is eliminated by the absence of that variety's more savoury, bramble-fruited profile.

**Wine 2.** Deeper ruby-pink colour, a dense, foaming mousse and a fragrant nose of raspberry, violet and a faint savoury edge again point to tank method, but to a red-skinned base rather than a white variety merely tinted by a red addition. The brighter acidity and more overtly red-fruited palate, still light at 11.5%, are typical of Lambrusco rather than Glera, placing this in Emilia rather than the Veneto — the second Charmat-family wine of the pair.

**Wine 3.** A fine, persistent, slow-rising mousse, salmon-copper colour and a nose combining red cherry with brioche, toast and a nutty, faintly reductive depth are killer evidence for traditional method and extended lees ageing, separating this wine from the two Italian examples. The richer mid-palate weight at 12% alcohol, red-fruited and structured, points to Pinot Noir as the dominant blend component, with colour from a red wine addition — the Champagne-specific exception to saignée — rather than skin contact. High, racy acidity and depth of autolysis confirm Champagne over any other traditional-method rosé.

**Wine 4.** A fine bead and salmon-pink hue again confirm traditional method, but a lighter, more linear frame and subtler brioche note than Wine 3, despite the same 12% alcohol, suggest a shorter lees period and less concentrated base. Bright citrus and red berry fruit with high, taut acidity is consistent with Pinot Noir blended with Chardonnay or Aligoté rather than Champagne's fuller reserve-wine complexity, pointing to Crémant de Bourgogne.

## b) Comment on the key winemaking techniques (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** The clean, primary fruit and absence of autolytic character indicate a short, cool primary fermentation followed by a pressurised second fermentation in an autoclave rather than in bottle. Minimal tank time — likely close to the DOC's 60-day minimum — preserves the varietal aromatics, and early isobarometric bottling retains both the fine, quickly-fading mousse and fruit-forward freshness; a brut dosage rounds an otherwise crisp finish.

**Wine 2.** The vivid red-fruit intensity and slightly grippy finish suggest the base wine retained some skin contact before pressing, giving more colour and phenolic grip than Wine 1's approach. Secondary fermentation again took place in tank, kept brief to preserve the variety's naturally exuberant character; the coarser, more foaming bubble structure versus Wines 3 and 4 confirms the shared Charmat method across the Italian pair.

**Wine 3.** The toasty, nutty depth alongside primary red fruit indicates a meaningful proportion of reserve wines, some barrel-aged, blended into the base before the second fermentation in bottle. Extended lees contact, well beyond the statutory minimum, explains the fine, persistent mousse and biscuity layer, while the wine's weight suggests disgorgement after several years, with a measured brut dosage integrated rather than dominant.

**Wine 4.** Clean fruit with only a light dusting of yeasty complexity points to a much shorter lees period than Wine 3, close to the regulatory one-year minimum for Crémant de Bourgogne, after a straightforward bottle-fermented second fermentation. The purity of fruit and absence of oak or reserve-wine influence suggest a base of current-vintage, stainless-steel-fermented Pinot Noir and Chardonnay or Aligoté, disgorged relatively young to preserve freshness over complexity.

## c) Comment on the quality within the context of the origin (4 x 5 marks)

**Wine 1.** Judged against Prosecco Rosé DOC, this is a good quality, well-made commercial example — clean, fresh and true to type, retailing around £10-12 — but the short tank time and simple fruit profile place it at entry to mid-DOC level rather than among the more concentrated single-vineyard or Rive expressions.

**Wine 2.** Within Lambrusco Reggiano DOC this shows above the bulk, sweet commercial tier — the drier finish and cleaner fruit indicate a quality-focused rather than cooperative-volume producer — but it lacks the concentration and structure to rival the finest Lambrusco di Sorbara; a good example at around £10-15, more serious than most Lambrusco reaching export markets.

**Wine 3.** Against the benchmark of NV Champagne rosé, the depth of autolytic development, textural richness and length place this well above standard NV level, closer to a prestige house's flagship rosé — outstanding quality within the category, consistent with a £45-60 price point.

**Wine 4.** Relative to Crémant de Bourgogne, this is a very good, well-balanced example with genuine traditional-method texture and freshness — above generic Crémant level though without the depth to be mistaken for Champagne — good value at around £18-22 and a fair reflection of the appellation's quality ceiling.
