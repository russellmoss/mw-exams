# Mock answer — 2021 Paper 2 Question 3

## a) Compare and contrast the styles and consumer appeal (30 marks)

Both wines share a New World profile: deep, near-opaque colour, ripe dark fruit, full body, dry finish and identical 14.5% alcohol — the trap is that structure and alcohol alone will not separate them, so the comparison has to rest on tannin texture, aromatic signature and oak handling. Wine 7 shows the most massive tannin of the pair, thick and grippy, framing blue-black fruit, mocha and sweet spice under a blanket of American oak vanilla — an inky, structured style built for concentration rather than finesse, typical of a variety used as a blending workhorse before being bottled solo. Wine 8 is plusher and more polished, its ripe dark plum and cassis cut through by a distinctive green, herbal, peppery edge — bell pepper, dried herb, faint soy — the signature of a late-ripening variety that needs real warmth to tame its vegetal streak; oak here reads more integrated and restrained. Consumer appeal differs accordingly: Wine 7 targets drinkers of bold, jammy, high-impact reds — steak, barbecue, mass-market comfort in a fine-wine glass, sold through supermarkets and casual on-trade around $15–20. Wine 8 speaks to a more curious, food-focused consumer seeking a distinctive savoury red with a point of difference, sold through independent specialists and better restaurant lists at a premium $25–35, and trading on its status as a rediscovered signature variety rather than sheer power.

## b) Identify the variety and origin as closely as possible (2 x 10 marks)

**Wine 7** — Petite Sirah, California, USA. The near-opaque purple-black colour, thick tannin and dark, brambly, peppery fruit point to Durif/Petite Sirah rather than Syrah, whose tannin is usually finer-grained; Zinfandel was considered but its jammy, brambly fruit typically carries more overt sweetness and less structural grip. The generous, ripe, oak-driven style with soft acidity is consistent with a warm Californian appellation rather than a cooler coastal site.

**Wine 8** — Carmenère, Cachapoal, Chile. The plush dark fruit combined with unmistakable green pepper, dried herb and soy notes is the calling card of Carmenère, long confused with Merlot in Chile until ampelographic identification in 1994; Cabernet Franc was ruled out by the riper, more velvety tannin than that variety's typically firmer, leafier profile. The ripeness and polish, with the pyrazine character tamed rather than raw, point to a warm central Chilean valley such as Cachapoal.
