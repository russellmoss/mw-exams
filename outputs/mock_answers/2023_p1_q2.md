# Mock answer — 2023 Paper 1 Question 2

## a) Identify the grape variety (4 x 5 marks)

The four wines span an alcohol range from 8% to over 14%, and that spread is the spine of the identification work: it separates a low-strength, sweet-edged style from three fuller, dry aromatic whites, within which the genuine confusion sits between the two most floral wines.

**Wine 5** — Riesling. Very pale lemon colour, intensely aromatic lime and white peach fruit, slatey lift and a thread of residual sweetness carried on razor-sharp acidity at only 8% alcohol point directly to Riesling; almost no other variety pairs genuine sweetness with such piercing acid at this low a strength. Muscat was briefly weighed for the aromatic lift, but its perfume runs more grapey and floral than lime-and-peach, and rarely shows this degree of racy acid at Kabinett ripeness.

**Wine 6** — Viognier. Apricot, citrus peel and honeysuckle aromatics sit over a broad, oily, faintly waxy texture with only moderate acidity at 13.5% alcohol — the classic Viognier fingerprint. Muscat was the main alternative given the floral register, but its perfume is more overtly grapey and linear with a noticeably crisper acid line; this wine's softer acid and heavier, honeyed weight rule Muscat out.

**Wine 7** — Chardonnay. Ripe apple, nectarine and citrus blossom fruit over a broad, creamy, faintly buttery and toasty mid-palate, with moderate-plus acidity carrying a substantial 14.3% alcohol, is textbook Chardonnay. Viognier was briefly weighed for the stone-fruit note, but Chardonnay's more citrus-driven fruit, firmer acid spine and evident lees/oak-derived richness rather than innate viscosity confirm the call.

**Wine 8** — Muscat. An unmistakably grapey, overtly floral orange-blossom perfume, allied to a bright, precise citrus palate and a crisp, disciplined acid line at 13.5% alcohol, points to Muscat. Viognier was eliminated: Muscat's perfume is more linear and grapey, without Viognier's honeysuckle weight and oilier mouthfeel, and the acidity here is markedly higher and more taut.

## b) Describe the acidity and texture, and their contribution to quality (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 5** — The piercingly high, racy acidity is the structural backbone, slicing through genuine residual sugar to leave the palate feeling weightless despite real sweetness; texture is light, translucent and delicate, with no phenolic grip or oak to intrude. This acid-sugar tension is the hallmark of top Kabinett fruit and lifts the wine above simple, off-dry commercial Riesling — very good quality, equivalent to a classified-site Mosel Kabinett, retailing around £20–25.

**Wine 6** — Acidity is moderate rather than searing, typical of this naturally low-acid variety, but freshness is retained through cooler, higher-altitude plateau fruit rather than correction. Texture is the more important quality driver: a supple, lees-derived creaminess coats the palate without becoming heavy, and underlying freshness stops the wine tipping into blowsy weight. This freshness-over-power balance marks very good quality, above generic Viognier de pays level and closer to a well-sited Northern Rhône appellation wine, around £25–30.

**Wine 7** — Moderate-plus acidity is well judged for the ripeness level, lifting what could otherwise be a heavy, high-alcohol wine; texture is rich, rounded and creamy, with a buttery, faintly toasty grip suggesting barrel and lees contact. The acid keeps the wine from feeling flabby despite 14.3% alcohol — good to very good quality, equivalent to a premium regional Russian River bottling around $30–35, rather than a top single-vineyard cuvée.

**Wine 8** — Acidity is bright, higher than the Viognier's and cleanly disciplined, giving a savoury, mineral-tinged finish that reins in the exuberant Muscat perfume; texture is light-to-medium, dry and taut rather than oily, with no obvious oak or malolactic softening. This acid-driven precision, unusual for Muscat, elevates the wine above a blowsy off-dry style into a genuinely serious dry Alsace expression — very good quality, comfortably above generic Alsace Muscat, closer to a well-regarded village-level bottling around £20–25.

## c) Discuss ageing potential and evolution (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 5** — The pale colour and pure primary lime and peach fruit indicate a young wine, 2–3 years old. High acidity and residual sugar give real ageing capacity: it should hold and slowly gain weight for a further 8–10 years, developing petrol, honeyed and dried-apricot notes typical of mature Kabinett Riesling, with the acid keeping it fresh throughout.

**Wine 6** — The fresh fruit and moderate acid suggest a wine at or near its peak, best drunk over the next 2–4 years. Viognier's naturally low acid limits long-term cellaring, and further age is likely to bring a loss of aromatic lift rather than complexity, so this is a wine to enjoy on its vibrant fruit rather than hold.

**Wine 7** — The wine already shows primary-to-secondary development, with buttery, toasty notes alongside fresh fruit, suggesting around two years old and entering its drinking window now. It has enough concentration and acid to hold for a further 2–3 years, but is not built for long ageing; further time will soften the fruit and deepen the toasty, nutty notes rather than add real complexity.

**Wine 8** — The still-primary, intensely grapey aromatics and precise acid line point to a young wine, around two years old, ready to drink now on its aromatic freshness. Muscat's perfume fades relatively quickly with bottle age, so short-to-medium-term drinking, over the next 2–3 years, is recommended; further ageing would likely dull rather than develop the wine's principal appeal.
