# Mock answer — 2016 Paper 3 Question 4

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible (20 marks)

Both wines share a sour-cherry, bitter-almond fruit core with a herbal, slightly bitter finish, pointing to a Corvina-led blend with Corvinone and Rondinella rather than Sangiovese, whose red-cherry fruit is more one-dimensional and lacks the bitter-almond edge, or Nebbiolo, which would bring higher tannin and tar/rose aromatics not present here. That combination of variety and a raisined, glycerol-rich intensity layered onto fresh cherry fruit is the calling card of northern Italy's dried-grape (appassimento) tradition, narrowing this immediately to Valpolicella, Veneto. Wine 11 is dry but shows more depth, colour and alcohol (13.5%) than basic Valpolicella, consistent with the enriched Ripasso category; Wine 12 is unmistakably sweet at 14.5% alcohol with concentrated dried black-fruit character, placing it in the Recioto della Valpolicella style. Given both wines share an identical grape source and producer, and show a level of precision and freshness beyond commercial bulk examples, this points to fruit from the higher-quality Classico zone: **Valpolicella Classico, Veneto, Italy** — Wine 11 as Ripasso Superiore, Wine 12 as Recioto della Valpolicella DOCG.

## b) Discuss the key winemaking techniques used to arrive at these styles (20 marks)

Wine 11 was fermented dry as a standard Valpolicella, then racked onto the still-warm, sugar-rich pomace freshly drained from an Amarone or Recioto fermentation — the "ripasso" — triggering a short secondary fermentation that lifts alcohol, extracts extra colour and tannin, and adds the raisined, cocoa-edged richness sitting over brighter cherry fruit; the Superiore designation confirms a minimum year's ageing and higher minimum alcohol. Wine 12 instead takes its own fruit through months of rack-drying (appassimento) to concentrate sugar and flavour before pressing, after which fermentation is deliberately arrested — by cooling and rising alcohol — while substantial sugar remains, leaving the wine sweet. This is the parent technique of Amarone, which simply lets the same dried must ferment fully dry.

## c) Discuss how the styles of these wines determine their commercial appeal (10 marks)

Wine 11's dry, food-friendly richness at an accessible 13.5% suits mainstream on- and off-trade listings around $20–25, competing as an affordable step up from basic Valpolicella for Independent Specialists and premium supermarkets, with strong by-the-glass and export volume. Wine 12's small-batch sweetness restricts it to a niche dessert-wine channel — Independent Specialists and fine-dining lists — at $50+ for a 50cl bottle, targeting consumers seeking an alternative to Port or Banyuls with chocolate or blue cheese, but limited by low yields and a narrow occasion-driven market.
