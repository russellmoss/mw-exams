# Mock answer — 2017 Paper 3 Question 5

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible (2 x 8 marks)

**Wine 9.** Pale-medium ruby, red cherry and raspberry fruit with a savoury, earthy undertow, bright but moderate acidity and 13% alcohol point to Pinot Noir from a cool continental climate — Burgundy, given the stem. Simple fruit, gentle tannin and a whisper of old-oak vanilla argue against a top site; the rustic, mineral-tinged concentration suits old-vine fruit. This points to Côte de Nuits-Villages, the catch-all appellation for outlying communes at the northern and southern ends of the Côte de Nuits, rather than a named village such as Gevrey-Chambertin, which would show more precision.

**Wine 10.** Deeper ruby, greater concentration, finer tannin and a longer, more mineral finish point to the same variety and region but a markedly superior site. More pronounced spice and toast suggest a higher new-oak proportion than Wine 9, comfortably carried by riper fruit. This precision and depth rule out village Nuits-Saint-Georges and point to a named Premier Cru climat, most plausibly the limestone-rich southern end around Prémeaux-Prissey, where sites show more finesse than power.

## b) Consider the likely vintage (2 x 7 marks)

**Wine 9 — 2012.** Dense, concentrated fruit allied to freshness rather than jamminess is consistent with a small, hail- and frost-affected crop that concentrated the surviving berries — a hallmark of the difficult but high-quality 2012 in the Côte de Nuits. A riper, more diffuse recent warm year was considered and ruled out by the tauter acid line.

**Wine 10 — 2011.** Approachable fruit, softer tannin and a forward character point to a lighter, more fruit-driven vintage than Wine 9 — consistent with 2011, generally elegant and enjoyable young rather than built for long ageing. Note the inversion: the lesser appellation shows more structural stuffing than the Premier Cru, 2012's concentration outweighing 2011's charm — precisely the trap this pairing sets.

## c) Comment on quality and potential to improve further in the bottle (2 x 10 marks)

**Wine 9.** Very good quality for Côte de Nuits-Villages, punching above its tier on old-vine concentration — comparable to a well-made village Burgundy at around £25–30. Balanced, good length, enough grip from 2012 to hold rather than fade; still fruit-driven at five years old, it will hold its plateau a further 3–4 years rather than improve, softening into savoury, undergrowth notes.

**Wine 10.** Outstanding quality, genuine Premier Cru — greater intensity, complexity and oak integration justify a price around £80–90. Now six years old, showing early tertiary, gamey complexity alongside primary fruit, it is approaching its window rather than needing further time; it will improve gently for 2–3 years and hold for 6–8 more, developing forest-floor, dried-fruit and spice characters as fruit recedes.
