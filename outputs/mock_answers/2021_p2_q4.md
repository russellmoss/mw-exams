# Mock answer — 2021 Paper 2 Question 4

## a) Identify the region as closely as possible (2 x 10 marks)

**Pair 1 (Wines 9 & 10) — Southern Rhône.** Both show deep ruby colour, a garrigue-scented core of black cherry and dark plum, peppery spice and generous alcohol, pointing to a Grenache-led southern Rhône blend rather than a northern Syrah monovarietal (too much sweet-fruited warmth, not enough violet/olive perfume). Wine 9's greater depth of colour, firmer structured tannin, savoury leather and warm-stone character, allied to 15% alcohol, are consistent with a low-yielding, galets roulés-influenced site — this narrows to Châteauneuf-du-Pape. Wine 10 is broader, more one-dimensionally fruited and carries more overt, polished oak with a touch less concentration; this reads as a wider, higher-volume appellation drawing on cooler and more varied southern Rhône sites — Côtes du Rhône.

**Pair 2 (Wines 11 & 12) — Rioja.** Both show a Tempranillo-dominant core with a vanilla-and-coconut oak signature typical of American barrels, ruling out Ribera del Duero (which would show darker fruit, higher extraction and less overt sweet-oak lift). Wine 11's medium ruby hue, red-fruited primary character and moderate tannin place it as a youthful Rioja; Wine 12's garnet rim, dried-fig and cedar complexity and fully resolved tannin confirm a long-aged, traditionally elevated Rioja.

## b) Compare and contrast style, quality and method of production (2 x 20 marks)

**Pair 1.** Wine 9's firm, rounded tannin, savoury complexity and restrained, foudre-derived oak (no obvious vanillin) suggest hand-harvested, low-yield old-vine fruit fermented and aged in large neutral wood — a traditional estate approach consistent with Châteauneuf-du-Pape's appellation rules on minimum alcohol and permitted yields. This is outstanding quality, equivalent to a leading traditional cru-level estate, retailing around $70-90. Wine 10 shows riper, simpler fruit and a more polished, sweeter oak veneer suggesting shorter maceration and a more commercially engineered élevage (likely a mix of barrel and larger vessel) aimed at early accessibility rather than complexity. It is good quality, a well-made commercial Côtes du Rhône around $15-20 — sound and true to origin but lacking the depth, structure and length of Wine 9.

**Pair 2.** Wine 11's fresh red fruit, moderate tannin and lighter oak imprint point to Rioja's Crianza minimum — one year in oak plus a further year in bottle before release — giving a fruit-driven, early-drinking style. This is good quality, Crianza level, around $15-18. Wine 12's fully integrated, fine-grained tannin, tertiary cedar-tobacco-dried-flower complexity and long, seamless finish indicate extended oak ageing (a minimum of two years, evidently longer here) followed by several years further in bottle before release, consistent with Gran Reserva — indeed showing complexity beyond the legal minimum. This is outstanding quality, flagship traditional Rioja, commanding $150-200+. Both pairs illustrate the same underlying idea — an entry-level regional style set against a prestige tier from the same denomination — but the Rhône pair achieves this through site and yield while the Rioja pair achieves it through legislated ageing.

## c) Comment on the commercial position (4 x 5 marks)

**Wine 9** suits independent fine wine specialists and premium on-trade lists at $70-90, targeting collectors of classic Old World Rhône reds; it competes with top Gigondas, Vacqueyras and rival Châteauneuf estates, with strongest demand in the US, UK and Benelux. **Wine 10** is a supermarket and chain-restaurant by-the-glass staple at $15-20, appealing to consumers wanting reliable, fruit-forward southern Rhône character; it competes with branded GSM blends from the New World and sits well in UK and US grocery. **Wine 11** fits independent specialists and modern mid-market restaurant lists at $15-18, aimed at drinkers seeking a fresher, Garnacha-led Rioja style, competing with young Ribera del Duero and other Spanish reds in Nordic and US markets. **Wine 12** belongs in fine wine merchants and collector auctions at $150-200+, for connoisseurs of mature traditional Rioja, competing with aged Bordeaux and Barolo at equivalent price points in US and Asian luxury markets.

## d) Suggest an ideal drinking window (4 x 5 marks)

**Wine 9**, at around six years old, is drinking well now but its firm tannin and depth mean it will continue to improve for a further 5-8 years, developing leather and game complexity, and hold for 15+ years overall. **Wine 10**, at around four years old, is fully ready and best enjoyed over the next 2-3 years before its primary fruit fades. **Wine 11**, also around four years old, is made for early drinking and should be consumed over the next 3-4 years while its fresh red fruit remains dominant. **Wine 12**, at roughly eleven years old, is at or near its peak, showing full tertiary development; it needs no further ageing but its structure and acidity mean it will hold comfortably for another 10-15 years without decline.
