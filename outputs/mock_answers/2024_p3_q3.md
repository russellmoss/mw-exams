# Mock answer — 2024 Paper 3 Question 3

## a) Identify the region as closely as possible, commenting on the commercial opportunities and challenges with reference to the styles (30 marks)

Wines 6 and 7 show unmistakable noble-rot signatures — apricot, orange marmalade, saffron and honeyed pineapple over a deep gold colour, unctuous texture and high alcohol for a still wine (14%) balanced by racy acidity. This confirms botrytised Sémillon-dominant Sauternes/Barsac rather than late-harvest or air-dried sweetness, since the freshness and precision argue against simple over-ripeness. Wine 5 fixes the exact commune: a dry Sauvignon Blanc–Sémillon blend with a salty, gravel-and-limestone mineral finish and light new-oak toast on the Sémillon portion, at just 12.5% alcohol from a cooler vintage. Alone it could suggest Pessac-Léognan, but alongside the botrytis wines it reads as a top Sauternes estate's dry white, made from parcels not destined for the sweet wines — a structure (grand vin, second wine, dry white) common to leading Sauternes properties responding to commercial pressure. That pressure is real: global demand for sweet wine has contracted, food-matching is narrow (foie gras, blue cheese), production is labour-intensive (multiple botrytis tries, low yields) and vintage-dependent on autumn humidity, and cellaring costs for long-lived styles are high. The dry white generates cash flow and on-trade by-the-glass listings; the second wine broadens accessibility; the grand vin anchors prestige and the secondary market — genuine opportunities in half-bottle formats and growing Asian interest, against the structural decline in everyday sweet-wine consumption.

## b) Discuss the winemaking and quality

**Wine 5 (10 marks)** — The clean, pure citrus and passionfruit character with no phenolic grip suggests the Sauvignon Blanc component was picked early and fermented cool in stainless steel to protect thiols, while the riper stone-fruit weight and subtle vanilla toast point to a portion of Sémillon barrel-fermented in a small proportion of new oak. A brief lees contact would explain the creamy, saline finish. Very good quality — an accessible, well-made dry Bordeaux Blanc Sec from a classified Sauternes estate, comparable in style and price (around $35–40) to a mid-tier Pessac-Léognan Blanc, though lacking that appellation's concentration.

**Wine 6 (10 marks)** — The vivid, relatively lighter-weight botrytis fruit indicates selective picking over fewer passes than a grand vin, with hard basket-pressing to concentrate the must and a fermentation arrested by rising alcohol and sugar to preserve sweetness; a shorter, partly-oaked élevage explains the fresher, less layered profile. Very good quality, equivalent to a well-made second-wine Sauternes — retailing around $35–45 per half-bottle, an earlier-drinking, less concentrated foil to the grand vin.

**Wine 7 (10 marks)** — Greater depth, spice and botrytis intensity point to fruit selected only from the best parcels over multiple successive tries, pressed hard and fermented slowly, then matured in a high proportion of new oak barriques for an extended period — explaining the polished toast and layered complexity. Outstanding quality, Premier Cru Classé (1855) level: dense, precise and long, retailing upwards of $100 per half-bottle among the finest examples of the appellation.

## c) Comment on the wine's maturity

**Wine 5 (5 marks)** — Pale lemon-green colour and primary citrus/passionfruit fruit indicate a young wine of around three years old; it is ready now and best drunk over the next 2–3 years before its fresh, thiol-driven aromatics fade — it is not built for extended ageing.

**Wine 6 (5 marks)** — Deep gold colour with vibrant, still-primary botrytis fruit suggests a wine of around six years old that has further to go; it should improve over the next 5–8 years, gaining marmalade and nutty complexity, and will hold for a decade or more given its sugar-acid balance.

**Wine 7 (5 marks)** — Full gold colour with an amber rim and fully developed marmalade, saffron and caramelised notes confirm a mature wine of around seventeen years; it is at its peak now and, supported by high residual sugar and acidity, should hold at this plateau for a further 10–15 years without significant further gain in complexity.
