# Mock answer — 2017 Paper 3 Question 1

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible with reference to the style (3 x 7 marks)

**Wine 1.** Pale pink with an orange tint, noticeably sweet with confected strawberry and melon fruit, low alcohol at 9.5% and a short finish. This off-dry, simple, fruit-forward profile is the calling card of a mass-produced, arrested-fermentation rosé rather than a serious dry style. The non-vintage, multi-sourced character rules out a single-appellation European wine; low alcohol, obvious residual sugar and confected fruit point to a warm, high-volume New World source — California, most plausibly a broad, blended Central Valley origin.

**Wine 2.** Pale salmon colour, bone dry, 13% alcohol, fresh red-berry and melon fruit, no oak. The very pale hue suggests direct pressing rather than saignée, and the freshness and dryness are classic Mediterranean rosé. Tavel was considered but its deeper colour and richer weight are absent; the paler, more delicate style and dry convention point to Côtes de Provence over Tavel or a Languedoc equivalent.

**Wine 3.** Fine, persistent mousse, autolytic brioche and biscuit notes over red berry, citrus and orchard fruit. The mousse and autolysis confirm traditional method rather than still or tank-method rosé. Champagne rosé was considered given the Chardonnay/Pinot Noir base, but the riper citrus and berry fruit and less austere structure point to a cooler New World source — Marlborough.

## b) Highlight the key winemaking techniques used (3 x 8 marks)

**Wine 1.** The pale, orange-tinged hue suggests brief skin contact or a saignée bleed. Fermentation was evidently arrested by chilling and sterile filtration well short of dryness, leaving obvious residual sugar and the low 9.5% alcohol. Clean, simple fruit with no oak confirms stainless steel throughout, with sterile filtration and early bottling to lock in sweetness and freshness.

**Wine 2.** The very pale colour and pure, unbruised red-fruit character point to direct pressing, minimising phenolic extraction. Cool, temperature-controlled fermentation in stainless steel preserved primary aromatics, and the absence of any buttery note suggests malolactic conversion was blocked to retain freshness. No oak and youthful, un-evolved fruit indicate early bottling and release.

**Wine 3.** The Chardonnay and Pinot Noir components were evidently fermented separately, with the textured mid-palate suggesting a portion aged in older oak alongside a stainless-steel majority, then blended into cuvée. The mousse quality and pronounced autolysis indicate tirage, bottle-fermented secondary fermentation and extended lees ageing of at least two years before disgorgement, with modest dosage bringing the wine back to a dry finish.

## c) Discuss the commercial appeal and market positioning (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** A high-volume, entry-level commercial product rather than a quality benchmark — technically clean and fit for purpose, retailing around $8–10 in US supermarkets and equivalent grocery channels internationally. Its off-dry, low-alcohol, easy-drinking style targets entry-level and younger drinkers seeking a soft, unchallenging chilled style needing no food match. The style traces back to an accidental stuck fermentation that created an entire American commercial category and introduced millions to wine; it should be judged on fitness for purpose, not fine-wine benchmarks. Wide distribution is its strength; declining category interest among younger US drinkers is its challenge.

**Wine 2.** This rides the global boom in pale, dry Provence rosé, where lifestyle branding supports retail around £12–18 UK off-trade and a premium further by the glass on-trade — above what the liquid alone would justify. It competes with premium New World rosés and other southern French pinks, with strong seasonal and growing year-round demand across the US and UK. The opportunity is continued growth and gastronomic versatility; the challenge is intensifying competition as the category crowds.

**Wine 3.** The traditional method and extended lees ageing justify premium/specialist positioning, retailing around £25–30 through independent specialists and better on-trade lists rather than supermarkets. It offers a fruit-forward, lower-priced alternative to rosé Champagne and English sparkling rosé for consumers seeking celebratory sparkling wine with New World vibrancy. Export opportunity is strong into the UK, US and Australia given rising demand for sparkling rosé; the challenge is the prestige Champagne commands at a similar price.
