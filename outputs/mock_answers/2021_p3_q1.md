# Mock answer — 2021 Paper 3 Question 1

## a) Identify the origin and grape variety/ies used (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** The fine, persistent bead and welcoming bready, toasty autolytic lift confirm traditional method. The mid-palate carries both citrus-driven freshness and a touch of red-berry weight, pointing to a blend of black and white grapes rather than a pure blanc de blancs, whose profile would be leaner and more purely citric. The autolysis is present but not deep, and the fruit reads primary rather than developed — consistent with a young base wine lifted by a modest proportion of reserve wine, the house-blending signature of a large-scale non-vintage rather than a single-vintage or single-vineyard cuvée. High, fresh acidity and a chalky undertone point to a cool, chalk-soil origin; Loire Crémant was considered but its typically waxier, orchard-fruit register is absent, and English sparkling was also considered but eliminated by the riper, rounder mid-palate than that racier, leaner style usually shows. This funnels to a blended Pinot Noir/Meunier/Chardonnay Champagne NV.

**Wine 2.** Deeper gold colour, more advanced brioche and toasted-almond notes, white cherry fruit and a longer, more precise, chalk-driven finish show markedly greater structural depth and tension than Wine 1 — the key marker of a declared vintage rather than a blended NV. The citrus-zest freshness alongside riper white cherry suggests a Chardonnay–Pinot Noir blend, with the higher 12.5% alcohol and firm acid backbone pointing to a demanding but ultimately high-quality, structured rather than opulent, growing season. The extended tertiary development indicates lees ageing well beyond the regulatory vintage minimum. This is vintage Champagne, from a cooler, tension-driven year.

**Wine 3.** The fine mousse and light bready lift again confirm traditional method, but the register is shorter and less deeply autolytic than Wines 1–2. Riper stone-fruit and pineapple-tinged citrus, a rounder mid-palate and softer, though still refreshing, acid line move this outside Champagne toward a cooler New World site retaining real freshness — ruling out warmer regions such as coastal Australia. A subtle toast note reads as oak rather than lees-derived, suggesting reserve wines aged in neutral wood. This points to cool, fog-influenced coastal California — Anderson Valley — on a Chardonnay–Pinot Noir base.

## b) Comment on the method of production (3 x 5 marks)

All three show traditional method: fine, persistent mousse, secondary fermentation character and autolytic complexity. Wine 1's moderate toast reflects cuvée-and-taille blending across many parcels and a reserve-wine component for consistency, with lees ageing at or just above the 15-month NV minimum. Wine 2's deeper complexity indicates lees ageing well past the statutory vintage minimum, likely six years or more, giving its greater tension and length. Wine 3's oak-derived toast, distinct from lees autolysis, points to barrel-aged reserve wines blended in before tirage — an unusual, distinctly Champenoise touch for a New World base.

## c) Comment on the quality and commercial position (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1** is very good quality, the benchmark standard for a grande marque NV Champagne — reliable, well-balanced, but built for consistency over complexity. Retailing around £35, it sits across premium supermarkets, on-trade by-the-glass and airline/duty-free channels, competing with other grande marque NVs for a broad consumer base valuing recognisable brand and typicity over individuality.

**Wine 2** is outstanding quality, equivalent to a prestige family-house vintage cuvée from a genuinely excellent year. At around £70 through Independent Specialists and fine-wine merchants, it targets collectors and export markets such as the US and Japan, competing with rival house vintage cuvées and entry-level prestige releases, with real ageing potential to reward cellaring.

**Wine 3** is very good quality, a premium New World traditional-method sparkling with a distinctly Champenoise winemaking hand. At around £25 through premium supermarkets and Independent Specialists, it competes with Cava's top tier, Crémant and entry Champagne NV, offering strong value to US and UK consumers seeking Champagne-style complexity — its challenge being brand recognition outside its domestic market against Champagne's protected name.
