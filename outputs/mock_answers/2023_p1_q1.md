# Mock answer — 2023 Paper 1 Question 1

## a) Identify the grape variety (2 x 10 marks)

**Wines 1 & 2 — Sauvignon Blanc.** Wine 1's pale lemon colour, lime and white-flower nose with a flinty, non-tropical edge, searing acidity and a lean, linear palate at 13% alcohol are textbook Sauvignon Blanc; Chenin Blanc was considered for the high acid but lacks the pungent gooseberry-citrus lift and shows no waxy lanolin note. Wine 2 shares the same varietal thumbprint — grassy-to-exotic citrus, cut-grass pungency underneath the riper honeysuckle and peach — but with fuller body, softer acidity and 14.1% alcohol; Semillon-dominant white Bordeaux blends were ruled out since there is no honeyed weight without the pungent aromatic drive that Sauvignon Blanc alone supplies here.

**Wines 3 & 4 — Semillon.** Wine 3's pale lemon colour, restrained lemon-pith and lanolin nose, waxy mid-palate texture and remarkably low 10.5% alcohol against very high acidity point to Semillon rather than Sauvignon Blanc, which would show more overt aromatic pungency at this acid level. Wine 4 confirms the variety with a similar waxy, lemon-and-orchard-fruit core but broader, riper and more textural at 13.5% alcohol; Chardonnay was briefly considered for the textural weight but the lemon-pith and lanolin character, not stone fruit or malic creaminess, sit more squarely with Semillon.

## b) Compare and contrast the major winemaking influences (2 x 15 marks)

**Sauvignon Blanc pair.** Wine 1's pure citrus and flint with no oak or buttery weight point to a protective, stainless-steel regime with minimal lees contact — consistent with a cool, low-yielding vintage where fruit purity rather than winemaking overlay was prioritised. Wine 2 shows a markedly different hand: the honeysuckle-peach fruit sits under a light vanilla-oak seasoning and a creamy, textural mid-palate, suggesting partial barrel fermentation in new French oak with malolactic fermentation on the barrel-aged portion and extended lees ageing, while a stainless-steel component preserves the varietal lift underneath. The contrast is one of philosophy as much as climate — restraint versus deliberate textural build.

**Semillon pair.** Wine 3's utterly neutral aromatics, taut acid line and very low alcohol indicate cool, early picking with gentle pressing and a purely reductive stainless-steel ferment with no lees or oak influence, leaving the wine a blank canvas built to develop toast and honey in bottle rather than in the cellar. Wine 4 is worked far harder: a subtle savoury oak seasoning, rounder texture and more evolved fruit tone point to whole-bunch pressing, barrel fermentation with native yeast, and extended maturation in large-format oak with a smaller proportion of barrique — a warmer-climate, old-vine wine shaped deliberately toward richness and earlier drinkability.

## c) Comment on quality and ageing potential (2 x 25 marks)

**Wine 1** — Very good quality, equivalent to a top Village-level Sancerre, retailing around $30–35. The precision, chalky mineral drive and persistent citrus length reflect a low-yielding, concentrated vintage; under the skin, the taut acid-to-alcohol balance at 13% is what marks this as serious rather than generic Sancerre. Youthful, 2–3 years old; will hold its freshness for a further 3–4 years, gaining a rounder, honeyed edge without losing tension.

**Wine 2** — Good quality, a premium commercial style at $25–30, more polished than profound. The oak and lees work add breadth but slightly mute the pure varietal snap that would push this toward outstanding; balance is sound but complexity is more additive than terroir-driven. Approachable now at 2–3 years old, it has enough weight to hold for another 2–3 years before the oak character begins to dominate the fading fruit.

**Wine 3** — Outstanding quality, benchmark Hunter Valley Semillon at $30–40, its unusually low, precisely balanced alcohol against searing acidity is the signature of a wine built specifically for bottle age rather than immediate drinking — a genuinely "under the skin" structural clue. Closed and austere now at 3–4 years old, it needs a further 5–7 years to unlock toasted, honeyed complexity and will hold a decade or more beyond that.

**Wine 4** — Very good quality, a serious old-vine expression at $20–25, more immediately generous than Wine 3 but with real concentration from dry-farmed fruit. The oak is well integrated rather than dominant, giving textural complexity without masking the granite-derived savoury edge. Drinking well now at 3–4 years old, with capacity to improve for a further 3–4 years before the primary fruit gives way to nuttier, more tertiary notes.
