# Mock answer — 2025 Paper 2 Question 2

## a) Identify the origin and the grape variety/ies as closely as possible (4 x 13 marks)

All four wines share deep ruby-purple colour and dry, moderate-plus to high alcohol, confirming a warm-climate French red source, but the flight splits cleanly into two structural camps. Wines 5 and 6 show a firmer, more linear acid line, a peppery, savoury Syrah register (white and black pepper, cured meat, graphite) and finer, more angular tannin at lower alcohol (13%) — all pointing to single-variety Syrah from the Northern Rhône. Wines 4 and 7 show riper, broader black fruit, warm garrigue spice, softer, rounder tannin and higher alcohol (14.5% and 14%), consistent with Grenache-led Southern Rhône blends.

**Wine 4** — Gigondas. The dominant kirsch and black cherry fruit, dried thyme and violet aromatics, and full, velvety yet chalky tannin structure point to Grenache as the backbone, with a smaller proportion of Mourvèdre and Syrah adding structure and pepper lift — a classic GMS blend. Châteauneuf-du-Pape was considered given the power and 14.5% alcohol, but the freshness, tighter tannic grip and absence of the more baked, kirsch-liqueur intensity typical of Châteauneuf argue instead for a cooler, limestone-influenced cru site — Gigondas, on the Dentelles de Montmirail.

**Wine 7** — Côtes du Rhône. Similar ripe black fruit and garrigue spice to Wine 4 but with less density, softer tannin and a simpler, more linear fruit profile point again to a Grenache-Syrah-Mourvèdre blend, but at a broader, less site-specific level. Côtes du Rhône-Villages was considered, but the wine's more generic, blended fruit character and lack of a single-village concentration point instead to the base regional Côtes du Rhône AOC.

**Wine 5** — Crozes-Hermitage. Pure varietal red and dark cherry fruit, white pepper and liquorice, with soft, velvety young tannin and no discernible oak, is textbook Northern Rhône Syrah. St-Joseph was considered for its similarly fruit-driven style, but the flatter, more open fruit profile without St-Joseph's granite grip points to Crozes-Hermitage's gravel plateau rather than a hillside cru.

**Wine 6** — Cornas. Denser purple fruit, graphite, iodine and dried herb notes, with firmer, fine-grained tannin, medium-plus acidity and greater overall grip than Wine 5, indicate a granite hillside site. Côte-Rôtie was eliminated for the absence of any co-fermented Viognier lift or violet-perfumed top note; Hermitage for the wine's leaner, more mineral-driven rather than opulent profile — both point instead to Cornas.

## b) Comment on quality within the context of origin (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 4** is outstanding for Gigondas — concentrated, layered and long, with the balance and precision to rival entry-level Châteauneuf-du-Pape; a benchmark example of the appellation retailing around $45–55.
**Wine 7** is good, honest commercial quality for basic Côtes du Rhône — well balanced with more fruit concentration than the $15–18 category norm, but simple and one-dimensional against the crus.
**Wine 5** is good quality Crozes-Hermitage, typical but pleasant at the entry level ($25–30) — correct varietal expression without the depth or minerality to approach cru status.
**Wine 6** is very good to outstanding for Cornas — showing more concentration, precision and structure than Wine 5 at a similar alcohol, excellent value at $35–45 against pricier Hermitage or Côte-Rôtie.

## c) Comment on the wine's maturity (4 x 5 marks)

**Wine 4**, still deep and youthful with primary black fruit dominant, is 2–3 years old and needs a further 2–3 years to soften; it will improve for 6–8 years, gaining dried-fruit and leather complexity, and hold for a decade or more.
**Wine 7** is a young, primary wine best drunk over the next 2–4 years for its fresh fruit; it lacks the structure to improve significantly further.
**Wine 5**, bright and fruit-forward with velvety young tannin, is ready now to 3 years and will hold rather than develop further complexity.
**Wine 6** is youthful and tightly wound, needing 2–4 years to unfurl; it should improve for 6–10 years, developing game, tobacco and cedar notes, and hold well beyond that.
