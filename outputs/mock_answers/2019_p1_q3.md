# Mock answer — 2019 Paper 1 Question 3

## a) Comment on the winemaking (2 x 10 marks)

**Wine 11** — The deep amber colour, dried orchard fruit, tea leaf and walnut aromas, and the firm phenolic grip on a savoury, bitter-edged finish point to months of maceration on skins in a porous, oxygen-permeable vessel rather than pressed-off tank production — consistent with fermentation in a buried clay vessel, likely wild-yeast driven given the textural, earthy complexity and absence of the high-toned fruit typical of inoculated ferments. The moderate 12.5% alcohol and bone-dry finish suggest a short hang time and no chaptalisation, while the faintly volatile, savoury edge implies minimal or no added sulphur.

**Wine 12** — The mahogany-amber colour, walnut, rancio, orange-peel and salted-caramel intensity on a bone-dry, high-alcohol frame indicate long, deliberate oxidative ageing in part-filled old wooden casks, encouraging controlled acetaldehyde development rather than reductive handling. The complete absence of residual sweetness rules out a dessert style, and the 16.5% alcohol reads as natural concentration through evaporative loss rather than spirit addition — this is not a fortified wine. The layered complexity and non-vintage consistency of style suggest a fractional blending or solera-type system, topping younger wine into older casks over many years.

## b) Discuss the wine's style, quality and commercial potential (2 x 15 marks)

**Wine 11** — This dry, phenolic, skin-contact white has textural grip closer to a light red in mouthfeel than to a conventional aromatic white; it should be judged within the natural-wine, skin-contact category rather than against clean, reductive whites. Within that context the balance of tannin, acidity and savoury length shows real winemaking control, giving good to very good quality, comparable to a well-made artisanal amber wine retailing around $30-40. Commercially this is a niche, sommelier-led proposition — on-trade by-the-glass lists, natural-wine specialists and independent merchants are the natural channels, targeting adventurous drinkers who prize texture over fruit purity; volumes will always stay narrow.

**Wine 12** — The concentrated, nutty, rancio-driven style with high natural alcohol and a bone-dry finish makes this a serious sipping wine rather than an easy commercial white, closer in spirit to an aged oxidative Sherry than to table wine, though reached without fortification. The intensity, length and layered complexity are outstanding within this small, historic category — arguably equivalent to a well-aged Palo Cortado or old Solera Reserva in seriousness — meriting a price around $50-70 for a half bottle. Commercial potential is confined to fine-wine independents, specialist by-the-glass lists and collectors in markets with an established appetite for oxidative styles such as the UK, Japan and the US; it has no place in mainstream retail.
