# Mock answer — 2016 Paper 2 Question 5

The stem signals three flagship national reds, each so closely tied to a single origin that naming the variety is effectively naming the country — the reasoning runs in both directions at once.

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible, with particular reference to the grape variety used (3 x 12 marks)

**Wine 10.** Malbec. Opaque purple-black colour, dense blackberry, blueberry and violet fruit, and a cool graphite edge are firmer and fresher than the plush, jammy style typical of valley-floor fruit. Full body, ripe but grippy tannin and a punchy 14.5% alcohol confirm a hot, high-UV site, yet the finish stays linear rather than broad — evidence of acidity preserved by a marked diurnal swing. Cabernet Sauvignon was considered for the depth and structure, but its tannin here is angular rather than rounded and the violet lift sits closer to Malbec. That altitude-driven freshness points beyond Mendoza to Argentina's high-altitude Andean vineyards — Salta's Calchaquí Valley, among the highest commercial sites on earth.

**Wine 11.** Blaufränkisch. Medium ruby depth, sour cherry and blackberry-skin fruit, black pepper, dried herb and a faintly ferrous mineral note give a Central European rather than Mediterranean signal. High, tensile acidity and fine, grippy tannin at a restrained 13.5% alcohol rule out riper, warmer-climate reds. Pinot Noir was considered for the cherry fruit and savoury feel, but the deeper colour and firmer grip argue against it; Zweigelt lacks this wine's acid drive and spice. The peppery, sinewy structure funnels this to Austria, and specifically to Burgenland's limestone and schist soils in Mittelburgenland, the variety's most serious heartland.

**Wine 12.** Carménère. Deep ruby-purple colour with ripe black plum and cassis sits over an unmistakable green streak of bell pepper, black olive and smoked paprika — a pyrazine signature too pronounced for Merlot, the historic point of confusion until the variety was correctly identified in 1994. Full body, plush tannin and 14% alcohol show enough warmth to tame that greenness into savoury complexity rather than raw stalkiness. Merlot is eliminated on the herbal, olive-like edge; Cabernet Franc lacks this wine's soft, fleshy mid-palate. The ripe-yet-herbal balance points to Chile, and to Colchagua Valley specifically, whose warmth ripens Carménère fully.

## b) Discuss the key winemaking techniques used to arrive at this style (3 x 6 marks)

**Wine 10.** The saturated colour and dense tannin point to a firm, extended maceration on thick, sun-hardened skins. The polished, sweet-oak frame around the fruit suggests around 15 months in French oak, a meaningful proportion new, chosen to support rather than mask the fruit's intensity.

**Wine 11.** The absence of overt toast or vanilla, with fruit and spice left unobscured, argues for maturation in older, larger-format oak rather than new barrique — protecting the variety's peppery, savoury identity and naturally high acidity rather than adding a winemaking veneer.

**Wine 12.** Ripe but well-managed tannin alongside retained herbal lift suggests a warm, fairly extended fermentation for colour and tannin extraction, with careful cap management avoiding harsh pyrazine austerity. Oak reads as mixed — part new French for spice, part neutral — rounding the palate without erasing the variety's signature greenness.

## c) How would you sell this wine to a potential customer? (3 x 7 marks)

**Wine 10.** A dense, mountain-grown Malbec with freshness Mendoza examples often lack — ideal with chargrilled steak or empanadas. At around $18–22 through premium supermarkets and independents, it out-classes generic Mendoza Malbec on the same shelf, rewarding drinkers trading up without paying Bordeaux-red prices.

**Wine 11.** A savoury, structured red for drinkers bored of default Pinot Noir or Merlot, with acid and spice to partner duck, game or spiced sausage. At roughly $25–30 through independent specialists and by-the-glass lists, it offers point-of-difference — Austria's most serious, still under-known red — that Beaujolais Cru or Loire Cabernet Franc cannot tell.

**Wine 12.** A plush, herb-edged red showcasing Chile's own variety rather than another Bordeaux blend — pair with lamb or grilled vegetables. At around $15–20 in supermarkets and chain restaurants, it targets drinkers trading up from commercial Merlot, offering more complexity at a similar price.
