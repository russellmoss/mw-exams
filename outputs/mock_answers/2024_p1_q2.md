# Mock answer — 2024 Paper 1 Question 2

## a) Method of production (3 x 7 marks)

**Wine 7** — The buttery creaminess beneath the rose and citrus-blossom lift suggests a small barrel-fermented portion (French, American and acacia oak) blended into a mainly stainless-steel, cold-fermented base. The mid-palate phenolic grip is consistent with a pre-fermentation skin soak, and the round texture softening the racy acidity points to lees ageing with bâtonnage under spontaneous, wild-yeast fermentation.

**Wine 8** — The pure apricot and white-blossom fruit, with only a whisper of vanilla, indicates ageing in mostly older, neutral barriques and puncheons rather than new wood — enough barrel time to build textural richness and a faint saffron spice without masking the fruit. The absence of any buttery character suggests malolactic was largely avoided, preserving the fresh finish.

**Wine 9** — The pure, unblemished floral and lychee character, with no bitter phenolic edge, points to whole-cluster pressing rather than extended skin contact. Indigenous-yeast fermentation and ageing in old, neutral foudre explain the textural breadth and honeyed spice without overt toast or vanilla, while the unusually lifted acidity for the variety suggests a cooler, later-ripening season.

## b) Style, quality and commercial position (3 x 12 marks)

**Wine 7** — Style: bone dry, medium-bodied and aromatic, with a textural complexity from wild fermentation and partial barrel work that lifts it above the simple, tank-fermented commercial style this variety usually shows. Quality: very good for a Greek aromatic white, above basic varietal PGI bottlings and closer to a serious single-site Mantineia, retailing around $18-22. Commercial: suits Independent Specialists and Greek-focused restaurant lists in the US and UK, appealing to drinkers seeking an alternative to Alsace or Loire aromatics; competes with premium Muscat or dry Gewürztraminer.

**Wine 8** — Style: dry, medium-plus bodied, restrained and elegant rather than the broad, oily style Viognier can show from warmer sites — the moderate 13% alcohol and well-integrated oak keep it fresh rather than heavy. Quality: very good, a benchmark regional style for Eden Valley Viognier, above generic Barossa-floor examples, retailing around $20-25. Commercial: a strong by-the-glass and premium-supermarket listing in Australia, the UK and North America, aimed at Chardonnay drinkers moving toward scented, textural whites; competes with Condrieu at a fraction of the price.

**Wine 9** — Style: off-dry to medium, full-bodied and powerful, with residual sweetness held in check by this vintage's unusually high acidity — a serious, age-worthy wine rather than a simple aromatic quaffer. Quality: outstanding, Grand Cru level; concentration, purity and structure place it well above Village or generic Alsace Gewürztraminer, retailing around $50-65. Commercial: sold through Independent Specialists and fine-dining lists in France, the US and UK, targeting collectors and sommeliers; competes with other top-tier Grand Cru Gewürztraminer or late-harvest Alsace styles.

## c) Grape variety and origin (3 x 6 marks)

**Wine 7** — The rose-petal, orange-blossom and grapefruit-pith aromatics on a lighter, more citrus-driven palate than true Muscat point to Moschofilero. Muscat and Gewürztraminer were both considered but eliminated on the lighter phenolic weight, paler colour and racier acidity shown here. This profile, together with the moderate 13% alcohol and delicate structure, is classic Peloponnese — the Mantineia plateau.

**Wine 8** — Ripe apricot, ginger and white-blossom aromas on a broad, low-acid, oily-textured palate point to Viognier. Gewürztraminer was considered given the floral lift but ruled out by the stone-fruit rather than lychee-rose register and the lower phenolic grip. The restraint and freshness at 13% alcohol suggest a cooler New World site rather than a hot one — Eden Valley, South Australia.

**Wine 9** — The intense rose petal, lychee and Turkish-delight spice, oily texture and off-dry finish are unmistakably Gewürztraminer; Moschofilero was eliminated for lacking this weight, spice and touch of sweetness. The depth of fruit, elevated natural sugar and unusually structured acidity point to Alsace, and the layered spice and concentration place this at Grand Cru rather than village or generic Alsace level.
