# Mock answer — 2016 Paper 1 Question 5

## a) Identify the grape variety (4 x 8 marks)

**Wine 9** — Torrontés. The intensely perfumed nose of rose petal, lychee, peach blossom and jasmine reads as grapey and Muscat-like, which immediately raises Gewürztraminer or Moscato. But the palate contradicts the nose: it is dry to barely off-dry, medium-bodied, with a brisk, refreshing line of acidity that cuts straight through the tropical, floral fruit rather than the broader, oilier weight Gewürztraminer would carry. That aroma/palate mismatch — heady on the nose, taut and dry on the palate — combined with 13.9% alcohol, points to Torrontés grown at altitude, where night-time cooling preserves freshness despite full aromatic ripening.

**Wine 10** — Gewürztraminer. Unmistakable rose petal, musk and ginger spice with a honeyed, exotic-floral finish, a smooth, slightly oily texture and confirmed off-dry sweetness (around 9-10 g/L) set it apart from Wine 9's drier, leaner structure. Torrontés was the obvious alternative given the shared rose/lychee register, but the broader mouthfeel and genuine residual sugar here are diagnostic of Gewürztraminer rather than the grapey, bone-dry Argentine variety.

**Wine 11** — Chenin Blanc. Quince, green apple, honey, lanolin and chamomile over a searingly high-acid, medium-bodied palate is textbook Chenin. Riesling was briefly considered for the citrus-mineral thread, but the waxy, wool-like texture and honeyed weight, without Riesling's more linear, steely drive, rule it out in favour of Chenin.

**Wine 12** — Riesling. White peach, apricot, lime blossom and emerging petrol over a dry, high-acid palate with unusual richness and extract for the variety (13.5% alcohol, full body) is classic ripe Riesling. Chenin Blanc was eliminated as an alternative — its honeyed lanolin note is absent here, replaced by the more citrus-and-stone-fruit-driven, mineral-slate character specific to Riesling.

## b) Identify the origin as closely as possible (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 9** — Salta, Cafayate Valley, Argentina. This aromatic intensity paired with real acid retention at nearly 14% alcohol points to high-altitude viticulture; coastal Chile was eliminated as it lacks the diurnal swing to deliver both this ripeness and this freshness together. Cafayate's extreme day-night temperature variation is the specific New World signature for the variety.

**Wine 10** — New Zealand, most likely Nelson. Alsace was considered but ruled out by the brighter, more citrus-tinged fruit and less phenolic grip than an Alsatian example would show at this alcohol. Within New Zealand, the tighter, saline-edged minerality alongside the ginger-spice fruit is more consistent with Nelson's stony alluvial sites than the riper, more overtly tropical drive typical of Marlborough.

**Wine 11** — Vouvray, Loire Valley, France. South African Chenin was eliminated — riper, more tropical fruit and higher alcohol would be expected there. The searing acidity, honey-lanolin complexity and moderate 12.5% alcohol are Loire hallmarks, and the demi-sec-leaning off-dry style with real ageing potential is squarely Vouvray rather than the drier Savennières further downstream.

**Wine 12** — Wachau, Austria. Alsace Riesling was eliminated for showing riper stone fruit and softer acid at comparable weight; German Riesling was eliminated because equivalent ripeness there is usually carried with residual sugar rather than fully dry. The combination of full body, high extract and bone-dry finish at 13.5% alcohol is the Wachau's own idiom, more Smaragd than Federspiel in its concentration.

## c) Comment on quality and state of maturity (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 9** — Good, commercial quality, equivalent to Argentina's IG Salta / Vino de Mesa tier, retailing for around $12-15. Pale, brilliant and vividly primary, this is a wine of around one year old, tasted at its intended freshness peak. Torrontés is not built to age — it is at its very best now and will lose its aromatic lift within another year rather than gain complexity; there is no benefit to holding it.

**Wine 10** — Very good quality, above generic entry level, comparable to a well-made regional New Zealand GI wine at around $20 retail. The pale straw colour and still-primary rose and ginger fruit suggest a wine of about two years old, currently drinking well. It may hold its aromatic freshness for a further one to two years but is unlikely to develop meaningful tertiary complexity; best enjoyed in its youth.

**Wine 11** — Very good quality, comparable to a well-regarded village-level Vouvray, retailing for around $20-25. At three years old the pale gold colour and honeyed-but-still-fresh profile show early development rather than full maturity. Chenin's naturally high acidity gives this wine real capacity to improve over the next five to eight years, gaining deeper honeyed, waxy and nutty complexity, and it should hold well beyond a decade given Vouvray's known longevity.

**Wine 12** — Outstanding quality, equivalent to a top Smaragd-level Wachau bottling, retailing for around $40-50. The developing gold colour and emerging petrol note alongside still-vivid peach and lime fruit place this wine at around four years old, just entering its drinking window. The concentration and extract suggest it will continue to improve for five to ten more years, developing deeper honeyed and kerosene complexity, and should hold comfortably for fifteen years or more from vintage.
