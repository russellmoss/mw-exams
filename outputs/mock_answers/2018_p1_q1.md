# Mock answer — 2018 Paper 1 Question 1

## a) Identify the grape variety (20 marks)

Chardonnay. The four wines share a family fingerprint that runs across a huge stylistic range but keeps reappearing: ripe stone and orchard fruit as the fruit core in every glass, from simple peach and melon on Wine 1 through white peach on Wine 2 to pineapple and ripe apple on Wines 3 and 4. The variety's hallmark neutrality of primary aroma — no floral, herbal or spicy signature of its own — leaves winemaking to do the talking, and that is exactly the pattern here: everything from a barely-touched commercial style through a mineral, barrel-fermented Old World wine to two overtly oaked, malolactic New World examples. Chenin Blanc and Sémillon were both considered given the oak and lees handling seen on Wines 2–4, but neither fits: Chenin would show more orchard/quince acidity and a waxy, sometimes honeyed edge even in dry styles, and Sémillon would carry more lanolin and less crisp citrus drive than the flint and lime zest on Wine 2. The combination of moderate-to-high alcohol (12.5–14.9%), dry finish and adaptability to both stainless steel and heavy new oak across four very different climates points conclusively to Chardonnay.

## b) Identify the origin as closely as possible

**Wine 1 (10 marks)** — South Eastern Australia, generic GI blend. Pale straw colour, simple peach and melon fruit, soft low acidity and a clean but short finish with no minerality or oak signature indicate a warm, high-yield, multi-region source rather than a defined site. This lack of site expression, combined with 13% alcohol and an uncomplicated, fruit-forward profile, is consistent with a large-volume South Eastern Australia blend rather than a single GI such as Adelaide Hills or Margaret River.

**Wine 2 (10 marks)** — Chablis, Grand Cru level, Burgundy. The flint, lime zest and oyster-shell salinity are the calling card of Kimmeridgean limestone, and the taut, high acidity at a comparatively low 12.5% alcohol confirms a cool, northern French site. The depth of fruit, textural richness and emerging notes of beeswax and toast — unusual for village or 1er Cru Chablis at this stage — point to a Grand Cru climat rather than a lesser tier.

**Wine 3 (10 marks)** — Napa Valley, California, Carneros-influenced. Deep golden colour, pineapple, roasted hazelnut and crème brûlée aromatics with a creamy, low-acid palate at 14.9% alcohol scream ripe Californian fruit and heavy new-oak handling. The moderating streak of acidity beneath the richness suggests a cooler corner of Napa rather than the valley floor, pointing to a Carneros-sourced site with San Pablo Bay influence.

**Wine 4 (10 marks)** — Auckland, New Zealand, single-vineyard Kumeu. Struck-flint reduction alongside white peach and toasted hazelnut, a savoury lees-driven palate and a firmer acid backbone than Wine 3 despite similar 14% alcohol point to a maritime New World site worked in a deliberately Burgundian manner. This combination — precision and structure rather than sheer opulence — narrows the origin to Kumeu/Auckland rather than a warmer Australian or Californian equivalent.

## c) Discuss quality with reference to winemaking techniques used

**Wine 1 (10 marks)** — Acceptable, entry-level commercial quality, retailing around $8–10. The clean, simple fruit and absence of flaws show competent, hygienic stainless-steel fermentation with no new oak and minimal lees contact, techniques chosen deliberately to preserve fruit and cut cost at scale rather than build complexity. Correct for its category but with no length or textural interest beyond the primary fruit.

**Wine 2 (10 marks)** — Outstanding quality, Grand Cru level, retailing around $70–90. Whole-cluster pressing preserves the pure citrus and mineral core, while a restrained mix of older barrel and stainless with extended lees contact builds textural richness without masking site character. The crystalline acidity, saline length and now-emerging secondary complexity after six years confirm both the classification and considerable ageing potential.

**Wine 3 (10 marks)** — Very good quality, premium single-estate Napa level, around $45–55. Full barrel fermentation in a high proportion of new French oak, complete malolactic fermentation and extended lees ageing with bâtonnage explain the toasty, creamy, opulent style; the wine is well made and true to its intentional house idiom, though the lower acidity and pronounced oak leave it less complex and site-transparent than Wine 2.

**Wine 4 (10 marks)** — Outstanding quality, single-vineyard New Zealand level, around $40–60. Whole-cluster pressing, wild-yeast barrel fermentation, full malolactic fermentation and extended lees ageing with periodic bâtonnage are applied with genuine restraint, delivering flinty reduction and savoury complexity alongside real structural cut. The judicious balance of richness and acid drive marks this as a serious, age-worthy wine that rivals — without imitating — the Old World benchmark in this flight.
