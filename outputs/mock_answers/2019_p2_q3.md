# Mock answer — 2019 Paper 2 Question 3

Wines 9–12 are stated to be European but not French, Italian or Spanish — a stem exclusion that immediately funnels the flight toward Germany, Austria, Portugal, Hungary and the wider Balkan/Central European arc. All four show restrained alcohol (13–13.5%), retained acidity and medium body, consistent with cool-to-moderate continental or Atlantic-influenced sites rather than the riper southern-European mainstream. Given the question's own weighting — quality and commercial potential first, maturity second, origin and variety last and worth least — the identification calls below are offered as the most probable reading of the evidence rather than certainties.

## a) Quality and commercial potential (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 9.** Pale ruby, pure red-fruit precision, racy acidity and fine, silky tannin with a distinct wet-slate minerality signal very good to outstanding quality — comparable to an Erste Lage-level Spätburgunder, retailing around $45–55. Commercially this is a niche, sommelier-led proposition: Independent Specialists and fine-dining by-the-glass lists targeting Burgundy drinkers seeking an alternative, with export interest concentrated in the UK and US enthusiast trade; the challenge is low consumer awareness of quality German Pinot Noir.

**Wine 10.** Firm acidity, dark cherry fruit, peppery spice and a savoury mineral edge point to very good quality, equivalent to a single-vineyard DAC bottling, around $25–35 retail. It suits Independent Specialists and adventurous on-trade lists as an alternative to mid-tier Rhône or Cru Beaujolais, with growing export traction in Germany, Scandinavia and the US; the obstacle remains limited recognition of the grape name itself.

**Wine 11.** Deep colour, concentrated dark fruit, robust yet integrated tannin and real length indicate outstanding quality, on a par with a premium single-quinta Douro red, around $35–45. This has strong commercial potential in fine-dining and Independent Specialist channels as Portugal's unfortified reds gain export recognition beyond Port in the UK and US, competing with premium Priorat or Rhône; the challenge is shifting consumer perception of the Douro away from fortified wine alone.

**Wine 12.** Ripe, velvety tannin, plush dark fruit and a graphite edge suggest very good quality, comparable to a premium single-estate Central European red, around $30–40. It offers on-trade and Independent Specialist appeal as an accessible Bordeaux-variety alternative, with export opportunity in the UK, Germany and Benelux; low regional brand recognition is the main commercial barrier.

## b) Maturity (4 x 8 marks)

**Wine 9.** Pale colour and pure primary red fruit place this at around 3–4 years old; drinking well now but with enough acid-driven freshness to improve for a further 2–3 years, holding for 5–6, developing forest-floor and dried-cherry complexity.

**Wine 10.** The still-violet rim and firm, grippy tannin suggest a wine of about 3–4 years; approachable now, it should improve over the next 2–3 years as tannins resolve, holding for 4–5 more, gaining dried herb and leather notes.

**Wine 11.** Deep, youthful colour with ripe, structured tannin points to roughly 3–4 years of age; still primary, it has the concentration to improve for 5–8 years and hold for a decade or more, developing tobacco, leather and dried fig.

**Wine 12.** Ripe, already-softened tannin and moderate acidity indicate around 3–4 years; drinking well now, with capacity to improve for a further 2–3 years and hold for 4–5, gaining cedar and dried-fruit complexity.

## c) Possible origin and grape variety (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 9.** The pale hue, cranberry-redcurrant purity, racy acidity and light-to-medium body point to Pinot Noir over Zweigelt or St Laurent, both of which would show brighter, less structured fruit. The pronounced wet-slate minerality and a relatively high 13.5% for the style suggest a warm vintage on a classically cool, slate-based site — most probably the Mosel/Ruwer in Germany, though a cool-climate Alsace example cannot be entirely excluded.

**Wine 10.** Dark cherry and bilberry fruit with black pepper, dried herb and mineral earthiness, on firm acid and moderate-to-high tannin, suggest Blaufränkisch rather than the softer, more confected Zweigelt or the gentler St Laurent. The ripe but not jammy fruit with limestone-edged austerity points most likely to Austria's Burgenland, probably the Leithaberg zone rather than the riper Mittelburgenland style.

**Wine 11.** Multi-layered dark fruit, schist-derived minerality and complex spice without one dominant varietal signature suggest a field blend of indigenous Iberian varieties rather than a single-variety wine. The sun-baked concentration and firm, ripe tannin most plausibly indicate the Douro Valley, Portugal, possibly its warmer Douro Superior sub-zone.

**Wine 12.** Ripe black cherry and plum with graphite and dark chocolate, velvety rather than green tannin, and moderate acidity point to Cabernet Franc rather than Merlot, whose fruit would be softer and less graphite-edged. The riper, fuller expression than a typical Loire example suggests a warm continental climate, most probably Hungary's Villány region, its recognised benchmark for Bordeaux varieties.
