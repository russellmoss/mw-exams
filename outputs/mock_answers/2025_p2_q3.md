# Mock answer — 2025 Paper 2 Question 3

The stem signals that each of these five wines is a regional signature — the variety call and the origin call are effectively the same act of reasoning, so evidence is spent once and used twice.

## a) Identify the origin and the grape variety as closely as possible (5 x 10 marks)

**Wine 8.** Deep ruby-violet colour, a concentrated core of blackberry and black currant with cedar, violet, cinnamon and a balsamic lift, broad velvety tannin, 14% alcohol and a long finish with a chalky, schist-like mineral edge point to Touriga Nacional. Syrah was considered for the peppery lift but rejected — this fruit is darker and more floral, with a violet note and firmer, more extracted tannin than Syrah's typically peppery, savoury frame. The schist-derived minerality, the granite-lagare tannic grip and the new-oak polish funnel this to the Douro, Portugal's noblest red variety in its home terroir.

**Wine 9.** Bright, lighter ruby, crunchy dark cherry and wild berry fruit, a floral lift, modest tannin, only light spice-oak framing and 12.5% alcohol suggest a fresh, mid-weight Central European red. Blaufränkisch was eliminated — it typically shows deeper colour and firmer, more grippy tannin than this juicy, easy-drinking profile. The bright fruit, loess-soil freshness and gentle oak touch point to Zweigelt, Austria's own crossing and its most widely planted red, here from Niederösterreich.

**Wine 10.** Deep ruby with a youthful purple rim, ripe red and black cherry, plum, violet and a touch of leather, but the defining feature is the acid-tannin split: searingly high acidity paired with notably soft, low tannin at a full 14.5% alcohol. Nebbiolo was the obvious alternative given the Piedmont-typical violet lift, but its high tannin and characteristically pale garnet hue rule it out — this wine is far deeper in colour and far softer in grip. That acid-high/tannin-low combination is close to diagnostic for Barbera, confirmed by the limestone-clay soils and mixed cask-and-barrique ageing of Alba.

**Wine 11.** Bright ruby-purple, lifted raspberry and red cherry, blossom and a granitic mineral edge, juicy acidity, light-to-medium body and 13% alcohol — the lowest alcohol in the flight — point to Gamay. Pinot Noir was considered for the pale, fresh red-fruit profile but eliminated: this wine lacks Pinot's savoury, earthy undertow and shows a brighter, more primary immediacy with softer tannin. The pink granite soils and Villages-level structure place this in Beaujolais.

**Wine 12.** Medium ruby-purple, small red and blacker berry fruit, leather, pepper, clove and overt oak spice, with high acidity, firm structured tannin and a long, quince-tinged finish at 14% alcohol. This is the flight's trap: the high-acid, high-tannin frame and savoury, faintly tarry register invite a Nebbiolo call. It is eliminated on colour depth (Nebbiolo is characteristically pale garnet even young, owing to a low anthocyanin-to-tannin ratio, whereas this wine shows real ruby-purple depth) and on fruit register (darker berry fruit and a dried-tomato, olive-adjacent savouriness rather than Nebbiolo's rose-petal and tar). That combination of Mediterranean savouriness with high-altitude freshness points to Xinomavro, and specifically to Amyndeon in northern Greece.

## b) Comment on the quality within the context of wine globally (5 x 7 marks)

Wine 8 is very good quality, a well-balanced, single-estate Douro red at roughly $25–35 retail, showing good concentration and integrated new oak, though it lacks the depth and old-vine field-blend complexity of a flagship Douro red above $60. Wine 9 is good, reliable commercial quality — an appellation-level Niederösterreich red around $12–18, fresh and true to type but modest in concentration. Wine 10 is very good quality, a polished, multi-village Barbera d'Alba DOC around $20–25 — above simple trattoria Barbera but short of Nizza DOCG or superiore-level intensity. Wine 11 is good to very good, a near-cru Beaujolais-Villages around $18–22 with more depth than generic Beaujolais AOC but less concentration than a named cru such as Fleurie or Morgon. Wine 12 is outstanding quality, comparable to a leading single-vineyard Amyndeon or Naoussa bottling at $30–40, with the structure and length to rival fine Nebbiolo.

## c) Comment on the style and commercial appeal (5 x 8 marks)

Wine 8 is a modern, internationally styled, full-bodied dry Douro red suited to independent specialists and premium on-trade lists at $25–35, appealing to New World Cabernet or Shiraz drinkers trading up into Iberian reds, with strongest appeal in the US and UK. Wine 9 is a fresh, fruit-forward, lightly oaked, easy-drinking style for supermarket shelves and by-the-glass on-trade at $12–18, competing with light Beaujolais or Valpolicella and finding its main audience domestically and in Germany. Wine 10 is a polished, fruit-forward Barbera with sweet oak texture, suited to independent retail and mid-tier restaurant lists at $20–25, competing with premium Chianti or Montepulciano for drinkers moving up from everyday Italian reds. Wine 11 is a crunchy, minimally worked, unoaked Villages Gamay for wine bars and natural-leaning independents at $18–22, competing with cru Beaujolais and appealing to low-intervention consumers, especially in the US and UK on-trade. Wine 12 is a structured, age-worthy, savoury modern Greek red for specialist fine-wine merchants and ambitious restaurant lists at $30–40, positioned against mid-tier Nebbiolo or northern Rhône Syrah and gaining traction with adventurous sommeliers in the US, Germany and the Nordics.
