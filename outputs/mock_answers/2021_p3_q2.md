# Mock answer — 2021 Paper 3 Question 2

## a) Method of production, with reference to residual sugar and alcohol (5 x 10 marks)

**Wine 4** — 16% and technically dry rules out fortification: no spirit addition could leave a wine bone dry at this strength, so the alcohol must have come entirely from grape sugar. This points to *appassimento*: grapes dried on racks or crates for three to four months, concentrating sugar before a long, warm fermentation runs to completion. The raisined, fig and dried-cherry concentration alongside firm, ripe tannin and a bitter-almond finish confirm the drying rather than simple late harvest.

**Wine 5** — 5.5% alcohol with obvious sweetness and a gentle mousse indicates fermentation was arrested very early, long before dryness, under pressure in a sealed tank (the Asti/Martinotti method), trapping both residual sugar and carbon dioxide. The intensely floral, grapey rose-and-strawberry perfume is the signature of an aromatic Muscat-family variety rather than a neutral base — this is not a still wine with added sweetness, it is a single-tank arrested ferment.

**Wine 6** — 20% alcohol combined with pronounced sweetness cannot arise from fermentation alone; the sugar level is too high for a dry ferment to reach 20%. This is fortification mid-ferment: neutral grape spirit added when roughly 90-100 g/L of sugar remained, killing the yeast and locking in both strength and sweetness. Rapid, hot extraction in shallow vessels ahead of fortification explains the deep, saturated colour and firm grip even after two decades in bottle.

**Wine 7** — 15.5% and sweet again signals *mutage* — spirit added partway through fermentation — but at a lower target strength than Wine 6, consistent with the vin doux naturel method rather than Port-style fortification. The oxidative, mocha-and-dried-fig complexity and lack of primary fruit point to extended ageing in old wood or glass demijohn after fortification, developing rancio character rather than the fresher, bottle-aged profile of Wine 6.

**Wine 8** — 18.5% and intensely, viscously sweet, well beyond the sweetness of Wines 6 or 7, indicates very late, partly raisined harvest fruit fortified after minimal fermentation, preserving almost all natural sugar. The tawny-amber colour with an olive rim and baked, coffee-toffee complexity point to a fractional, solera-style blending system in small old barrels exposed to heat cycling — the stated age represents an average across blended parcels, not a single vintage.

## b) Origin and grape variety (5 x 10 marks)

**Wine 4** — The dried, structured, still-dry profile at 16% is distinctively Italian in style; Corvina-led blends are the only major variety group producing this drying-then-fermenting-to-dryness style at volume. The bitter-almond, tobacco and dried-cherry character funnels to Corvina Veronese with Rondinella (and possibly Molinara or Oseleta) from Valpolicella, Veneto — Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG.

**Wine 5** — The low-alcohol, sweet, lightly frizzante red with a rose-and-strawberry aromatic signature is unmistakably Brachetto rather than Lambrusco (drier, more savoury) or Moscato (white). This narrows to the Acqui Terme zone of Piedmont, Italy — Brachetto d'Acqui DOCG.

**Wine 6** — The 20% fortified sweet red with granite-influenced structure and a traditional field-blend character (dark stone fruit, walnut, cedar) is characteristic of the Douro's indigenous varieties — principally Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz and Tinta Barroca — from the Upper Douro, Portugal: Vintage Port.

**Wine 7** — The 15.5% fortified sweet red with dark cherry, fig and rancio complexity, distinguished from Port by its lower fortification strength and more oxidative, chocolate-and-mocha register, points to Grenache Noir-based vin doux naturel from schist soils in the Agly Valley, Roussillon, France — Maury AOC.

**Wine 8** — The intensely sweet, tawny, coffee-and-dried-orange fortified wine with extreme viscosity has no direct parallel outside northeast Victoria; this is Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains (Rutherglen/Brown Muscat) from Rutherglen, Australia.

## c) Style and quality within the context of origin (5 x 5 marks)

**Wine 4** — A very good, structured Amarone; the depth of dried-fruit concentration and integrated tannin place it above basic DOCG level, though it lacks the extra layering of a Classico or Riserva bottling — comparable to a solid single-vineyard release retailing around £35-45.

**Wine 5** — A well-made, correctly balanced example of a lesser-known DOCG; fresh acidity keeps the sweetness in check. Good commercial quality at entry-to-mid DOCG level, priced around £12-15 — a crowd-pleasing curiosity rather than a serious ageworthy wine.

**Wine 6** — An outstanding, classically structured Vintage Port from a well-regarded, widely declared year; now at 21 years the tannins have softened and integrated while retaining real concentration — equivalent to a premium shipper's vintage declaration, £70-90.

**Wine 7** — A very good, characterful Maury showing genuine rancio complexity for its style tier; the "Grande Reserve" designation suggests it sits above basic Maury but below the rarest old-solera bottlings — around £20-25, strong typicity for the appellation.

**Wine 8** — An excellent, richly layered example at the Classic tier of Rutherglen's four-step hierarchy — well above entry Rutherglen but below Grand or Rare — showing real complexity for around £20-30 a half-bottle, true to house style.
