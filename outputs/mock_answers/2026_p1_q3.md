# Mock answer — 2026 Paper 1 Question 3

## a) Grape variety and origin (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 10.** Pale lemon-green with pure white peach, lemon zest and green apple, lifted by a pine/maquis herbal note and a saline finish on a racy, medium-bodied, unoaked palate — this herb-and-salt combination points to Vermentino rather than Verdicchio (more almond-driven) or Falanghina (riper, more tropical). The saline, coastal-mineral edge and DOC-level purity indicate an Italian island origin; Sardegna is the natural home, ahead of Liguria, given the more overtly salty, less floral register typical of Sardinian Vermentino.

**Wine 11.** Bone-dry, high-acid and saline, with citrus and orchard fruit on the nose and grapefruit-pith bitterness on the finish, alongside fully integrated 13% alcohol — this searing acid retention paired with phenolic grip is the calling card of Assyrtiko, not Vermentino (accounted for in Wine 10, less phenolic) or Picpoul (softer, less mineral). The volcanic minerality and racy structure despite evident ripeness point specifically to Santorini, whose pumice soils and ancient bush vines are famous for this freshness-under-heat signature.

**Wine 12.** Honeysuckle, orange blossom, peach and pear on a pale gold, medium-plus, creamy-textured palate with saline minerality, a bitter almond/citrus-pith finish and only moderate acidity suggest a Rhône-family white blend rather than a single aromatic variety. The neutral, waxy profile with restrained fruit concentration rules out Roussanne or Marsanne as the lead component (too much apricot/marzipan weight expected); the bitter-almond signature and moderate acid are more consistent with Clairette as the principal component, blended with Bourboulenc. The chalky salinity and structural finesse point to Bandol in Provence.

## b) Winemaking and freshness (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 10.** The purity of fruit and lack of any bruised or oxidative note suggest gentle, free-run-only pressing and cold settling of the must — protective handling designed to exclude solids and phenolics that would blunt freshness. The clean, racy acid with no buttery softening indicates a cool, controlled fermentation in stainless steel with malolactic avoided or minimal; the silky lees texture without oxidative weight points to a short period of fine-lees ageing in inert vessel, adding mid-palate texture while protecting the zesty, saline finish.

**Wine 11.** The taut acidity and grapefruit-pith bitterness, retained despite a warm, drought vintage, suggest fully reductive handling: gentle, light-skin-contact pressing to control phenolics, followed by cool fermentation entirely in stainless steel. The complete absence of oak or buttery character indicates malolactic was blocked, locking in the sharp, linear acid backbone; short, protective lees ageing pre-bottling explains the textural roundness without dulling the wine's cutting freshness.

**Wine 12.** The creamy, textured mid-palate against only moderate acidity suggests indigenous-yeast fermentation and extended lees contact, rather than an emphasis purely on primary purity. Ageing in a mix of stainless steel and large, older barrels — evidenced by textural gain with no toast, vanilla or new-oak spice — shows a deliberate choice of neutral wood to build structure without masking freshness. The clean, bitter-almond, saline finish with no dairy or hazelnut richness suggests malolactic was limited or blocked, preserving the acid line that the moderate-acid fruit alone would not otherwise carry.

## c) Commercial position (3 x 5 marks)

**Wine 10.** A good quality, DOC-level Sardinian white retailing around $15-18, well placed on premium supermarket shelves and by-the-glass on Italian-focused restaurant lists, competing with Vermentino di Gallura and Picpoul de Pinet for consumers seeking a crisp, saline seafood white; strong fit for the UK and US on-trade Italian category.

**Wine 11.** Santorini's tiny, drought-reduced yields and prestige narrative justify a premium price of around $28-35, sold mainly through independent specialists and fine-dining by-the-glass programmes; it competes with premium Sancerre or Chablis as a mineral-driven alternative, with growing export demand in the US and UK.

**Wine 12.** An organic-certified, single-site Bandol white commanding $35-45 through fine-wine independents and high-end restaurant lists, appealing to collectors of the estate's red Bandol seeking a food-focused white; it competes with white Châteauneuf-du-Pape, though its tiny production limits it to a niche, allocation-driven export market.
