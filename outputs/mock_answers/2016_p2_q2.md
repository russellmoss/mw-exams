# Mock answer — 2016 Paper 2 Question 2

## a) Identify the grape variety as closely as possible (15 marks)

Pinot Noir. All three wines share pale-to-medium ruby colour rather than the deep purple of thicker-skinned varieties, fine, silky rather than grippy tannin, and a red-fruited rather than black-fruited profile — red cherry, cranberry and strawberry running through all three glasses. Wine 3 adds a savoury, herbal, faintly reductive edge; Wine 4 shows a similar fine-grained structure with a smoky, mineral topnote; Wine 5 is simpler and more fruit-forward but shares the same light tannin and moderate body. Gamay was briefly considered for the pale colour and red-fruit character, but the fine, structured tannin and the savoury/earthy complexity in Wines 3 and 4 go beyond Gamay's typically simple, juicy style. Grenache was also considered for Wine 5's lighter colour, but its tannin is too fine and its acidity too fresh for Grenache, which usually shows more alcohol warmth and a drier, spicier finish. The stem confirms all three are the same variety and rules out France, so the consistent thread of pale colour, fine tannin, red fruit and savoury lift across three very different climates points firmly to Pinot Noir grown outside Burgundy.

## b) Identify the origin as closely as possible, with particular reference to the climate (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 3** — Marlborough, New Zealand. The bright ruby colour, ripe but not jammy red cherry fruit and 14% alcohol point to a sunny, maritime climate with high diurnal range rather than a marginal one — this rules out cool continental Europe. The herbal, dried-flower lift alongside brisk acidity and fine tannin is typical of the clay-based Southern Valleys sites that give Marlborough Pinot Noir its fuller, more structured, less overtly fruity style than the region's earlier, lighter examples.

**Wine 4** — Ahr, Germany. The pale colour, crunchy acidity and only 13.5% alcohol immediately suggest a genuinely marginal, cool climate rather than a New World one. The smoky, mineral edge and taut, savoury red-fruit profile point specifically to the Ahr, Germany's most northerly red-wine region, where narrow, steep, south-facing slate slopes trap and radiate heat, allowing Pinot Noir to ripen fully in an otherwise unsuitable climate — the slate itself is the mechanism behind the wine's mineral tension.

**Wine 5** — South Eastern Australia. The lightest colour and lowest alcohol at 12.5% could suggest a cool site, but the simple, fruit-led red cherry and strawberry character, soft tannin and lack of savoury complexity instead point to early picking for an accessible commercial style rather than genuine cool-climate expression. The broad, undefined regional character rules out a specific cool GI such as Yarra Valley or Tasmania and instead points to a blended, multi-region sourcing base.

## c) Comment on quality and consider key selling points (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 3** is very good quality, comparable to a premium single-estate New Zealand Pinot Noir, with real textural depth and ageing potential; its point of difference is the savoury, food-friendly style rather than fruit sweetness — a strong fit for Independent Specialists and premium on-trade at around £30, competing with village-level Burgundy for consumers wanting more fruit generosity.

**Wine 4** is very good quality for the Ahr, showing site-driven minerality and fine structure that lift it above simple Qualitätswein trocken level; its selling point is novelty and terroir story — a rare, small-production German red for Independent Specialists at around £35, appealing to Burgundy drinkers seeking something different.

**Wine 5** is an acceptable, entry-level commercial style at around £10, competing on supermarket shelves against branded New World Pinot Noir; its selling point is easy, fruit-forward drinkability and reliable availability rather than complexity or ageing potential.
