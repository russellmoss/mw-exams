# Mock answer — 2019 Paper 3 Question 3

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible (4 x 6 marks)

**Wine 5** — Côtes de Provence, France. The very pale salmon-pink hue, red berry and white peach fruit, garrigue herbal lift and stony minerality on a light-medium, bone-dry palate at 13% point to a Grenache/Cinsault-led Mediterranean blend. Tavel was considered but its typically deeper colour and fuller structure do not fit this pale, precise style; a generic Languedoc IGP rosé was also ruled out by the wine's refinement and length. This is the paler of the two Old World wines and lands squarely in Provence.

**Wine 6** — Rioja, Spain. Against Wine 5 this shows a distinctly deeper pink, riper red cherry and strawberry fruit, a savoury, slightly earthy grip and fuller body at a higher 13.5% alcohol. That combination of structure and rusticity, rather than the more floral, Grenache-driven freshness of a Navarra rosado, points to Tempranillo, and with France already accounted for, this must be the second Old World wine — Spain, specifically Rioja.

**Wine 7** — Russian River Valley, Sonoma, USA. The lowest alcohol in the flight (12.5%), a delicate raspberry-strawberry-watermelon profile with a floral lift, and racy, cool-climate acidity all argue against a warm New World site. The clean, polished fruit character (rather than Grenache's herbal register) points to Pinot Noir, and the precision and freshness are consistent with a fog-cooled Californian AVA rather than a warmer New World rosé — Sonoma's Russian River Valley.

**Wine 8** — New Zealand. This is the remaining New World slot once California is accounted for. The riper, more overtly fruit-forward tropical-and-stone-fruit register, a touch of residual sweetness and generous acidity, without the varietal precision of Wine 7, suggest a commercially blended New World rosé rather than a single-site Pinot Noir. The bright, sunshine-driven fruit character points most plausibly to a Marlborough-sourced New Zealand rosé.

## b) Comment on the method of production (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 5** shows the palest colour and cleanest fruit in the flight, with no phenolic grip — consistent with direct pressing of whole clusters and minimal skin contact, rather than saignée. Cool, temperature-controlled fermentation in stainless steel with no oak preserves the primary fruit, and early bottling protects the delicate aromatics; this is the archetypal premium Provence template that now sets the industry's colour benchmark.

**Wine 6** is markedly deeper in colour with more phenolic weight, indicating a longer, deliberate period of skin contact — likely 24–48 hours — before the juice is drained off, rather than the fleeting contact seen in Wine 5. Fermentation at a slightly warmer control point than a delicate rosé would allow preserves Tempranillo's more savoury fruit; again no oak, and an early release to keep the wine fresh.

**Wine 7's** pale-medium hue and pure red-fruit precision point to a brief skin contact or direct press, with cool stainless fermentation protecting Pinot Noir's aromatic delicacy — no evidence of oak influence. **Wine 8** shows a similar pale-medium colour but riper, broader fruit and a hint of sweetness, suggesting a short maceration with fermentation halted marginally short of full dryness to soften the wine for commercial appeal; both are bottled early on their fruit. Across the flight, the trend toward ever-paler colour (most extreme in Wine 5) reflects a marketing-driven stylistic choice as much as a quality signal — deeper colour does not mean lower quality, simply a different, food-friendlier intention.

## c) Comment on the style, quality, and commercial positioning (4 x 12 marks)

**Wine 5** is outstanding quality — pure, precise, long and typical, the benchmark against which global rosé is judged, equivalent to the top commercial tier of Côtes de Provence retailing at £18–25 in UK off-trade and by the glass in premium on-trade. The moderate 13% alcohol despite a warm vintage suggests fruit was picked deliberately early for rosé rather than left to ripen for red — the hallmark of a producer treating pink wine as a primary style, not a by-product. It targets aspirational lifestyle consumers in the UK, US and increasingly Asian markets; the challenge is rising fruit costs and commoditization as demand outstrips supply.

**Wine 6** is good to very good quality, fuller and more gastronomic than the Provence style, retailing around £9–12. It targets drinkers wanting a food-friendly pink wine for tapas-style dining, competing with Navarra rosado and Provence imitators; the opportunity lies in leveraging Rioja's red-wine reputation into a repositioned rosado category, with the challenge being to shake off rosado's historic image as a lesser, by-product style.

**Wine 7** is very good quality, a premium varietal statement retailing around $18–22 in premium US supermarkets and independents, targeting consumers seeking Provence-style delicacy with Pinot Noir cachet. It competes with premium Californian and Oregon rosés and imported Provence; the opportunity lies in the ongoing US premiumization of rosé, though demand remains strongly seasonal.

**Wine 8** is good quality, an accessible commercial tier around £9–12 in UK and export supermarkets — sound and crowd-pleasing rather than complex. It targets casual, price-conscious drinkers, competing with other branded New World rosé; the opportunity is cross-selling through New Zealand's strong Sauvignon Blanc distribution networks, with the challenge being a lack of distinct terroir identity to justify premiumization.
