# Mock answer — 2019 Paper 2 Question 2

The stem confirms four wines from four different countries of the Americas, so each is treated as that country's calling card: variety first, then structural evidence to fix the country and region as closely as possible.

## Wine 5

**a) Origin and variety (15 marks).** Pale-to-medium ruby with a brick-tinged rim, red cherry, cranberry and a savoury, faintly earthy undertone, fine silky tannin and bright acidity at 13.5% point to Pinot Noir — the pale colour and delicate tannin rule out Grenache, and the modest tannin without tar or rose aromatics rules out Nebbiolo. The restrained oak and savoury lift argue against a riper Russian River or Sonoma Coast style, instead suggesting a genuinely cool, fog-influenced California site: the fog-funnelled Santa Maria Valley of Santa Barbara County, which gives Pinot Noir this Burgundian restraint rather than California's more typical fruit-forward register.

**b) Quality and market position (10 marks).** The pure red-fruit intensity, fine tannin and long finish indicate very good to outstanding quality, on a par with a premium single-vineyard Californian Pinot Noir, retailing around $35–55. It suits Independent Specialists and premium by-the-glass on-trade, competing against village-level Burgundy or top Oregon Pinot, and appeals to drinkers who favour restraint over fruit-forwardness; UK and Japan are natural export markets.

## Wine 6

**a) Origin and variety (15 marks).** Deep, dense violet-black colour, a floral violet lift over ripe plum and blueberry, plush velvety tannin and chocolate-mocha oak at 14% point to Malbec — the floral lift and rounder tannin separate it from Cabernet Sauvignon's firmer, cassis-edged grip, and the absence of cracked pepper rules out Syrah. That the wine retains real freshness despite its ripeness is the regional clue: the signature of high-altitude viticulture rather than a warm, low-lying site, funnelling the call to Argentina and specifically Mendoza, where the diurnal swing of the Andean foothills — likely Luján de Cuyo or the Uco Valley — preserves acidity a flatland Malbec of this ripeness would lack.

**b) Quality and market position (10 marks).** Concentration, polish and structural grip put this at a very good, premium single-estate tier of Mendoza Malbec, above generic bottlings at $10–15 and comparable to top Uco Valley estate wines at $20–35. It sits on domestic and US steakhouse on-trade lists and premium UK/European supermarket ranges, competing with New World Bordeaux-blend reds; the US, UK and Brazil are its principal export markets.

## Wine 7

**a) Origin and variety (15 marks).** Deep ruby-purple, dark plum and black cherry shot through with green pepper, dried herb, tobacco and graphite, full body and dense, ripe tannin at 14.5% identify Carménère — the pyrazine note alongside genuinely ripe fruit rules out Merlot, which would show softer plum without the green edge, while the riper tannin and greater depth rule out Cabernet Franc. This herbal-savoury signature layered onto full ripeness is Carménère's calling card, historically mistaken for Merlot in Chile until formal identification in 1994; the warm, dry season needed to reach this phenolic ripeness while retaining the herbal lift points to Colchagua Valley, the grape's Chilean heartland.

**b) Quality and market position (10 marks).** Concentration, ripe tannin management and well-judged new oak indicate very good quality, equivalent to an icon-tier Colchagua varietal Carménère retailing around $20–35, above generic Chilean varietal wine at $8–12. It suits premium supermarket ranges and Independent Specialists, competing with premium Merlot and mid-tier Bordeaux, and finds demand in the US, UK and increasingly Asia, where Chile promotes Carménère as its signature variety.

## Wine 8

**a) Origin and variety (15 marks).** Near-opaque purple-ruby, blackberry and dark plum with leather, iron and tobacco, and a massively firm, dense tannic structure alongside high acidity at 14.5% is the hardest call in the flight. The tannin exceeds anything typical of Cabernet Sauvignon, and the savoury iron-tobacco register without cassis or cedar rules that out; the colour depth and absence of tar-and-rose aromatics rule out Nebbiolo. This near-black colour with exceptional tannic mass is the defining trait of Tannat — a variety named for its tannin — originally from Madiran in south-west France but now Uruguay's national grape. The high, ripe acidity, tempering what could otherwise read as rustic, suggests moderating Atlantic influence on a coastal site, pointing to Uruguay's Maldonado department and a granitic hillside vineyard.

**b) Quality and market position (10 marks).** The depth of fruit, structural power and evident care taken to soften Tannat's naturally aggressive tannin indicate outstanding quality, near the top of current Uruguayan production, consistent with a single-vineyard wine retailing around $40–60. This is a niche, discovery-driven listing for Independent Specialists and premium on-trade rather than mainstream retail, competing with premium Madiran or bold New World reds, aimed at adventurous consumers seeking something beyond familiar varieties; export volumes remain small but growing in the US and UK.
