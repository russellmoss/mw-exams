# Mock answer — 2024 Paper 1 Question 3

The stem confirms all three are French blends, so the task is to separate three quite distinct blending traditions rather than to prove the country.

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible (3 x 8 marks)

**Wine 10.** The pale colour, saline waxy edge and ripe citrus/orchard-fruit/white-flower aromatics, with brisk, racy acidity and a grippy, textural mid-palate, point to a blend built for tension rather than a single-variety Loire or Alsace white. The pithy, citrus-pip grip of the acid, distinct from Sauvignon's greener malic edge, combined with 13.5% alcohol and a discreet old-wood texture, indicates Gros and Petit Manseng with Petit Courbu. Bordeaux was considered but ruled out by the absence of cut grass or blackcurrant leaf; this is consistent with Jurançon Sec, in the foothills of the Pyrenees.

**Wine 11.** Pale gold colour, cut herb and boxwood over pure citrus, flint and light smoke, a taut layered palate and no buttery malolactic softness funnel to a barrel-fermented Sauvignon Blanc/Sémillon blend rather than a varietal Sauvignon — the waxy Sémillon breadth under the herbaceous drive rules out Sancerre or Touraine. At 12.5% alcohol with a long, cool, mineral finish this is consistent with Bordeaux Blanc, and the precision of the oak narrows it further to Pessac-Léognan over a generic Entre-Deux-Mers.

**Wine 12.** A deep gold colour, broad glycerol texture, white flowers, stone fruit, fennel and a toasted, nutty edge, with moderate rather than high acidity and pronounced warmth at 14.5% alcohol, point to a warm southern Rhône Grenache Blanc/Roussanne blend — Alsace Pinot Gris/Pinot Blanc was eliminated by the riper, lower-acid, more savoury profile and clear wood influence. This weight and noble, lingering finish is consistent with Châteauneuf-du-Pape Blanc; the appellation is far better known for red, making this the deliberate curveball of the flight, but the blend recipe and structure leave little doubt.

## b) Comment on the method of production with reference to the use of oak (3 x 7 marks)

**Wine 10's** only discreet wood character alongside a creamy, lees-derived texture suggests hand-harvested, whole-bunch-pressed fruit, indigenous-yeast fermentation, then ageing mostly in used demi-muids and egg rather than new barrique, with a brief steel finish sharpening definition. **Wine 11's** dense, integrated toast and flinty, smoky lift without buttery softness indicate barrel fermentation in a meaningful proportion of new French oak, balanced by a steel component to preserve Sauvignon's drive; the absence of malolactic fermentation is as deliberate a choice as its use, protecting cut and precision through roughly a year in wood. **Wine 12's** obvious toasted, nutty oak over a still fruit-forward palate suggests fermentation and ageing predominantly in barrels and demi-muids, with a modest steel component keeping the multi-terroir blend from turning heavy — a shorter, less new-wood-driven regime than Wine 11's.

## c) Comment on style, quality, and commercial position (3 x 10 marks)

**Wine 10** is a serious, textural dry white of very good quality — above simple co-operative Jurançon Sec, comparable to a top single-estate bottling retailing around £20-25 — suited to independent specialists as an alternative to premium Chablis or white Rioja for drinkers seeking saline tension over fruit. **Wine 11** shows outstanding quality, at the top of Pessac-Léognan's hierarchy and close to Grand Cru Classé de Graves level, retailing around £45-60 through fine-wine merchants and premium restaurant lists, competing with white Burgundy and needing 5-8 years to show its best. **Wine 12** is very good to excellent, a premium expression of a red-dominated appellation retailing around £40-50 through specialist channels, offering an unusual, food-friendly alternative to white Hermitage for adventurous, informed consumers; still youthful, it should gain complexity over the next 3-5 years.
