# Mock answer — 2016 Paper 3 Question 1

## Variety

Across the flight the recurring thread is citrus and orchard fruit carried on a chalky or stony backbone, with several examples showing buttery, creamy texture from malolactic fermentation and lees work rather than aromatic weight from the grape itself. This neutral, terroir-transparent profile, together with the readiness to absorb oak without being overwhelmed by it, points to Chardonnay. Chenin Blanc was considered for the still examples but rejected — there is no honeyed lanolin or waxy quince character and the acidity, while present, is not the racy, high-toned line typical of that grape. Sémillon was also discounted; none of the wines shows the lanolin/waxy weight or the capacity for the more overt tropical development Sémillon takes with age.

## a) Winemaking, with particular reference to oak (5 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1.** Deep gold colour for a sparkling wine, with bruised apple, hazelnut, brioche and smoke rather than fresh citrus, indicates barrel-fermented base wine rather than the stainless-steel norm for blanc de blancs. The savoury, oxidative complexity and layered depth suggest a multi-vintage reserve system — effectively a solera-style blend — built specifically to add texture and maturity beyond what a single base year could offer. The dry, vinous finish points to low dosage, letting the barrel and oxidative character speak rather than masking it with sweetness. This is a deliberately atypical, artisanal approach to Champagne rather than a faulty one.

**Wine 2.** Fine, persistent mousse with lemon curd, chalk and brioche, but none of Wine 1's smoky, nutty edge, points to a predominantly stainless-steel-fermented base wine, with malolactic likely partial or blocked to preserve precision. The complexity and bottle-age development come from extended lees autolysis rather than wood — consistent with the minimum ageing period required for a vintage-dated blanc de blancs, here clearly exceeded given the layered brioche character.

**Wine 3.** A fine oak sheen over struck-stone minerality points to fermentation and roughly a year's maturation in French oak barriques, with a modest new-oak proportion — enough to add spice and texture without dominating the citrus-mineral core. The creamy, integrated texture without a hard malic edge confirms full malolactic fermentation. The absence of any waxy, heavier lees character suggests the producer chose not to stir the lees, protecting freshness and letting old-vine concentration, not bâtonnage, carry the mid-palate weight.

**Wine 4.** Riper stone fruit and citrus married to sweet, toasty oak spice and a creamy, full-bodied palate point to grapes pressed straight to barrel for fermentation, giving early, thorough integration of oak and lees rather than a later transfer. The maturation period reads shorter than Wine 3's, but riper New World fruit gives an equivalent impression of richness from a smaller new-oak proportion. Full malolactic is again suggested by the round, creamy texture.

**Wine 5.** Clean apple and citrus fruit with only the lightest cream and no toast points to fermentation and ageing largely in stainless steel or neutral vessels, with at most partial malolactic — enough to soften the edges without adding butter. This is a wine made for freshness and immediate commercial appeal rather than structure from wood.

## b) Origin (5 x 8 marks)

**Wine 1.** Fine, persistent mousse confirms traditional method; the oxidative, barrel-worked style combined with 12% alcohol and pronounced chalky minerality point to Champagne rather than a New World traditional-method wine, which would show riper fruit and higher alcohol. The intensity and savoury drive suggest top-tier chalk sites of the Côte des Blancs, sourced by a grower rather than a grande marque house.

**Wine 2.** Fine mousse and a chalk-and-citrus core again confirm traditional-method Champagne; the single-vintage purity and layered, brioche-driven bottle development rule out a blended NV wine. Precision alongside real breadth at 12.5% alcohol points to Côte des Blancs Chardonnay, likely a broader village blend around Épernay rather than a single grand cru site, which would show a starker, more austere line.

**Wine 3.** Still, dry, with mineral tension running through a barrel-shaped texture, this points to Burgundy over other French Chardonnay sources. The precise citrus-and-stone line and long chalky finish, carried by judicious rather than dominant oak, point specifically to Puligny-Montrachet on the Côte de Beaune, rather than the broader, nuttier richness typical of Meursault or the more overt power of Chassagne-Montrachet.

**Wine 4.** Ripe stone fruit, sweet oak spice and 13.5% alcohol point to a warm New World Chardonnay region; the retention of line and freshness alongside that ripeness, rather than tropical excess, rules out hotter inland sites and points to Hawke's Bay, New Zealand's warmest major wine region.

**Wine 5.** Simple, clean fruit, moderate acidity and minimal oak influence, at 13% alcohol, point to a generic, larger-volume Burgundian appellation rather than a village or premier cru site. The lack of Chablis's flinty, higher-acid drive and the absence of Côte de Beaune's concentration point to the Mâconnais, specifically Mâcon-Villages.

## c) Quality and commercial appeal (5 x 7 marks)

**Wine 1.** Outstanding quality — the combination of oxidative complexity, freshness and remarkable length is equivalent to a top grower Grand Cru Blanc de Blancs, retailing upward of £100. This is a niche, on-trade and specialist-merchant wine, sought by sommeliers and collectors as an artisanal alternative to grande marque prestige cuvées, and it under-the-skin reveals how deliberate cellar philosophy, not just site, defines Champagne's top tier.

**Wine 2.** Outstanding quality, showing the length, complexity and ageing potential of a prestige vintage blanc de blancs, retailing around £70–90. This suits premium on-trade lists and fine wine merchants, competing directly with vintage cuvées from other grandes marques, with strong export appeal in the US and Japan.

**Wine 3.** Very good to excellent quality, equivalent to a serious village-level Puligny-Montrachet retailing around £45–60; concentration and length edge toward premier cru territory without quite the extra layering that level demands. Suited to independent specialists and by-the-glass fine dining lists, competing with premium Meursault and top-tier New World Chardonnay.

**Wine 4.** Very good quality, a premium New World Chardonnay comparable to a top estate bottling, retailing around £20–25; good concentration and oak integration for the price. Suited to premium supermarkets and independent specialists, competing with village Burgundy and other cool-climate New World Chardonnays, with strong UK and North American export potential.

**Wine 5.** Good quality, a reliable commercial village wine retailing around £12–15, delivering straightforward Chardonnay typicity and freshness rather than complexity. Suited to supermarkets and by-the-glass lists, competing with entry-level Chablis and unoaked New World Chardonnay, appealing to consumers wanting easy-drinking, dependable white Burgundy.
