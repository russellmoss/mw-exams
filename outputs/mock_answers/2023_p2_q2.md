# Mock answer — 2023 Paper 2 Question 2

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 5.** Deep, youthful ruby with an overtly ripe nose of plum, blackberry and sweet vanilla-mocha spice points straight to warm-climate Shiraz; there is none of the white pepper or savoury reduction of cool Rhône Syrah, which rules that out. The soft, broad tannin and generous, uniform fruit profile — showing none of the site-specific tension a single-vineyard Barossa or McLaren Vale wine would carry — suggest a large-scale, multi-region blend. This points to South Eastern Australia GI rather than a named Australian sub-region.

**Wine 6.** A mature garnet rim over a still-vivid core, layered with dried fruit, clove, leather and roasted coffee, alongside a savoury, slightly herbal lift, indicates Tempranillo rather than Cabernet or Sangiovese, neither of which shows this coconut/clove oak signature so distinctly. The combination of freshness, firm acidity and structured tannin under obvious bottle-derived complexity is classic mature Rioja; the precision and backbone, rather than the riper, softer style of Rioja Baja, point to Rioja Alta.

**Wine 7.** Cassis, cedar, graphite and cigar-box aromas over fine, persistent, fully resolved tannin identify a Cabernet-dominant Left Bank Bordeaux blend — Merlot alone would show rounder, plummier fruit without this graphite austerity. The perfume and finesse, rather than the more powerful, blockier structure of Pauillac or St-Estèphe, funnel this to Margaux.

**Wine 8.** Brighter ruby, lifted red and black berry fruit, plum and warm spice with soft, velvety tannin and buoyant acidity point to Grenache, not Shiraz or Mourvèdre, which would carry more depth of colour and firmer structure. The ripe concentration allied to real freshness — a signature of very old, low-yielding bush vines — places this in Barossa.

## b) Comment on the method of production, with specific reference to oak (4 x 8 marks)

**Wine 5.** The vanilla-mocha lift sitting on top of, rather than woven into, soft commercial tannin suggests oak staves or chips, or brief contact with older barrels, used for flavour rather than structural build. Clean, uniform fruit and a lack of any reductive or wild-yeast complexity point to cultured yeast, temperature-controlled tank fermentation and a blending regime designed for consistency across large volumes.

**Wine 6.** The dual signature of sweet coconut (American oak) against spicier clove (French oak) indicates mixed cooperage, while the seamless integration of tannin and oak into a genuinely developed, savoury whole points to extended cask ageing well beyond a year, plus significant further bottle age before release — consistent with Gran Reserva's minimum two years in oak and five years total ageing before sale. The concentrated, pure fruit core beneath this development suggests careful, likely hand-harvested fruit and gentle cap management.

**Wine 7.** The fine-grained, polished tannin and cedary, toasty perfume, still present despite full bottle maturity, point to ageing in a high proportion of new French oak barriques, the hallmark of top classified-growth élevage. Precise fruit selection is implied by the purity and lack of any green or dilute character even at this age.

**Wine 8.** Restrained spice, gentle toast and an absence of aggressive tannin extraction despite genuinely concentrated old-vine fruit suggest ageing in older, neutral oak of a slightly larger format, preserving primary fruit purity rather than building oak-driven structure — a deliberate choice to let the fruit and freshness speak.

## c) Comment on quality and commercial position (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 5.** Clean, fault-free and immediately likeable but simple and short on complexity — an acceptable, entry-level GI style retailing around $8–12 through supermarkets and chain retail worldwide. It competes with other global commodity brands rather than regional specialists, targeting a price-driven, mainstream consumer who wants dependable, recognisable drinking rather than terroir expression.

**Wine 6.** Very good to outstanding quality, genuinely at Gran Reserva level: the layered complexity, savoury length and harmonious integration of fruit, oak and tannin separate it clearly from lower Crianza or Reserva tiers. Likely retailing $30–50 through independent specialists and fine-wine lists, it competes with Ribera del Duero Reserva and appeals to consumers seeking classic, traditionally aged red wine, with strong export demand in the UK and US.

**Wine 7.** Outstanding quality, fully justifying its Second Growth (1855) classification — the balance of fine tannin, freshness and persistent length after nearly two decades in bottle shows real ageing pedigree. Currently in a beautifully mature, secondary/tertiary window with several years of life still ahead, it retails in the $150–250 range through fine-wine merchants and top restaurant lists, competing with other classified-growth Margaux and drawing collectors across the UK, US and Asian markets.

**Wine 8.** Good to very good quality: old bush-vine fruit purity and freshness lift this clearly above generic commercial Barossa reds, though it lacks the depth and layering of a truly fine wine. Retailing around $20–30 through premium and independent retail, it competes with other single-region Barossa GSM-style reds and suits consumers seeking authentic, fruit-forward, modern Grenache with a story to tell.
