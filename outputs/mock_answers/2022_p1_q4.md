# Mock answer — 2022 Paper 1 Question 4

## a) Identify the grape variety and origin as closely as possible (4 x 15 marks)

**Wine 9.** The intensely perfumed nose of orange blossom, jasmine and white peach, allied to a dry, medium-to-full bodied palate at 13% alcohol, points immediately to Torrontés — Muscat was considered but rejected, as Muscat's grapiness usually carries through to a softer, more overtly sweet-seeming finish, whereas here the palate stays taut and savoury despite the floral lift. That combination of explosive aromatics with a genuinely dry, well-defined finish is the signature of good Argentine Torrontés from altitude. The freshness retained at this alcohol level, without any dilution of the perfume, points to a high-altitude site; Salta's Calchaquí Valley vineyards are the benchmark for this style, so Salta is the call over the lower, warmer Cafayate-adjacent valley floor sites or Argentina's more generic Mendoza production.

**Wine 10.** A citrus and white-peach nose with a distinct saline, sea-spray edge, on a dry, crisp, medium-bodied palate at 13% alcohol, is textbook Albariño. Riesling was briefly considered for the citrus lift but ruled out by the absence of any petrol or high, racy acid line — this palate is rounder and less linear than Riesling typically shows. The maritime salinity together with stone-fruit generosity rather than green, herbaceous character indicates Atlantic Spain rather than Vinho Verde's leaner, spritzier Portuguese expression, and the textural weight without oak points to Rías Baixas, specifically the Val do Salnés sub-zone closest to the Atlantic, where this saline character is most pronounced.

**Wine 11.** A dry, taut, linear palate carrying yellow apple, citrus, white pepper and a saline finish, at a modest 12% alcohol, is diagnostic of Grüner Veltliner — the peppery, lentil-and-radish register has no real parallel in other white varieties. Sauvignon Blanc was considered given the freshness but eliminated by the absence of any gooseberry or blackcurrant-leaf pungency. The precision, drive and loess-derived saline minerality on the finish are typical of Austria's Danube valleys; Kamptal is favoured over Wachau on the slightly lower alcohol and less overtly mineral, more fruit-forward balance than a top Wachau Federspiel or Smaragd would show.

**Wine 12.** This is markedly richer and more textural than the other three: honeyed pear, apricot blossom, almond and a faintly waxy, brioche-tinged palate at 14% alcohol, with broad, mouthfilling texture but retained freshness. Marsanne and Chardonnay were both considered — Marsanne's almond note is close, but Roussanne's more overt floral lift and higher natural acid line fit better, while the lack of overt buttery malolactic or new-oak toast rules out an oaked Chardonnay. The combination of textural richness with a mineral, saline lift rather than a broad, flat mid-palate points to granite-influenced northern Rhône rather than a warmer, flatter southern French white. Saint-Péray, the northern Rhône's only white-exclusive appellation, is the natural home for a serious, single-variety Roussanne of this weight.

## b) Comment on quality and market position (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 9.** Very good quality, above simple commercial Torrontés — the precision and length here suggest an estate-level bottling rather than a bulk Cafayate blend, retailing around $15-18. Best placed through independent specialists and Latin-focused restaurant lists in the US and UK as an alternative to Viognier or aromatic Alsace whites, capitalising on rising interest in Argentine whites beyond Malbec.

**Wine 10.** Outstanding quality for Rías Baixas — the concentration and saline drive place it well above generic DO level, close to a top single-vineyard bottling, at $18-22 retail. This suits premium supermarkets and independent merchants across the US and UK export markets, competing directly with Sancerre and Picpoul as a crisp, food-friendly seafood white.

**Wine 11.** Good, reliably commercial quality at DAC level rather than a single-vineyard Erste Lage wine — clean and precise but without the extra concentration or minerality that would signal top-tier Kamptal, at around $16-20. Well suited to independent retail and by-the-glass restaurant lists, competing with village Chablis and other precise, unoaked whites for the same drinker.

**Wine 12.** Very good to excellent quality, with the concentration, textural handling and judicious use of older oak pointing to a serious northern Rhône producer rather than a generic Saint-Péray, at $28-35 retail. This is a niche but high-value proposition for independent specialists and sommelier-led restaurant lists, positioned against white Hermitage or premium Condrieu for drinkers seeking texture without heavy new oak.
