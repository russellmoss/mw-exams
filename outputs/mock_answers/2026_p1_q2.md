# Mock answer — 2026 Paper 1 Question 2

## a) Identify the grape variety (15 marks)

Riesling. All three wines are unoaked, retain naturally high, racy acidity despite alcohols ranging from 11.5% to 13.5%, and finish dry with a floral, lime-blossom lift over stone fruit — a combination that is hard to achieve without genuinely high natural acid at full ripeness. Wine 8, the most developed of the three, shows an emerging kerosene/petrol note alongside lemon, bergamot and ginger spice, which is close to diagnostic for bottle-aged Riesling and confirms the call. Chenin Blanc was considered given the shared high acid and unoaked handling, but none of the three shows Chenin's waxy lanolin or honeyed weight, and the aromatics lean far more toward lime and petrol than quince. Grüner Veltliner was also considered for Wine 7 given the Austrian origin, but there is no white pepper or lentil note, and the taut, saline, phenolic-grip structure recurs identically across the French and Australian wines, arguing for one variety expressed across climates rather than a national style.

## b) Discuss the quality (3 x 15 marks)

**Wine 7.** The pale lemon-gold colour, cool nose of white peach, apricot and apple, and crystalline, saline acidity over a smoky, stony minerality point to a cool, terraced Central European site; the moderate 12.5% alcohol and bone-dry finish, rather than the riper, higher-alcohol profile of the region's top tier, place this at the mid-weight, single-vineyard "second tier" of the Danube's classic dry Riesling ladder — very good quality, equivalent to a Federspiel-level Ried bottling, retailing around $25-30. It has the site-transparent minerality and phenolic grip of a serious single vineyard but lacks the depth and riper concentration that would push it to the top, riper single-site tier.

**Wine 8.** The deeper gold hue, dense concentration and 13.5% alcohol against racy, penetrating acidity, chalky minerality and a long, ginger-spiced finish indicate riper fruit from a warmer, south-facing amphitheatre site than Wine 7, consistent with a top Grand Cru-quality slope. The purity and dry extract here are outstanding for the appellation — equivalent to Grand Cru level even where the label itself sits at village/AOC tier by house choice, retailing around $70-90, well above generic Alsace Riesling at $20-25, and justified by the structure's evident ageing potential.

**Wine 9.** The palest, greenest hue, featherweight body and lowest alcohol (11.5%) of the flight, with laser-precise lime and chalky/talc minerality, point to a cooler, elevated New World site rather than under-ripeness — a deliberately austere house style. This is a pure, well-made estate expression, very good quality for a single-vineyard regional bottling at around $20-25, just below the textural complexity a wild-fermented, lees-aged old-vine bottling from the same subregion would show.

## c) Comment on the wine's maturity (3 x 5 marks)

**Wine 7.** The lemon-gold colour and primary-to-developing stone fruit suggest a wine of around six years old, drinking well now; the racy acid and phenolic grip give it capacity to improve for a further 3-4 years and hold for 4-6 beyond that, gaining toasty, honeyed secondary notes.

**Wine 8.** The deeper gold and incipient petrol point to nine years of bottle age and the start of secondary development, though the wine's concentration keeps it youthful for its age; expect continued improvement for 5-8 years and a long hold beyond, with petrol, honey and dried-fruit characters building steadily.

**Wine 9.** The pale straw-green colour and pure, undeveloped lime fruit confirm a young wine of around two years; enjoyable now for its freshness, it has the acid backbone to improve over 3-5 years and hold for a further 5 or more, slowly building toast and lime-marmalade complexity.
