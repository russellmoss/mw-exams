# Mock answer — 2026 Paper 2 Question 2

All three wines share deep, near-opaque colour and powerful 15-16% alcohol. The organising question is not "which is riper" but how that concentration was reached, since each arrives there by a different route.

## Wine 4

**a) Variety and origin.** Ruby-garnet colour with dried, raisinated black cherry and plum, bitter chocolate, leather and a balsamic lift, high but buffered acidity and a firm, ripe tannin frame at 16% alcohol point to grapes concentrated by drying rather than sun alone. The sour-cherry fruit and bitter-chocolate finish suggest a Corvina-based blend rather than Sangiovese (would show harsher acid-tannin austerity) or Nero d'Avola (more jammy, less savoury). This is consistent with Amarone della Valpolicella, Veneto — the dried-fruit power well beyond simple Valpolicella Classico.

**b) Method of production.** The raisinated character points directly to appassimento: hand-picked bunches dried on racks for weeks to months, losing significant berry weight and concentrating sugar, extract and glycerol ahead of a long, warm fermentation. The spiced, rounded finish suggests extended barrel ageing, likely several years, well beyond a basic red's élevage.

**c) Quality and commercial position.** Single-vineyard concentration and structural depth place this at Riserva level, above basic Amarone — retailing around $80-100 — not a sweet Recioto. Sold through independent specialists and fine-dining lists, it competes with premium Ripasso and top Tuscan reds for collectors seeking age-worthy power.

## Wine 5

**a) Variety and origin.** Dense blackberry, blood plum and cherry compote over dark chocolate, vanilla and cedar, with polished tannin and a savoury, meaty edge, point to Syrah/Shiraz from a warm climate rather than Grenache (too fleshy, less tannic) or Cabernet Sauvignon (lacks this cassis-cedar backbone). Old-vine density and sweet spice indicate New World Shiraz; savoury olive and pipe-smoke development alongside sheer ripeness point to Barossa Valley, Australia rather than cooler Yarra or McLaren Vale fruit.

**b) Method of production.** Density with retained freshness suggests small-parcel, hands-on cap management, likely basket-pressed to protect tannin quality. Prominent vanilla-cedar oak indicates barrique ageing in a meaningful proportion of new French oak for around a year, framing without masking the old-vine fruit.

**c) Quality and commercial position.** This sits near the top of the Barossa quality ladder — a single-varietal icon-tier Shiraz, retailing around $150-200 — above generic Barossa Shiraz but short of the region's very top flagships. Sold through fine-wine merchants and allocation lists in the US, UK and Asia, competing with premium Rhône Syrah and other icon Australian Shiraz.

## Wine 6

**a) Variety and origin.** Supersaturated black cherry, blackberry and black olive, lead-pencil, dried flowers and toasty new-oak spice with firm, grippy tannin suggest varietal Cabernet Sauvignon, but a plush, fleshy mid-palate beneath the firm frame and extra red-plum lift point to a Bordeaux-style blend, Merlot and Petit Verdot rounding out fruit and colour — Cabernet-led rather than pure varietal. Riper, more opulent fruit than Bordeaux itself, with heavy new-oak toast, rules out the Left Bank and points to Napa Valley, California.

**b) Method of production.** Saturated colour and ripe, grippy tannin point to a warm, dry season giving small, concentrated berries, likely with some whole-cluster inclusion and extended maceration. Pervasive toasty spice and polished texture indicate maturation entirely in new French oak barriques for close to two years — the dominant stylistic signature.

**c) Quality and commercial position.** This is a well above premium-tier estate red — a serious "second label"-adjacent proprietary blend rather than the producer's absolute flagship, retailing around $70-90 — below top Napa cult Cabernets but firmly in the fine-wine bracket. Sold through specialist retailers and restaurant lists in the US and increasingly Asia, competing with premium Right Bank-style Bordeaux blends and other sub-flagship Napa proprietary reds.
