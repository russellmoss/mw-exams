# Mock answer — 2024 Paper 1 Question 1

## a) Identify the grape variety with reference to both wines (3 x 10 marks)

**Pair 1 (Wines 1–2) — Riesling.** Wine 1's pale, green-flecked colour, crushed wild-thyme and crunchy green-apple aromatics, off-dry residual sugar shouldered by racy, almost searing acidity, and a feather-light 8% alcohol are textbook cool-climate Riesling; Muscat and Scheurebe were both considered for the aromatic lift, but neither delivers this acid spine without a more overt grapey or blackcurrant-bud character, which is absent here. Wine 2 shows the same variety in its dry register: bergamot, mirabelle and slatey, still-reductive stone fruit, a bone-dry, silky-rich palate and the same racy acid line, now at 13% alcohol. Grüner Veltliner was considered for the herbal, stony intensity but ruled out — there is no white pepper or lentil note, and the finish is too lemony and mineral-driven for that variety.

**Pair 2 (Wines 3–4) — Chardonnay.** Both wines share a stone-fruit and melon core wrapped in a naturally broad, faintly creamy texture, with a light vanilla or spice topnote — Chardonnay's most reliable varietal signature, since it is one of the few whites that reads recognisably the same whether vinified with grand ambition or none at all. Viognier was eliminated on both counts: neither wine shows the apricot-blossom lift or viscous, higher-alcohol texture that variety typically carries. Wine 3 layers citrus zest, baked bread, beeswax and buttered popcorn onto that core, with a chalky, dry-extract grip at 14.5% alcohol that speaks of serious barrel-fermentation; Wine 4 keeps the same fruit spectrum simpler, brighter and more linear, with lighter oak-derived vanilla rather than genuine barrel complexity.

**Pair 3 (Wines 5–6) — Sauvignon Blanc.** Wine 5's pungent tomato leaf, green pepper and passion fruit over bright, dry acidity is unmistakably pyrazine-driven Sauvignon Blanc. Wine 6 is more of a puzzle at first glance: gunflint, struck match and toast could point toward barrel-fermented Chardonnay or even Chenin Blanc, but underneath the winemaking veneer sits the same blackcurrant-leaf, herbaceous pyrazine note as Wine 5, carried on a racy, saline acid line that Chardonnay rarely shows with this intensity — this confirms Sauvignon Blanc rather than an oak-worked Chardonnay, whose baked-bread and beeswax notes would dominate rather than sit alongside green-leaf fruit.

## b) Compare and contrast the quality, maturity and capacity to age (3 x 20 marks)

**Pair 1.** Both wines are outstanding, but in different sweetness categories, and that is the real contrast here. Wine 1 is a beautifully balanced, off-dry Kabinett — equivalent to a VDP Erste Lage bottling — where 8% alcohol and residual sugar are held in check by racy acidity; it is youthful and precise now, but the sugar and acid combination gives it real ageing capacity, likely to gain honeyed, petrol-tinged complexity over 5–8 years and hold for a decade beyond that. Wine 2 is the dry, powerful counterpart, equivalent to a Grosses Gewächs Grand Cru: concentrated, still faintly reductive and structurally tight, it is a baby at this stage and needs a decade to unwind its density into full aromatic expression, with 20–30 years of ageing potential given its acid backbone. Neither wine should be marked down against the other for style — the contrast is ripeness category (fruit-sweet aperitif versus bone-dry gastronomic wine), not quality.

**Pair 2.** This pair is a genuine quality gulf and must be judged honestly in both directions. Wine 3 is outstanding, Grand Cru quality — concentrated, layered, with racy acid and chalky dry extract underpinning real structural depth; still primary and tightly wound, it needs several years to open and will develop toasted, hazelnut and honeyed complexity over 15–20 years, holding well beyond that. Wine 4 must not be under-called out of origin bias: it is a clean, technically correct, commercially very successful Chardonnay, good quality for its price tier, with fresh peach and melon fruit and no faults — but it is simple and one-dimensional, lacking the concentration, structure and length that would elevate it, and shows no meaningful capacity to age. It is fully ready and best drunk within 12–18 months of release while the primary fruit is fresh; further time will only dull it.

**Pair 3.** Same region, same variety, but a tier separation driven entirely by winemaking ambition. Wine 5 is a good-quality, correct entry-level Marlborough style — pungent, fresh and immediately appealing, but built for consumption within a year or two before its pyrazine lift fades; it has no development potential. Wine 6 is outstanding and highly atypical: a deliberately non-interventionist, barrel-fermented, wild-yeast wine already showing early tertiary complexity (toast, dried mango, praline) alongside racy acid and real textural weight. It is mid-development now and should continue to gain complexity for another 5–8 years, holding comfortably beyond that — a wine built to transcend, rather than showcase, the aromatic Marlborough template.

## c) Identify the origin as closely as possible (6 x 10 marks)

**Wine 1** — Mosel, Ürzig (Ürziger Würzgarten). The combination of very low alcohol, off-dry balance and pronounced slatey minerality points to Germany's steep, slate-soil river valleys. The riper, almost spicy red-fruited slate character (as opposed to the more austere blue-slate profile of villages further downstream) points specifically to the red volcanic slate and sandstone of Ürzig's steepest sites.

**Wine 2** — Franken, Iphofen (Julius-Echter-Berg). The bone-dry, full-bodied style immediately rules out the Mosel; dry, structured Riesling of this weight is more typical of Franken, the Pfalz, Rheingau or Nahe. The restrained, herbal, gypsum-marl stoniness rather than the more overt tropical ripeness of the Pfalz points to Franken's cooler clay-marl terroir, consistent with a top single-vineyard site such as Iphofen's Julius-Echter-Berg.

**Wine 3** — Burgundy, Côte de Beaune, Corton hill (Corton-Charlemagne). The combination of citrus zest, baked bread and beeswax from barrel fermentation with a chalky, dry-extract grip and 14.5% alcohol is classic top white Burgundy. Meursault and Puligny were considered but would show softer orchard fruit and less structural power; the fuller weight and mineral drive here point to the limestone-scree slopes of the Corton hill.

**Wine 4** — South Eastern Australia (multi-regional blend). The simple, broad melon and white peach fruit with no site-specific structure or minerality points away from a defined single region such as Margaret River or Adelaide Hills, which would show more precision. This reads as a large-scale, multi-region blended commodity wine under the South Eastern Australia GI.

**Wine 5** — Marlborough, New Zealand. The classic pyrazine-driven tomato leaf and passion fruit profile at moderate alcohol is textbook Marlborough, but the straightforward structure and lack of any distinguishing site character point to a blended, multi-vineyard entry-level bottling rather than a specific sub-region.

**Wine 6** — Marlborough, likely Wairau Valley. The fuller body, riper stone fruit and higher 14.5% alcohol, layered with deliberate barrel-ferment complexity, suggest warmer, silt-and-loam valley-floor fruit rather than the cooler, stonier Awatere sub-region; the old-vine, single-block character reflects the premium end of Marlborough's emerging terroir hierarchy.
