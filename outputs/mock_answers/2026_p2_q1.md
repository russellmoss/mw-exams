# Mock answer — 2026 Paper 2 Question 1

## a) Style and commercial position (3 x 11 marks)

**Wine 1.** Dry, medium-bodied, soft and well-integrated tannins, moderate acidity, no oak — a fresh, fruit-driven style for early drinking, a light savoury/mineral edge lifting it just above basic. At 12% alcohol with easy "bistrot" drinkability, this is large-volume commercial rather than growers' fruit: supermarkets and mid-market independents at £10-14, competing with everyday Bordeaux AOC, appealing to casual weeknight or by-the-glass drinkers; UK, Benelux and Germany are the natural outlets for Loire co-operative reds.

**Wine 2.** Dry, light-to-medium bodied, bright acidity, fine delicate tannins, pure primary red-fruit and floral lift; unoaked, with no structure built for cellaring — a deliberately fresh, chillable, gastro style. This is a cru-level commercial proposition, priced above generic Beaujolais at £15-20, sold through independents, wine clubs and the "gateway to the crus" mixed-case trade, and by-the-glass in casual dining; it suits drinkers moving from rosé toward serious but approachable red, and travels well to the US and UK where cru Beaujolais is fashionable.

**Wine 3.** Dry, medium-bodied, markedly paler, with taut acidity, fine but persistent tannins and a savoury spice-and-mineral register beneath red and dried fruit; a light oxidative, low-intervention texture rather than a polished modern style. This is a serious, terroir-driven wine, not a commercial blend: fine-wine independents, natural-wine specialists and sommelier lists at a premium £35-45, competing with grower Burgundy or serious Loire Cabernet Franc for an adventurous consumer; UK, US and Japan are the strongest markets for this low-intervention Jura style.

## b) Method of production (3 x 6 marks)

**Wine 1.** Soft tannins and pure fruit, without green-stem edges, point to full destemming ahead of controlled vatting and maceration of two to three weeks in temperature-controlled stainless steel. No oak character indicates ageing in inert vessel, consistent with a co-operative prioritising freshness and volume.

**Wine 2.** The vivid, kirsch-like fruit intensity and soft, silky tannin with no green or reductive notes suggest semi-carbonic maceration in closed vats — the classic Beaujolais method — rather than conventional crushed vinification. The total absence of oak signature points to inert-vessel ageing, preserving primary aromatics.

**Wine 3.** The unusually pale colour and lifted floral aromatics in a wine with real structural presence point to partial whole-bunch/carbonic handling ahead of a longer, gentle open-vat fermentation with minimal punch-down — extraction built for perfume, not grip. Fine, textural tannins and subtle spice without fruit-sweetness suggest ageing in mostly neutral large wood plus some amphora, prioritising texture and mineral transparency over oak.

## c) Grape variety and origin (3 x 8 marks)

**Wine 1.** Deep ruby colour, blackcurrant and red-plum fruit with a leafy, herbal top note, soft tannins and moderate acidity point to Cabernet Franc; Merlot was considered for the plum fruit and soft tannins but ruled out by the herbal edge and leaner mid-palate that Merlot's riper fruit lacks. Moderate alcohol and a light mineral streak point to the Loire, and the softness and absence of pyrazine sharpness suggest a warmer, southerly site such as Saumur rather than firmer Chinon or Bourgueil.

**Wine 2.** Bright cherry-raspberry fruit, violet lift, low tannin extraction and juicy freshness point unmistakably to Gamay; Pinot Noir was briefly considered for the pale-medium colour but eliminated by the confected, kirsch-like fruit and very soft, low-grip tannin that carbonic handling produces, which Pinot Noir's firmer, more savoury structure does not show. This points to Beaujolais, and fruit purity and structural definition above simple Beaujolais-Villages indicate cru-level origin.

**Wine 3.** The pale colour initially suggests Pinot Noir, but structure eliminates it: the tannin has a grippy, savoury edge and the acidity is taut and almost citric, with dried-herb, white-pepper and faintly oxidative character alien to Burgundy's rounder, finer profile. Pale colour, high-toned red and dried fruit, savoury spice and a limestone-driven mineral finish point instead to Trousseau. The precision of that mineral streak and structural seriousness, above a simple varietal Arbois rouge, point to a top single-vineyard selection from Arbois in the Jura.
