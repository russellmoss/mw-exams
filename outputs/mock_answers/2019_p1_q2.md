# Mock answer — 2019 Paper 1 Question 2

## a) Identify the origin and grape variety(ies) as closely as possible (6 x 12 marks)

**Wine 5.** Deep lemon colour, a savoury, waxy texture and pronounced toast and spice over ripe citrus, apple and fennel point to a variety fermented and matured in barrel with real oxidative handling — Chardonnay was considered but the fennel-and-wax character, moderate rather than searing acidity and 13.5% alcohol point instead to Viura, most likely blended with a little Malvasía. This oxidative-oaked style with gastronomic weight is the calling card of white Rioja, and the depth of oak integration and broad, layered finish place it in Rioja Alta, the sub-zone associated with the most ambitious barrel-fermented Rioja Blanco.

**Wine 6.** Pale lemon, high aromatic intensity of citrus, white flowers and green herb, with a distinct saline edge and brisk acidity, points to an Atlantic Iberian white rather than the oxidative style of Wine 5 — the paired structure of the flight confirms these two are Spanish. The polished, rounded mid-palate without any oak signature suggests fine lees contact rather than barrel, and the combination of freshness with textural weight at 13.0% alcohol is characteristic of Albariño, here most likely a field-blend with Loureiro, Treixadura or Godello rather than a pure varietal bottling. This Atlantic, granite-inflected profile is textbook Rías Baixas, and the saline drive and structured palate point specifically to the O Rosal sub-zone on the Galician coast.

**Wine 7.** Medium lemon colour, ripe citrus and white peach with Mediterranean herb notes, moderate rather than piercing acidity, and a soft, saline texture at 12.5% alcohol suggest a warm, coastal, unoaked white. Vermentino and Fiano were considered, but the specific citrus-herb combination with a softly saline finish and no phenolic grip points to Grillo. The sun-warmed fruit profile and coastal salinity indicate Sicily, and the fruit purity without heat suggests the cooler, breeze-moderated vineyards of western Sicily rather than the island's hotter interior.

**Wine 8.** This is the loudest single clue in the flight: very pale lemon with a delicate mousse, an intensely perfumed nose of orange blossom, ripe peach and grapey Muscat character, low alcohol at just 5.5% and a sweet but airy palate immediately identify a partially fermented, CO2-retentive Muscat style rather than a still wine. The grapey, floral intensity is unmistakably Moscato Bianco, and the frothy spritz combined with low alcohol and bright acidity — rather than the fuller, more overtly sparkling Asti Spumante style — points to Moscato d'Asti DOCG in Piedmont.

**Wine 9.** Pale lemon, a lime-driven nose of white flowers, talc and wet stone, and a dry, taut palate with high, racy acidity at 12.5% alcohol point unmistakably to Riesling — Grüner Veltliner was briefly considered for the citrus drive but lacks the flowery, stony intensity and searing line here. Bone-dry with no oak or malolactic softening, this is New World rather than German in style; the chalky, mineral-driven fruit definition and firm acid backbone are classic Clare Valley, and the intensity of lime and slate specifically suggests the Watervale sub-district within Clare.

**Wine 10.** Medium lemon, ripe citrus and stone fruit with struck-flint reduction and integrated toasty oak, a creamy, weighty palate held together by cool acidity, and 13.0% alcohol point to Chardonnay in a modern premium style. Given the flight is paired by country with Wine 9, and the reductive, barrel-worked character with restrained rather than heavy oak is the signature of contemporary cool-climate Australian Chardonnay, this points to South Australia; the precision and savoury nutty lees character alongside genuine concentration are consistent with the elevated, cool Adelaide Hills.

## b) Key winemaking techniques (6 x 7 marks)

**Wine 5.** The deep colour, waxy texture and pronounced toast indicate barrel fermentation, most likely in a mix of new and older French oak, followed by extended lees ageing that has built the broad, savoury mid-palate. The absence of bright primary fruit and the layered, gastronomic finish suggest a longer élevage of a year or more, consistent with traditional Rioja Blanco philosophy.

**Wine 6.** The purity of citrus and floral fruit with no toast or vanilla points to stainless-steel fermentation at cool, controlled temperature to protect aromatics. The rounded mid-palate texture without any oxidative or oaky note suggests a period on fine lees, likely with bâtonnage, before an early bottling to preserve freshness.

**Wine 7.** The clean, saline fruit and absence of any oak marker indicate tank fermentation and maturation in stainless steel. The soft texture despite the lack of oak suggests a short period of lees contact, and the overall freshness points to an early, protective bottling aimed at retaining primary fruit rather than building complexity.

**Wine 8.** The combination of retained natural sweetness, low alcohol and fine spritz confirms a fermentation arrested part-way through by chilling and filtration under the Charmat/Asti method, using the Martinotti tank process rather than a second fermentation. Bottling under pressure at low temperature has locked in residual CO2 and primary aromatics, explaining the delicate mousse and immediate floral lift.

**Wine 9.** The pure citrus and mineral definition with no textural weight from lees or oak point to protective stainless-steel fermentation at low temperature. The taut, unadorned structure and screwcap-typical purity of fruit suggest only a short period of tank maturation before an early bottling designed to preserve line and ageing potential.

**Wine 10.** The savoury, struck-flint reduction alongside integrated toast points to whole-bunch pressing followed by barrel fermentation in French oak, with a portion — around 30% — new. The creamy, nutty texture indicates full malolactic fermentation and lees ageing with regular stirring, and the balance of richness against precise acidity suggests around nine months in barrel before blending and bottling.

## c) Comment on the quality (6 x 6 marks)

**Wine 5.** Very good quality, comparable to a leading Rioja Alta estate bottling at around $30-35 — the textural depth, oak integration and long, savoury finish sit well above generic Rioja Blanco, though it falls short of the rarest single-vineyard barrel-fermented examples for sheer concentration.

**Wine 6.** Very good quality for Rías Baixas, at the O Rosal benchmark level around $20-25 — precise, saline and structured rather than simply aromatic, though it lacks the extra layering of the very top single-parcel bottlings.

**Wine 7.** Good quality, a well-made commercial Sicilia DOC white at around $12-15 — clean, varietally accurate and texturally satisfying, but built for immediate drinking rather than complexity or length.

**Wine 8.** Very good quality for the category, at the top end of Moscato d'Asti DOCG around $18-20 — the fine mousse and pure, precise perfume distinguish it clearly from bulk Asti Spumante and confirm careful, specialist production.

**Wine 9.** Outstanding quality, equivalent to a top single-vineyard Watervale bottling at around $30 — the intensity, precision and chalky drive give real ageing potential and mark this well above generic Clare Valley Riesling.

**Wine 10.** Outstanding quality, at the top of premium Adelaide Hills Chardonnay around $45-50 — the concentration, judicious oak handling and savoury complexity place it alongside the benchmark examples of the region, with genuine capacity to develop further in bottle.
