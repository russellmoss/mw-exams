# Mock answer — 2021 Paper 3 Question 3

## a) Identify the origin as closely as possible (2 x 10 marks)

**Pair 1 (Wines 9 & 10)** — Jerez, Spain. Both wines show deep amber-mahogany colour with an olive-green rim and a fully oxidative nose of walnut, dried fig and toffee with no fresh fruit remaining — this rules out any biologically-aged, flor-driven style and points instead to a wine placed into oxidative ageing from an early stage. The saline, nutty structure, medium acidity and 20-20.5% alcohol are more consistent with Palomino grown on chalk-limestone soil than with the riper, more fruit-retentive character of aged Tawny Port. Together the evidence funnels to Oloroso-style Sherry from Jerez de la Frontera, DO Jerez-Xérès-Sherry.

**Pair 2 (Wines 11 & 12)** — Madeira, Portugal. Both wines carry the same tawny-amber, oxidatively-aged colour but are marked out by a burnt-sugar, roasted-coffee "cooked" character and a searingly high, almost citric acidity that lifts an otherwise sweet, rich mid-palate. This acid spine, far higher than anything seen in Sherry or Port, is Madeira's structural fingerprint and, combined with the caramelised rancio note, confirms Madeira island, Madeira DOC.

## b) Compare and contrast style, quality and commercial position (2 x 20 marks)

**Pair 1.** Wine 9 is bone dry with no perceptible residual sugar, its single-source, unblended character (rather than a standardised house style) suggesting an individual small-parcel bottling — outstanding quality, equivalent to a benchmark aged dry Oloroso, retailing around £35-45 for a half bottle through independent specialists and sommelier-led restaurant lists to collectors seeking a single-cask expression. Wine 10 shares the same oxidative Oloroso base but is intensely sweet, with raisin, coffee and chocolate depth suggesting a much longer declared average age — outstanding quality at the top of the sweet Oloroso ladder, priced significantly higher at £45-70 a half bottle, sold through fine-wine specialists and fine dining as a luxury digestif competing with aged Tawny Port and Pedro Ximénez itself. The contrast is stark: one austere and saline, the other opulent and dessert-styled, from an identical oxidative starting point.

**Pair 2.** Wine 11 is straightforwardly sweet and rich but comparatively simple, its short ageing pointing to a commercial, entry-level tier — good quality at best, retailing around £12-18, sold through supermarkets and by-the-glass lists as an accessible aperitif or cooking-wine alternative. Wine 12 shows extraordinary complexity — dried apricot, beeswax, roasted nuts and a persistent sweet-sour tension from decades of ageing — outstanding quality equivalent to a premium single-vintage (Colheita) bottling, priced £80-150 through fine-wine merchants and auction rooms, targeting collectors and comparing to historic vintage Bual or aged Tawny Port. The same house evidently spans both a commercial workhorse and a serious age-worthy flagship.

## c) Comment on the method of production (4 x 10 marks)

**Wine 9** — The dry, saline, nutty profile with no fruit sweetness suggests grapes fermented fully to dryness before fortification to around 17-18%, a level that suppresses flor and commits the wine to oxidative rather than biological ageing from the outset. The single-cask, individual character points to ageing outside a large blended solera, most likely within a small stockholder's own fractional system in seasoned American oak, developing the walnut and dried-fig complexity through years of controlled ullage and oxidation.

**Wine 10** — The same dry oxidative Oloroso base is layered with pronounced raisin, coffee and chocolate richness, indicating a fully-aged dry wine sweetened post-ageing with a concentrated sweet component — most plausibly sun-dried Pedro Ximénez — rather than a wine fermented sweet from the start. The extraordinary depth and seamless integration argue for many years of fractional blending through criadera-solera in old American oak, consistent with an officially verified average age of thirty years or more.

**Wine 11** — The pronounced burnt-sugar, caramel and roasted-coffee character with only moderate complexity suggests a short élevage of around three years in neutral old wood, with the cooked, slightly volatile lift pointing to artificial tank-heating (estufagem) rather than slow ambient ageing — the fastest, most economical route to Madeira's signature rancio note for a commercial-tier wine.

**Wine 12** — Greater layered complexity — roasted nuts, dried apricot, beeswax and a pronounced rancio/aldehydic lift — together with a declared single-harvest origin point instead to slow, natural cask ageing (canteiro) in sun-warmed lofts over two decades, the method reserved for serious single-vintage Madeira. The wine's searing acid, still vital after such extended oxidative ageing, reflects a variety naturally suited to long cask life and underlines why this bottling sits at the top of the range.
