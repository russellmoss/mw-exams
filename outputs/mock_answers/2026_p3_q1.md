# Mock answer — 2026 Paper 3 Question 1

## Wine 1 — Origin (10 marks)

Coarse, fast-fading bubbles at low pressure rather than a fine, persistent bead point to **ancestral method** — fermentation finished in the sealed bottle, no disgorgement or dosage — not Charmat, which would be cleaner and neutral. Pale-mid ruby, light tannic grip and a floral, wild-strawberry nose over a faint bready lift confirm a red grape with brief skin contact: **Lambrusco**. Prosecco was eliminated — white, tank-made, no tannin. High acidity, dry finish and 11.5% alcohol confirm a cool Italian origin: **Emilia-Romagna, most likely Sorbara**, paler and more perfumed than Grasparossa.

## Wine 2 — Origin (10 marks)

Pale straw, a fine persistent bead and pastry-driven autolysis over apple and pear suggest **traditional method** with extended lees ageing well beyond Charmat depth. The round, creamy palate with good acid grip rules out a basic six-month wine. Waxy apple/pear character, greener than Xarel-lo or Parellada, points to **Macabeo** as the dominant grape. This structural seriousness and cooler-climate acid poise argue against coastal Penedès and for **Cava DO from a high-altitude interior zone — Utiel-Requena**; Champagne was eliminated on cost and on the absence of Champagne's chalkier, more taut spine.

## Wine 3 — Origin (10 marks)

Pale gold, fine mousse, and quince, honeyed apple and biscuit point again to **traditional method** with solid lees contact. A rounded entry with dosage near the top of the Brut band (close to but under 12 g/L) gives a softer profile than bone-dry Champagne. The quince/wax signature is the killer evidence for **Chenin Blanc** over Chardonnay or Pinot Noir, both considered but ruled out by the absence of red-grape character and the distinct orchard-fruit register. This points to the **Loire, Vouvray**, rather than Crémant de Loire, which would show a broader blend and a drier dosage.

## Wine 4 — Origin (10 marks)

A fine, persistent mousse and lemon curd, orange blossom, brioche with toasted nut and a whisper of vanilla read as **traditional method** with long lees ageing; the oak-derived vanilla — a partial barrel-fermented component — is the key discriminator. The bright acid line and chalky, saline finish echo Champagne's Côte des Blancs idiom, but the riper stone-fruit register and richer barrel-worked texture eliminate it. This is 100% Chardonnay, a **Blanc de Blancs**, from a cool but ripening chalk site: chalk minerality plus this richly worked style points to **England**, most plausibly the **Thames & Chilterns** belt rather than Sussex/Hampshire.

## Quality in context (4 x 8 marks)

**Wine 1** — good, well-made Lambrusco, equivalent to a premium DOC bottling around £12-15: pure fruit, balanced dry finish and genuine ancestral character above simple, sweeter commercial Charmat norms, judged as excellent Lambrusco, not against Cava or Champagne.

**Wine 2** — very good quality at Reserva level (18 months lees exceeds the 9-month floor), pushing towards Gran Reserva seriousness; the autolytic depth is well above commodity Brut Cava (£6-8), closer to £14-18 premium single-variety Reserva.

**Wine 3** — very good, reliable mid-market Vouvray Brut (£15-20) with real autolytic depth, though generous dosage and NV blending place it below a grower-estate Tête de Cuvée from the same appellation.

**Wine 4** — outstanding quality, equivalent to a premium single-vineyard English estate cuvée (£40-50+), showing concentration, barrel-derived textural complexity and ageability rivalling Grower Champagne.

## Key winemaking decisions (4 x 7 marks)

**Wine 1** — light 24-hour maceration gave the pale ruby colour and gentle tannin; fermentation was arrested and the wine bottled unfinished to complete in bottle, with roughly six months on lees and no dosage, explaining the coarse mousse and pure primary fruit.

**Wine 2** — hand-selected Macabeo underwent traditional-method second fermentation and a minimum 18 months on lees, explaining the pastry autolysis and fine bead; no oak or malolactic character is evident, keeping fruit clean and acidity taut.

**Wine 3** — traditional-method second fermentation with roughly 14 months on lees gives moderate autolysis without masking Chenin fruit; steel vinification preserved varietal quince character, and dosage near 11 g/L explains the generous, rounded finish.

**Wine 4** — 50% fermentation in oak puncheons with wild yeast explains the toasted, vanilla-tinged texture; malolactic softened the acid line, and roughly 34 months on lees built the brioche depth, while a restrained 8.1 g/L dosage keeps the finish precise.
